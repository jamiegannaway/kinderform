# Where people are lost: fostering research and lessons from other fields

> **Status: first draft.** Quick scan, 25 November 2026. This is not a full review. It was made with an AI-assisted search of peer-reviewed papers and the web. See [how we made this](https://www.kinderform.org/about#how). Where the evidence is not about fostering, every view on what carries over is our own. Everything we propose is for discussion. It is not yet part of the Kinderform standard, the open standard that the project behind kinderform.org is drafting. The [research log](../research-log.json) says what each review could mean for the project.

Grades: A = randomised or strong quasi-experimental; B = controlled comparison or robust official statistics; C = descriptive, survey or qualitative; D = anecdote, single site or secondhand.

## In short

1. **Research on fostering and adoption agrees on where people are lost.** The first call or reply, the wait between steps, and how the service communicates come up again and again.
2. **Shorter is not always better.** In the largest study from another field (222,772 job applicants), people who quit an assessment mostly did so in the first 20 minutes, and length did not predict quitting. Honest time estimates went with fewer people leaving.
3. **Waiting is the clearer risk.** Several fields show more people dropping away as the gap before the next step grows.
4. **Who gets a reply is not always equal.** Field experiments in the US, UK, Sweden and Germany show public bodies sometimes reply less often, or less warmly, depending on the sender's name. Our search found no test of this for fostering enquiries.
5. **Honest information makes some people withdraw, and that is fine.** It supports informed decisions, which is the project's aim.

## 1. Research on fostering and adoption

| Finding | Source | Grade | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Of 91 people who enquired, 4% became carers. The rest split evenly between personal circumstances and being put off by the agency's response. | Keogh and Svensson, *Children Australia*, 1999 (Victoria) | C, old | Already held. |
| Survey of 347 people who contacted South Australia's recruitment service, 2004 to 2006. Worries about what fostering involves put off about 30%. Most stopped because of personal circumstances or fear of failing. | Delfabbro and others, *Children Australia*, 2008 | C, old | Adds reasons to the 2% figure already held. |
| Almost half of families who started training before approval did not finish it. Of 131 who finished, 46% had stopped or planned to stop at 6 months. Families with more resources, income above all, were more likely to continue. | Rhodes and others, *Social Work Research*, 2003 (US) | B/C, old | Income, not suitability, went with staying. |
| Steep losses from first call to adoption. Two crucial points: the first call, and matching. ## 2. Research from other fields

None of this is fostering evidence.

### Applying for demanding roles

| Finding | Source | Grade | Does it carry over? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 222,772 job applicants across 69 selection processes. Most who quit did so in the first 20 minutes. Length did not predict quitting. Processes that gave longer, more cautious time estimates lost fewer people. | Hardy and others, *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 2017 | B | Partly. Supports telling people honestly how long each step takes. A warning against assuming a shorter form fixes drop-out. |
| 143 women and minority police applicants who withdrew. Reasons, after a change of mind: another job, and the length of the process. Recommends more contact with applicants. | Gibbs, *Policing*, 2020 (US) | C | Yes, for the pattern: long silence, people move on. |
| 4.1% of 157,154 accepted job offers ended with the person never starting. Longer gaps before the start date and weaker contact beforehand went with higher risk. | Speer and others, *Human Resource Management Journal*, 2026 | B | Partly. Paid work, but the same cause: a gap plus silence. |
| 102 students agreed to take part in a short study. Response was 80% after a 1-day wait, 56% after a week, 42% after a month and none after 2 months. | Walsh and others, *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology*, 2015 | A, small | A little. A tiny task, but a clean test of delay. |
| Giving people a realistic picture of a hard start made more of them withdraw and lowered what they expected. | Capitano and others, *Group & Organization Management*, 2022 | A/B | Yes, as a principle: honest information leads to informed withdrawal, which is not failure. |
| 117 studies, 55,335 volunteers. Communication, support from the organisation and the relationship with the person who leads them are among the strongest links to volunteers leaving or staying. | Forner and others, *Journal of Organizational Behavior*, 2023 | B | Partly. About staying, not joining. |
| In a remote health trial, 26.6% of 364 pairs who agreed never finished the sign-up steps. People called the posted packs confusing and excessive, and asked for more human contact. | Haun and others, *Journal of Medical Internet Research*, 2023 | C | Partly. Close to the application pack stage. |

### Who gets a reply

| Finding | Source | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Emails to more than 19,000 US local public services. 72% of emails from white-sounding names got a reply. Black-sounding names were almost 4 points lower, and replies were less cordial. | Giulietti and others, 2015 | A |
| 10,268 UK local councillors. Replies were 6 to 7 points lower to names that sounded Muslim or Jewish. | Crawfurd and others, 2021 | A |
| A pooled analysis of experiments: minority senders are less likely to get a reply from public officials. | Costa, 2017 | A |
| US cities of over 50,000 people answered 54% of email requests. | Epstein and others, 2021 | B |
| Sweden: no clear gap in reply rates, but replies to Arabic-sounding names were less friendly. Germany: very limited effects. | Adman and Jansson, 2016; Grohs and others, 2015 | A |

*Does it carry over?* The question does; the answer is unknown. Our search found no test of fostering enquiries. **A caution:** these studies send made-up emails without consent. A hidden test of fostering teams would harm trust. This evidence supports recording reply times fairly for everyone, not secret tests.

### Taking up family services

- Drop-out from parenting programmes is put at up to 40%, and 40% to 60% for child mental health services generally (a review of interview studies, citing older work). Grade C, secondhand.
- A focused first phone call that deals with practical obstacles raised attendance at a first appointment in a small randomised study of 108 families (McKay and others, 1996). Grade A, small and old.
- A trial of reminder calls with 262 families raised attendance for one group only and made no difference to people declining the service. Grade A. This fits the reminders review: reminders help people turn up; they do not change the decision.
- *Does it carry over?* Partly. These are parents seeking help, not adults offering to help.

### Paperwork

- A tax office experiment with more than 35,000 people found that simpler notices, with the amount shown clearly, raised claims for a tax credit (Bhargava and Manoli, 2015). Grade A. A one-off claim is not a life decision. Exact figures not checked.

### Avoidable contact

- Some contact with a service happens only because the service did not do something, or did not do it right, the first time. A study with Gloucestershire Constabulary found this in 32% of incidents. Claims of up to 80% are widely repeated but rest on consultants' case studies. Grade C/D.
- *Does it carry over?* The idea does. A person ringing back to ask "did you get my form?" is a sign that the first reply failed, and it can be counted.

## 3. Where people are lost (our own reading, for discussion)

1. **Silence after the first contact.**
2. **Screening before welcoming.**
3. **Long gaps between steps.**
4. **Packs that confuse or overwhelm.**
5. **Unequal replies.** Untested in fostering.
6. **Not saying honestly how long it takes.**
7. **People having to chase for news.**
8. **Losing the person they first spoke to.**
9. **Treating an informed "no" as failure.** A person who decides fostering is not for them after honest information has been served well.

## 4. What it could mean for the standard

- Keep the short form, but stop saying length is the main cause of drop-out.
- Add how long each step takes to "what happens next".
- Measure the gap between each step, not only the first reply.
- Consider recording when a person had to chase for news, without blaming staff.
- Record reply time for every enquiry in the same way, so fairness can be checked from a service's own figures.
- Keep "withdrew, by person" neutral. Never count it against a service.

## 5. Gaps and limits

- The paper search stopped part-way. Not yet searched: kinship carers' first contact with services, living donors and Shared Lives carers, teacher and nurse recruitment, adult social care front doors, and completion figures for GOV.UK forms.
- Our search found no fostering study counting people who start an enquiry form and do not finish it.
- Several fostering studies are 20 or more years old.
