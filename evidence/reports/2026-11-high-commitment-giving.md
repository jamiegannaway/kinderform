# Kept promises outperform persuasion in giving

> **Status:** desk review, version 1, 19 November 2026. It looks at blood donation, stem cell registers and the assessment of people who offer to donate a kidney. An AI-assisted research tool produced it. None of the evidence is about fostering, so every view on what carries over is our own. The figures have **not yet been checked by a person** against the original sources. Many were read from a summary of the study only. Check the source before you quote anything, starting with the list under "Check before public use". Everything we propose is for discussion. It is not yet part of the Kinderform standard, the open standard that the project behind kinderform.org is drafting. The [research log](../research-log.json) says what each review could mean for the project.

The evidence comes from blood donation, stem cell registers and the assessment of people who offer to donate a kidney. It backs the shape of the Kinderform draft standard, but not its numbers.

Three things are well supported:

- A "not now" ends most new relationships, unless the service does 3 things. It explains the "not now" in person at the time. It agrees a date. And it gets back in touch itself shortly before that date.
- Long silences go hand in hand with losing touch.
- In a long assessment, the largest loss comes right after the first form, when nobody manages to speak to the person.

Three things are not supported. No study in these fields tests a 5-minute acknowledgement, a first contact within 1 working day or a 2-week rhythm for keeping in touch. So those figures are reasonable promises, not findings.

A named guide is firmly established in guidance and valued in interviews. Yet no trial has tested it from the volunteer's side. And the United States experience shows that naming a role without defining it produces very uneven practice.

In stem cell registers, ambivalence (mixed feelings about going ahead) is the strongest known predictor of stepping back. That is exactly why Kinderform must treat it as a normal part of a large decision. Kinderform must never measure it to score, rank or predict anyone.

Emotive appeals, urgency, gifts and "another way to help" mostly failed or backfired when tested. So the honest approach Kinderform already prefers is also the better-evidenced one.

Almost all of this evidence measures whether people came back or went ahead. Kinderform's aim is a well-informed decision and a respectful reply. So the direction of each finding matters more than its size.

## How to read this report

Every claim carries one of 3 labels:

- **Evidence** means a published study, with a grade:
  - **A**: randomised or quasi-experimental
  - **B**: large observational or administrative
  - **C**: qualitative or small survey
  - **D**: guidance or opinion
- **Observed practice** means what services or guidelines do or say, without a measured effect.
- **Recommendation (Kinderform)** means this report's own judgement. This includes every judgement about whether a finding carries over to fostering. We call that transfer.

Those judgements are inferences. None of the notes behind this report contains fostering research. So no claim here is evidence about fostering itself.

Some words in this report have a special meaning in research:

- A *randomised* study puts people into groups by chance, so the groups can be compared fairly. A *quasi-experimental* study compares groups that were formed in some other way.
- An *observational* study watches what happens without changing anything. An *administrative* study uses a service's own records. A *cohort* study follows a group of people over time.
- A *qualitative* study uses interviews or open answers, not numbers.
- A *systematic review* gathers all the studies on a question using a set method. A *pooled analysis* (or meta-analysis) combines the results of several studies.
- An *abstract* is the short summary at the start of a study. A *conference abstract* is a short summary given at a conference, not a full paper. A *preprint* is a paper shared before other researchers have reviewed it (peer review).
- An *odds ratio* compares the odds of an outcome in 2 groups. An odds ratio of 1 means no difference; above 1 means higher odds, and below 1 means lower odds. A *hazard ratio* and a *relative risk* are read the same way.
- *Adjusted* means the authors allowed for other differences between the groups.
- A *95% confidence interval* is a range around a result that shows how precise it is. A wide range means less certainty.
- A *p-value* shows how likely a difference this large would be if there were no real effect. Below 0.05 is usually called *significant*.
- A *null* result means a study found no clear effect.
- A *percentage point* is the simple gap between 2 percentages. For example, a rise from 54.3% to 65.0% is a rise of 10.7 percentage points.
- In the tables, OR means odds ratio, aOR adjusted odds ratio, HR hazard ratio and RR relative risk.

Two cautions apply throughout.

1. Almost every blood donation figure and many kidney figures were read from the published abstract only, through a research index. The full texts were not read.
2. Bailey et al. 2017 and the BOUnD study are 2 separate UK studies, and must not be merged.
   - Bailey et al. followed 805 people assessed as *directed* donors at 7 UK centres in 2014 to 2016 ([Bailey et al. 2017](https://core.ac.uk/download/96781141.pdf)). Directed donors offer a kidney to a particular person, usually someone they know.
   - BOUnD, by Mamode and colleagues, is the national study of *unspecified* donors across all 23 UK units ([Mamode et al. 2025](https://library.kab.ac.ug/Record/doaj-art-212fefbb094541b79514cbbc3d908c3e/Description)). Unspecified donors are people offering a kidney to a stranger.

Anything not yet checked, secondhand or internally inconsistent is marked where it appears. It is also gathered in the "Check before public use" list near the end.

## An hour, a procedure, a household: where the analogy holds and where it breaks

**Recommendation (Kinderform).** The 3 fields differ from fostering in ways that limit what can be borrowed. Giving blood takes about an hour and can be repeated a few times a year. A stem cell donation is a single procedure, usually requested years after joining a register. Donating a kidney is one operation after a long assessment. Fostering changes a household for years and involves a child. So in fostering, a considered "no" is likely to be more common than in any of these fields, and more often the right answer.

**Recommendation (Kinderform).** The research also has a different purpose. Blood studies count "came back to donate" as success. Register studies count "available when asked". In kidney donation, the trials of education at home aimed to raise the number of people offering a kidney to a relative or friend. Kinderform does not want more yeses. It wants people to decide well, and to feel respected whichever way they decide.

So this report borrows mechanisms: what silence does, what a kept promise does and how a refusal feels. It also borrows formats, such as a conversation at home with the household. It does not borrow targets or effect sizes (how large an effect was). A 10-point rise in people returning to give blood says nothing reliable about the size of any effect on a decision to foster.

**Recommendation (Kinderform).** What does carry over is the position of the person. In all 4 settings, a volunteer offers something large to an organisation that needs it. They are asked intrusive questions and wait on other people's processes. They can be told "not you" or "not now". Some of the evidence is about how the organisation behaves: speed, clarity, follow-up, honesty and fairness. That part carries over far better than the parts about the volunteer's character.

**Evidence (B).** A United States study surveyed 7,905 blood donors in 2003 and tracked them for 12 months. A convenient place and a good experience predicted whether people actually came back. Altruism, empathy and a sense of social responsibility did not ([Schlumpf et al. 2008](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:b28067b5-85c4-4d87-b293-85ea5ef0e944/)). That is a reason to examine the process before questioning anyone's motivation.

## A "not now" ends most new relationships unless the service keeps its date

### The size of the harm

**Evidence (B).** After being told "not today", far fewer people come back. The drop is steepest for people at their first visit. Blood services call this a deferral: the person cannot give now and is asked to wait.

- In the United States, 25% of first-time donors temporarily deferred in 2000 returned within 5 years, against 47% of eligible first-timers. Among repeat donors it was 81% against 86% ([Custer et al. 2007](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:064cf715-953d-43c8-8369-6e7972cedb45/)).
- In Australia, some donors had a 6-month deferral for low haemoglobin (the part of the blood that carries oxygen). Of the first-time donors, 20.9% returned within 3 years, against 69.9% of those not deferred. Among repeat donors it was 64.0% against 91.0% ([Hillgrove et al. 2011](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:3251bbfe-d55c-4225-8e12-6307ae9c167b/)).
- In the Netherlands, a study covered all 343,825 donors with a whole-blood attempt in 2013 to 2015. First-time donors returned at 69% if deferred, against 82% if not. Experienced donors returned at 92% against 96% ([Spekman et al. 2019](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:4a6bb6a6-82c9-48e9-ac44-39207c4717e8/)).
- In Iran, 51.77% of temporarily deferred volunteers returned over 4.8 years, against 74.77% of eligible donors. The median (middle) time to return was 1,467 days, against 315 ([Maghsudlu et al. 2024](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:c123aba0-95f5-4d3d-802d-1ed7177944b2/)).
- One Brazilian service had no follow-up activity at all. There, deferral was associated with a 50% lower likelihood of return. The study covered more than 2 million attempts over 26 years ([Ferreira et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:8b546132-e539-4a68-a392-19ed76c4af04/)).

**Evidence (B).** Three further patterns matter for the "not now" option.

- **Longer waits do worse.** One study covered 6 United States blood centres in 2006 to 2008. After deferrals of up to a year, the share who returned never rose above 50% ([Custer et al. 2011](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:d1429cf8-fa7d-482c-98d1-b0044e5e08c2/)).
- **Repeated deferrals compound.** In Austrian data on more than 123,000 donors, each extra deferral strengthened the effect ([Clément et al. 2021](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:443452bd-6bf2-47f2-9ec3-b2bd14362265/)). But the abstract's conclusion contradicts its own results on which group is hit harder, so check the full text.
- **People who have come back after a gap are fragile.** Dutch "reactivated" donors returned at 76% if their visit went ahead, and 61% if they were deferred. That is about the same as newcomers ([Spekman et al. 2019](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:4a6bb6a6-82c9-48e9-ac44-39207c4717e8/)).

A softer message does not remove the problem. Some Canadian women were advised, not required, to pause for 6 months. Over 2 years, 77.5% of them returned, against 88.5% of those with normal results. About a third came back early ([Goldman et al. 2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:cbb5531b-99e6-4ee8-9ba8-505f1d2ba0b0/)).

**Evidence (B/C).** Hurt feelings are not the whole story. A pilot study followed 96 first-time donors (country not stated in the abstract, believed Germany). Mood fell straight after deferral. Within 12 months, 36.4% of deferred donors returned, against 60.8% of non-deferred donors. But the drop in mood did not statistically explain who stayed away ([Greffin et al. 2021](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:a03d4b63-8e98-43ce-a662-9b3efb073a7b/)). Hassle and lost momentum probably matter as much.

### What reduced the harm, and what did not

**Evidence (A).** A reminder when the wait is over works, and plain words work better than emotional ones. Australian Red Cross Lifeblood put 1,676 deferred donors into groups at random. One group had no contact. The others had a reminder 4 weeks before, 1 week before or 1 week after their deferral ended. Any reminder doubled the odds of attempting to return within 3 months (odds ratio 2.01). A reminder 1 week before was best. In a second study of 1,973 donors, a plain, factual message beat an emotive one (odds ratio 1.38). Email did as well as text ([Gemelli et al. 2021](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:870c787e-5171-4777-b1e4-9a813c8b8102/)). The abstract does not give the underlying return rates, so no baseline can be reported.

**Evidence (A).** A conversation at the moment of "not now" works modestly; an email alone does not. Lifeblood ran a cluster randomised trial with 3 arms, or groups. A cluster trial puts whole groups of people into arms by chance, rather than one person at a time. In one arm, staff guided a conversation, gave a brochure and sent a follow-up email. This raised the odds of return within 3 months of the deferral ending (odds ratio 1.16, 95% confidence interval 1.00 to 1.33). The effects were larger for novice donors (1.38) and for people who had been deferred before (1.55). For that last figure, the interval is printed as 1.15 to 1.55, which looks like a typing error. **The email-only arm showed no significant difference from usual practice** ([Gemelli et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:aaebd0fb-79af-420d-9d7d-480f3ee6c24f/)). A Dutch randomised field trial in 10 centres found the same for extra information about the deferral. The abstract does not give effect sizes ([Spekman et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:92bca49f-0c4f-4bdd-aebb-399d7e248bc7/)).

**Evidence (A), a null.** In that same Dutch trial, **offering deferred donors an "alternative good deed" did not increase return** ([Spekman et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:92bca49f-0c4f-4bdd-aebb-399d7e248bc7/)). Suggesting another way to help can be kind. But this trial gives no reason to think it keeps the original offer alive.

**Evidence (A and B).** Two lower-pressure measures also have support.

- One study looked at 956 deferred novice donors (believed Quebec). Some made their own "if-then" plans: what they would do about forgetting, fitting it in and transport. Those donors had a 19% greater chance of returning within 4 years (p = 0.04) ([Godin et al. 2012](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:cf5a1452-f201-4c19-bf3c-6e14f2419019/)).
- From July to December 2020, Lifeblood asked eligibility questions at booking. People were then less likely to be deferred on the day (adjusted odds ratio 0.86; 0.79 for new donors). Those still deferred were more likely to return at 12 months (adjusted hazard ratio 1.12) ([Gemelli et al. 2025](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:6108457d-0540-4947-b4f9-82ee6d6cdb42/)).

**Evidence (B, weak).** Personal calls at the end of a deferral have only uncontrolled support. Two Indian programmes in 2026 report that 42.9% and 49.4% of called donors later donated. Neither had a comparison group ([Kaur et al. 2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:b95f71f6-e95e-48c2-8863-9796d07b8573/); [Bansal et al. 2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:379f6c5e-db2b-494f-9176-b9dfcc6a6660/)).

**Evidence (C).** Kidney donor research describes the same harm in people's own words. Australians judged ineligible spoke of a "suspended donor status", changeable criteria, and being "dismissed and discarded by the system" ([Ralph et al. 2019](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:d0c52a25-5500-4b2d-8839-940ab0fef3f1/)).

**Recommendation (Kinderform).** Transfer is strong in direction and weak in size. Everyone who enquires about fostering is at the "first visit" stage, where a "not now" does most damage. The ingredients that trials support are cheap and respectful. Someone from the service explains why at the time. A date is agreed with the person. And the service keeps its promise in plain words about a week before.

Two things do not transfer cleanly. First, in blood donation the service imposes the wait. Kinderform's "not now" is mainly the person's own choice, and no study in these notes tests a self-chosen pause. Second, the if-then planning result is acceptable only on one condition. The person decides for themselves what would need to be true before they come back. Scripted prompts used to push people would breach Kinderform's purpose.

## Silence predicts lost contact, and one message to an old list achieves little

### Recency of contact is the strongest thing a service controls

**Evidence (B).** One study looked at the British Bone Marrow Registry, now the NHS Stem Cell Donor Registry. It covered 10,258 verification requests in 2013 to 2018. In a verification request, the register asks a possible match for more tests. Overall, 36% of people were not available when first asked. The share not available rose with the time since the registry was last in touch:

- in touch in the past 6 months: 20.6%
- 6 months to 2 years: 32.4%
- more than 2 years: 47.4% (odds ratio 3.47, 95% confidence interval 3.13 to 3.86)

In the authors' model, recency of contact was the single most influential feature. It mattered more than time on the register ([Li et al., journal believed to be Biology of Blood and Marrow Transplantation 2020, not yet checked](https://core.ac.uk/download/326512372.pdf)). This is an association: recently contacted people differ in other ways.

**Evidence (B).** Most of the loss is not a change of heart. At Anthony Nolan, a 2013 report covered 7,542 confirmatory typing requests. Like verification requests, these ask a possible match for more tests. Of these requests, 38.2% were cancelled for donor reasons. Of those, 36% were "no contact made", 34.1% medical, 19.4% personal and 7.9% overseas or emigrated ([Lown et al. 2013, conference abstract](https://bmt.confex.com/tandem/2013/webprogram/Paper2256.html)). On the NHS registry the split was medical 41.1%, ability to contact 29.8%, personal commitment 11.4% and other 17.6% ([Li et al.](https://core.ac.uk/download/326512372.pdf)). Three different things hide inside "dropped out": cannot be reached, circumstances changed, and a considered no.

**Evidence (A, quasi-experimental).** One study with a strong design shows that a small, concrete check-in helps a little and sorts people honestly. DKMS Germany invited people on its register to send a fresh sample, to say when they were temporarily unavailable, or both. The study then looked at 91,479 later requests. Against a baseline availability of 77.1%, being invited raised availability by 2.5 to 3.2 percentage points. Among those who took part, the rise was 3.8 to 8.2 points. People who responded were more than 10 points more available than those who did not. The authors read this partly as a signal of who is still willing ([Haylock et al. 2022](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29857)).

A university press release puts the strongest effect as a 37% relative cut in later unavailability. That figure is secondhand and should be checked against the journal article ([University of Tübingen](https://uni-tuebingen.de/en/university/news-and-publications/press-releases/press-releases/article/better-contact-increases-stem-cell-donors-availability/)). No figures show how many people used the "temporarily unavailable" option, or came back after it.

### Re-contacting people after a long gap: mostly small effects and nulls

**Evidence (A), nulls.** A single message or call to an old list does little.

- In Guangzhou, 11,880 inactive donors were put at random into groups: a phone call, a text or nothing. The study followed them for a year. It counted everyone in the group they were put in, whether or not they were reached. This is called intention to treat. On that count, **there was no significant difference in re-donation between the three groups**. But calls helped among people actually reached ([Ou-Yang et al. 2020](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:dbe3b8e0-71f8-447e-80b4-16d6ac80f0c5/)).
- In Nigeria, 2,809 lapsed donors (people who had stopped giving) were put at random into groups: text, WhatsApp, phone or nothing. Of these, 27.7% responded positively, almost all by phone. But **one person donated within 30 days (0.04%)** ([Ogbenna et al. 2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:43ae0c40-dc78-478a-abc7-ee6adb814251/)).
- A study of 1,022 lapsed donors (believed Switzerland) found that overall return after 10 months was 3%. **Adding a "save lives" message made no difference** ([Moussaoui et al. 2016](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:8880c7cb-ccf9-4564-bd04-376d89a106a7/)).

**Evidence (A and C).** What has some support is quieter.

- One study looked at 7,000 donors who had lapsed for 2 years or more (believed Quebec). Some were simply sent a short questionnaire about their intentions. Against no contact, this raised registrations to give blood at 6 and 15 months (p < 0.05). The absolute rates are not in the abstract ([Godin et al. 2013](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:54fa8d57-f711-4365-82c5-66b29ba9c324/)).
- A German survey asked 1,263 people who had not given for more than 36 months. Of these, 44.9% had not consciously decided to stop, and only 16.9% had. A quarter of the first group were willing to restart. They asked for flexible hours (29.1%), online booking (24.8%) and reminders (18.1%). The authors suggest "pause options" ([Saltzmann et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:9c87188d-71c1-4c40-9d25-b00395f0872c/)).

### How much contact is too much: nobody has measured it

**Evidence (A and B).** At the levels of contact tested, more contact did no measurable harm. In England, the INTERVAL extension trial put 20,757 donors at random into groups with more intensive or routine reminders. It found slightly more blood collected (0.11 units a year in men, 0.06 in women). It found no detectable difference in quality of life ([Kaptoge et al. 2019](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:fc8cf664-7a98-47d8-b0b1-338b6ac62fa5/)). In Australia, 36,418 donors were invited into research. They were slightly more likely to donate afterwards than matched controls: similar donors who were not invited (relative risk 1.18 at one month) ([Kruse et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:4a65c47d-fcc6-4c61-a3ee-a35129ad629f/)). **But no study found measures opt-outs, complaints or harm from frequent contact.** And none tests how often to keep in touch during a long assessment.

**Evidence (C) and Observed practice (D).** What kidney donor research does show is that silence hurts. BOUnD interviewed 35 people who offered a kidney to a stranger. Those who did not go on reported dissatisfaction and lasting emotional effects "linked to lack of follow-up from transplant teams" ([Zuchowski et al. 2021](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:3e587fff-8088-42a1-b4ec-c1b2b4142f6e/)). Another study looked at young people who had joined a United States stem cell register. Speaking with register staff only once or twice was independently associated with opting out. Independently means the link held after allowing for other factors ([Hamed et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36455856/)).

UK kidney guidance sets a week-by-week pathway ending at week 18 ([BTS/Renal Association 2018](https://bts.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/BTS_LDKT_UK_Guidelines_2018.pdf)). Yet people who donated in the 2014 to 2016 UK cohort spent a median of 308 days, about 44 weeks, in assessment ([Bailey et al. 2017](https://core.ac.uk/download/96781141.pdf)). In a 2025 survey, most centres said they offered 18 weeks. But the survey was self-reported and internally inconsistent ([Nightingale et al. 2025](https://www.frontierspartnerships.org/articles/10.3389/ti.2025.15341/full)). In Canada and Australia, a donor's wait was often driven by someone else's process. Each month's delay in the recipient's referral added 0.9 months ([Habbous et al. 2018](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:f29c658c-1913-4999-b563-4a154289999a/)).

**Recommendation (Kinderform).** Transfer is high for the principle and nil for the number. The evidence supports keeping in touch, and telling people when a wait is not about them. It also supports measuring the time that has passed from the person's side, instead of trusting a service's description of itself. It does not support "every two weeks" over every three or four. The claim that delay causes people to withdraw is plausible and widely asserted, but untested. Say "associated with".

## The largest loss comes straight after the first form

**Evidence (B).** Where counting starts decides the headline figure. From a first form, 6% to 8% of people at 2 United States centres went on to donate a kidney. From the start of formal assessment, the figure was 15% to 20% in UK and United States cohorts across several centres. From research consent or a screened assessment day, it was 45% (BOUnD) to 66% (Belfast, secondhand through a journal blog).

- At the Cleveland Clinic, a study covered 3,001 first forms in 2016 to 2019. The largest single loss came immediately after the form. The main reason was that the person did not respond to the follow-up phone call (22.6%) ([Cholin et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:6baa6682-0f01-4c80-a62d-9a86cd416f39/)).
- At a rural United States centre, 1,776 people referred themselves. Of these, 63.0% left after the first form. The main reasons were that the centre lost touch with them (32.1%) or that they changed their mind (24.6%) ([Dobbs et al. 2025](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:9deac9be-197b-4f43-a400-cc78003dcd79/)).
- In the UK directed-donor cohort, 15.2% donated and 15.0% withdrew. Those who withdrew did so early, after a median of 61 days ([Bailey et al. 2017](https://core.ac.uk/download/96781141.pdf)).

**Observed practice (D, press release).** In June 2025, NHS Blood and Transplant gave a figure for people who had registered to give blood in England. It said "only 1 in 4 (24 per cent)" had gone on to donate ([NHSBT, 9 June 2025](https://www.blood.co.uk/news-and-campaigns/news-and-statements/nhs-in-england-calls-for-one-million-people-to-donate-blood-to-secure-the-nation-s-blood-supply/)). Even for a one-hour act with a national brand, most people who put their name down do not take the next step.

**Evidence (A).** Personal contact after a first step beats silence. In the better blood trials, the gain was about 10 percentage points.

- A randomised trial of 418 first-time donors tested a call 9 to 14 weeks after their first donation. The call began by asking how the first experience had been. It raised return within 6 months from 54.3% to 65.0% ([Marsano et al. 2024](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:71db5d8f-ca7e-425b-b1d5-0a770bcd524f/)).
- A Quebec quasi-experiment found 48.3% against 38.0% within 12 months ([Godin et al. 2011](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:9510567e-8d76-43e8-806d-d165689318c3/)).
- A pooled analysis of 7 trials gives a relative gain of 25% for any active contact, with **educational messages inconclusive**. It is a conference abstract and provisional ([Silva et al. 2025](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:acba8814-c3f7-4354-a296-a4f047806019/)).

A person on the phone did as well as text or email, or better.

**Evidence (A), nulls.** A T-shirt made no difference (20.5% against 20.6%) ([Reich et al. 2006](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:d805065f-7ff6-4961-a1df-d366e0de4cba/)). In Iran, a "motivational meeting" did no better than no contact (22% against 22.1%) ([Hashemi et al. 2019](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:2d722a8e-f4d6-4d53-a10f-d47ab8aaf066/)). Motivational interviewing is a style of conversation that helps people talk through their own reasons for and against a change. Automated web versions of it showed no advantage in what people actually did ([Jensen et al. 2020](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:685b78f1-4ae1-4815-b961-65061d1bf1fb/)).

**Evidence (A), a weaker result than its reputation.** The celebrated "your blood was used" text has one positive Australian study, which was not randomised. In it, 70.3% returned within 12 months, against 62.6% ([Gemelli et al. 2017](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:90cf5776-f065-4136-9c3d-f7fa57112ad4/)). One randomised trial of 20,365 donors (believed Denmark) found **no significant effect among whole-blood donors** ([Fosgaard et al. 2020](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:dadee526-1695-4c3a-832f-47e08a2b74a4/)). The Swedish claim about such texts rests on press reporting. NHS Blood and Transplant has published no evaluation of its own scheme ([NHSBT, 14 June 2016](https://www.blood.co.uk/news-and-campaigns/news-and-statements/blood-donors-texted-when-their-blood-goes-to-hospitals/)). It should not be cited as proven.

**Recommendation (Kinderform).** Transfer is high for the shape. The gap between a form and a first conversation is where people silently disappear. Services should record "we could not reach this person" as its own outcome, never folded into "withdrew". Nothing in these notes tests how fast the first reply must be. The 5-minute and 1-working-day promises rest on respect and common sense, and Kinderform should say so.

## Being turned away well has five parts, none of them medical

**Evidence (C).** People turned down as kidney donors commonly experience it as a personal failure. What made it worse was the handling, not the decision. Interviews with 27 Australians described "devastating loss and disappointment", being "ill prepared for rejection", and "having the donor role stolen" ([Ralph et al. 2019](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:d0c52a25-5500-4b2d-8839-940ab0fef3f1/)). A United States survey asked people who had been turned down, and 64 of 193 replied (a 33.2% response). Of those who replied, 50% felt disappointed and 34% sad, and 72% felt proud to have considered donating. **None regretted starting**, and 23% wanted a follow-up call after the decision ([Calkins et al. 2025](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:49aba195-5d11-4572-aada-80ee0cffd119/)).

**Evidence (B/C).** Standard questionnaires miss this. At the University of Pennsylvania, a study compared 43 people turned down in 2010 to 2013 with 128 donors. Their scores for quality of life, depression and financial stress were similar. Yet **32% said their life was worse after the centre's decision against 7% of donors** (p < 0.01) ([Reese et al. 2018](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:67aa9b04-5370-437e-bd67-101d2337d9f3/)). One direct question found what the scales did not.

**Observed practice (D).** UK guidance already states what is owed to people found unsuitable:

- a policy must be in place for them
- they "must be identified at the earliest possible stage"
- a second opinion "must always be available"
- a follow-up appointment is arranged to discuss results
- the need for emotional support "must be anticipated" ([BTS/Renal Association 2018](https://bts.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/BTS_LDKT_UK_Guidelines_2018.pdf))

BOUnD names support for people who do not go ahead as a priority. It recommends preparing people from the very beginning "for all possible outcomes" ([Zuchowski et al. 2021](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:3e587fff-8088-42a1-b4ec-c1b2b4142f6e/); [Mamode et al. 2025](https://library.kab.ac.ug/Record/doaj-art-212fefbb094541b79514cbbc3d908c3e/Description)).

**Evidence (B).** Being asked and then not needed is routine. At Anthony Nolan, 5.5% of requests were cancelled for transplant centre reasons ([Lown et al. 2013](https://bmt.confex.com/tandem/2013/webprogram/Paper2256.html)). No register study found measures how that feels, or whether those people are available next time.

**Recommendation (Kinderform).** Transfer is high. Put together, the evidence describes 5 parts of a decent "no":

1. Say at the start how often people do not go through, and that it is not a judgement of their worth.
2. Give the decision by voice, with time.
3. Give the real reason and what it means for the person.
4. Offer a route to a review.
5. Get in touch again afterwards, because the reaction comes later.

No trial has tested any of the 5. Naming other real ways to help answers the loss of identity that people describe. But the Dutch null result means it should be offered as a kindness, not counted on to keep the fostering offer alive. It should never be used as a brush-off.

## A named guide is established practice with no trial behind it

**Observed practice (D).** Transplant services have ended up with 3 roles around the volunteer.

- A coordinator organises and keeps in touch. UK guidance says most centres have designated staff who are a main source of support ([BTS/Renal Association 2018](https://bts.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/BTS_LDKT_UK_Guidelines_2018.pdf)).
- An independent person protects the volunteer's interests. In the UK, an Independent Assessor accredited by the Human Tissue Authority interviews every donor separately. United States rules require an independent living donor advocate who "protects the donor's right to withdraw" ([Hays et al. 2015](https://www.notifylibrary.org/sites/default/files/GUIDANCE%20DOC%20AST_The%20independent%20living%20donor%20advocate_a%20guidance%20document%20from%20the%20AST%E2%80%99s%20LDCOP.pdf)).
- A peer who has done it before offers support. Ontario donors asked for this role ([Getchell et al. 2017](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:8a500c9d-09de-4aa4-acd3-8239dd6634be/)).

**Evidence (C and B).** Volunteers say the quality of contact shapes everything. For 18 Danish potential donors, experience "largely depended on the quality of their communication and interaction" with staff ([Agerskov et al. 2015](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:a6b5a60b-e2f0-424a-87d4-884aafc62959/)). In BOUnD, withdrawal was less likely in high-volume, well-staffed centres ([Mamode et al. 2025](https://library.kab.ac.ug/Record/doaj-art-212fefbb094541b79514cbbc3d908c3e/Description)).

**Evidence (B and A), cautions.** Naming a role does not deliver it.

- A national survey of 120 United States advocates found wide variation in background, training and practice. Only 18% thought "not being part of the donor team" necessary for independence. And 53% attended donor selection meetings. These figures are quoted secondhand through Hays et al. ([Steel et al. 2012](https://scholars.mssm.edu/en/publications/a-national-survey-of-independent-living-donor-advocates-the-need--2/)).
- Navigator and champion programmes report large effects (hazard ratios of 5.8 to 9.3). But these measure patients finding donors, among people who chose to take part. They must not be quoted as the effect of a guide for the volunteer ([Locke et al. 2019](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:7ec63a9c-969b-4b87-9709-70de6cbdae85/); [King et al. 2020](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:f26b480d-eaf8-4756-871c-08629db782d3/)).
- There is one randomised test of adding a peer mentor, among 319 Black patients in 2018 to 2020. It found **no significant difference in transplants within a year** (0% usual care, 5% house calls, 6% house calls plus mentor; p = 0.07). It found no clear added benefit from the mentor ([Rodrigue et al. 2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:662d2c1a-bc32-4c0a-84ea-11292a3a0e46/)).

No controlled study was found of a coordinator, a named nurse or an advocate, measured from the volunteer's side.

**Recommendation (Kinderform).** Transfer is high for the named contact and the peer, and moderate for the independent role. Keep the named journey guide, and be honest that it rests on guidance and interviews. Define it by duties instead of a job title: what the guide must do, by when, and what happens when they are away.

The independent role raises a harder point. An agency that needs carers has a built-in interest, like a transplant team that needs a kidney. Transplant practice answers that with someone whose only loyalty is to the volunteer. Kinderform should at least require that the guide's stated job is to support the person's decision either way. Does anything independent exist for people at the enquiry stage in England? Check this against Department for Education and Ofsted sources before making any claim about it.

## Ambivalence is normal, predictive, and not to be scored

**Evidence (B).** One study interviewed 1,067 United States stem cell registrants who had been contacted about a possible match. Registrants are people who have joined a register. In every ethnic group, ambivalence was the strongest predictor of opting out. The adjusted odds ratios were 14.1 to 22.2 in 3 groups. Among Hispanic registrants the figure was 94.6, but that is too imprecise to quote as exact ([Switzer et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3578960)).

A 2023 study of 935 registrants aged 18 to 30 found the same. Its model classified 86.6% of people correctly ([Hamed et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36455856/)). The registry's summary of that study reports 2 more findings. These are secondhand. Joining "because it was easy" was associated with later opting out. Having medical concerns was associated with continuing ([NMDP summary](https://network.nmdp.org/research/research-publications/factors-associated-with-young-adults-opting-out-of-donation-for-hct)). One reading is that voiced worries get answered and silent doubt does not. The paper does not test that.

**Evidence (B, secondhand).** Pressure appears to backfire. A 2025 systematic review reports findings from earlier work it cites. Each of these was associated with dropping out:

- feeling pressured when contacted about a match
- less contact with staff
- staff not explaining the process

Receiving information was associated with staying ([Forbes et al. 2025](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/182833/1/British%20J%20Health%20Psychol%20-%202024%20-%20Forbes%20-%20Factors%20influencing%20unrelated%20stem%20cell%20donation%20a%20mixed%E2%80%90methods%20integrated.pdf)).

**Observed practice (D).** The researchers who found the ambivalence effect say what to do with it:

- address the specific worries
- offer a "cooling-off" period, after which the person gets back in touch themselves
- use brief motivational interviewing aimed at comfort with the decision, "not at encouraging donation" ([Switzer et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3578960))

None of these has been evaluated.

**Evidence (A), mixed.** Motivational interviewing in blood donation has one positive small trial (odds ratio 2.48 among 215 donors). One trial of 484 found no overall effect on behaviour. Automated versions showed no benefit. A 2026 systematic review of 36 studies calls the evidence "conflicting" ([Sinclair et al. 2010](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:58227acd-06e0-436b-b1b0-e4148f7e913b/); [France et al. 2017](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:c7992c4b-c4eb-4f7c-8941-a9776c0a67a1/); [Boult et al. 2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:79c5d7a6-db05-4926-9df1-93a7d8ead50e/)).

**Observed practice (D).** The clearest ethical model is consent renewed at each stage. The World Marrow Donor Association (WMDA) standards say adult donation "must be voluntary". They also say "a donor must be free to withdraw at any time" ([WMDA 2024](https://wmda.info/storage/2024/01/2024-WMDA-Stnds_20240101-final.pdf)). UK kidney guidance says 3 things ([BTS/Renal Association 2018](https://bts.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/BTS_LDKT_UK_Guidelines_2018.pdf)):

- a person may withdraw "without having to provide an explanation"
- this "should not be regarded as a failure"
- the assessment team's "primary responsibility" is to support the donor in deciding

**Evidence (B) and Observed practice (D).** Suspicion of an unusual offer is not supported. BOUnD compared people donating to a stranger with people donating to someone they know. It found no difference in outcomes, regret or withdrawal (hazard ratio 1.12, 95% confidence interval 0.75 to 1.67). But the stranger-donors reported fewer positive perceptions of the experience (79.9% against 86.2%) ([Mamode et al. 2025](https://library.kab.ac.ug/Record/doaj-art-212fefbb094541b79514cbbc3d908c3e/Description)). In Israel, 23.4% of 598 unrelated candidates were disqualified in 2008 to 2016. Of those disqualified, 41% were disqualified on mental health grounds, using criteria such as "ego strength" ([Ashkenazi et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:d6fa5395-0d16-4d4c-a0b6-daa6319b4ca2/)). This is a picture of where vague motive-testing ends up.

**Recommendation (Kinderform).** Transfer is high as a principle and forbidden as a tool. For a decision as large as fostering, doubt at enquiry is probably wiser than certainty. Services should make room for people to voice it, and answer worries straight. They should say at the start that stopping is always allowed and is not a failure.

Kidney donation has a so-called "medical alibi". A team gives a general reason so that a reluctant person can step back without blame. The notes summarise this from background knowledge and unread sources, so treat it as not yet checked. Its honest form is worth testing: one truthful, non-specific form of words ("this is not going ahead at the moment"). It would be agreed with the person and used for everyone. Nothing would be invented, and any safeguarding concern would be recorded truthfully.

## Equity: the gaps open where the system loses touch or asks without explaining

**Evidence (B).** On the NHS stem cell registry, people from most minority ethnic groups were more likely to be unavailable when asked. They were roughly 2 to 4 times as likely to be unavailable as White British registrants. For example, the odds ratio was 2.95 for Black African, 2.30 for Black Caribbean and 2.15 for Asian Pakistani registrants. The authors say the gap was mainly due to inability to contact them. People with no recorded ethnicity were also far less available (2.41) ([Li et al.](https://core.ac.uk/download/326512372.pdf)). Subgroup percentages in that paper's table do not add up, so rely on the odds ratios. Anthony Nolan data show the same pattern ([Lown et al. 2013](https://bmt.confex.com/tandem/2013/webprogram/Paper2256.html)). This points at out-of-date records and thin relationships more than at attitudes.

**Evidence (B).** In the UK, a study of 981 donors and non-donors found trust lower in every domain among ethnic minority respondents. For ethnic minority non-donors, trust in *individuals* predicted willingness. For White non-donors, it was trust in the blood service ([Ferguson et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:476ad961-85fa-4083-bd5c-5b4dfda777a0/)). Eligibility questions fell unevenly. In 2015 to 2019, 8% to 9.3% of ethnic minority donors were deferred on travel grounds, against 1.7% of White donors. And questions asked without explanation led to discouraging word of mouth, through perceived discrimination ([Ferguson et al. 2024](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:3a211c85-e708-4276-a1fb-a1b30aa6b795/)). Deferral sharply reduces return, so the 2 effects compound.

**Evidence (B).** Looking step by step shows where gaps open.

- At one United States kidney centre, African American candidates were about half as likely to get from referral to donation (adjusted hazard ratio 0.47). The gap was at 2 steps that need time off, travel and household support. There was no gap at first referral or at the approval decision ([Kumar et al. 2018](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:6ebffcd5-0a6b-4932-9ae6-7214a035ed5b/)).
- In the UK directed-donor cohort, non-white ethnicity was associated with withdrawal (odds ratio 2.98, 95% confidence interval 1.05 to 8.44). But this rests on very few people, in a cohort that was 92.8% white. People from the most deprived fifth of areas were about half as likely to donate (odds ratio 0.49, p = 0.05). The trend across all groups was consistent with chance ([Bailey et al. 2017](https://core.ac.uk/download/96781141.pdf)).

**Evidence (B) and Observed practice (D).** Cost is a real barrier. A systematic review found about 80% of donors had a financial loss, with lost income the largest share ([Fu et al. 2020](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:4d1200df-6f23-4d75-a737-bf32a5743bdb/)). NHS England's policy is that there should be "no financial incentive or disincentive". But people assessed and found unsuitable can claim travel and parking only ([NHS England 2021](https://www.england.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2017/08/Reimbursement-of-Expenses-for-Living-Donors-A06PA-Dec-2021.pdf)).

**Evidence (A), a possible backfire.** More information is not always the answer. A United States trial tested a patient education programme (a preprint, not peer reviewed). There was no overall effect. The authors also ran an exploratory analysis, beyond the main test the trial was designed for. In it, the programme was associated with *lower* completion among people who had experienced discrimination in health care (hazard ratio 0.56). It helped those who had not (1.32) ([Velez-Bermudez et al. 2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:10d4f5ad-9556-4cf6-b27a-205616094e50/)).

**Evidence (A/B, weak).** A 2019 review found only 8 intervention studies on blood donation in minority ethnic communities, all rated low quality ([Makin et al. 2019](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:c98531c7-608f-4d5a-a55b-660716cc8b8f/)). No evaluation was found that shows any register change closing the availability gap.

**Recommendation (Kinderform).** Transfer is high as a method. Before anyone concludes that a group is "less committed", check whether the service lost touch with them or never answered their questions.

The standard should let a service see, as group totals, the step at which different groups of people leave. It should require that every early question comes with a stated reason, or is dropped from first contact. The likely fostering equivalents are this report's assumptions, and need checking against current practice. They include questions on housing tenure (owning or renting), immigration status or time in the UK. They also include old rules that people believe still apply.

Trust runs through people, so who replies matters. The standard should also ask services to say at enquiry who bears the costs of assessment. They should say what happens to those costs if the person is not approved. All of this must use group totals and synthetic (made-up) examples only. It must never rank an individual.

## What Kinderform should not adopt

**Recommendation (Kinderform).** Kinderform's purpose rules out several things that appear in this research. Most of them also failed when tested.

| Do not adopt | Why |
|---|---|
| Any ambivalence scale, "risk factor" count or availability prediction used to score, rank, filter or prioritise people who enquire | A firm project rule. The register research proposes exactly this ([Li et al.](https://core.ac.uk/download/326512372.pdf) suggest a prediction score to choose between donors). Ambivalence may only prompt a conversation the person controls. Treating a reply to a check-in as a "signal" of commitment, as the DKMS authors do, is also out |
| Urgency, guilt or loss framing ("a child is waiting", "would you want someone there for a child in your family?") | A loss-framed text worked in a 14-day blood emergency in China ([Ou-Yang et al. 2021](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:c59d0af8-b26c-40b0-9861-42a9088f3218/)). Emotive reminders did worse than plain ones, and "save lives" added nothing. Reciprocity framing (appeals to give in return) edges towards guilt. Its authors estimate real behaviour at about a tenth of stated willingness ([Ferguson et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:9a9a5fa0-1185-4f1c-a0e6-9ae53b216763/)) |
| Gifts, rewards or incentives for enquiring or continuing | A T-shirt did nothing. Gift cards shifted timing with no lasting effect, and a surprise reward was followed by *less* donation ([Lacetera, Macis and Slonim 2014](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:35dfe955-1861-41d1-856c-faa2e5a3f7f3/)). In one Swedish experiment, a small cash payment almost halved women's willingness ([Mellström and Johannesson 2008](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:cafe42a9-2924-4249-a986-86a5fa377deb/)). A review of 71 papers found no incentive that is liked, effective and safe ([Chell et al. 2018](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:b620173f-b09a-46e5-be6f-d798f42bf95b/)). None of this settles anything about fostering allowances, which carers need to do the job |
| Asking for an explicit commitment or pledge early | It raised first attendance by 11.5 points in one blood trial ([Wevers et al. 2014](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:e375dd97-2e85-452d-8909-c54f96b2fcdf/)). That is persuasion, not information |
| Home visits or family sessions designed to increase offers | The kidney trials aim to raise the number of people who offer. UK families named coercion and family pressure as risks ([Bailey et al. 2021](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:2f7ca797-c12a-4645-84fa-e6192bfd6e18/)) |
| A check-in where "yes, still interested" is the easy answer | Services must offer "pause" and "remove me" as equal options, in the same place and with the same effort. Otherwise the check-in becomes a nudge |
| Invented reasons to help someone step back | An honest general statement only; never a false record |
| Counting a friendly reply as a decision | In Nigeria 27.7% responded warmly and one person donated ([Ogbenna et al. 2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:43ae0c40-dc78-478a-abc7-ee6adb814251/)) |
| Extra motive-testing for people whose offer seems unusual | Not supported by BOUnD. It needs a stated reason, a consistent method and an explanation to the person |

## Proposed changes to the Kinderform standard

Every row is a **Recommendation (Kinderform)**. "Basis" names the strongest evidence and its grade. Where the basis is principle alone, it says so.

| Element | Verdict | Proposed wording or change | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acknowledge within 5 minutes | Confirm, and label honestly | Keep. State in the standard that this is a courtesy promise with no trial behind it. The acknowledgement should say what happens next, when, and that enquiring commits the person to nothing | Principle. Nearest evidence: silent loss after the first form (B) |
| A person makes first contact within 1 working day | Tighten | Keep the promise. Add 3 things. Make more than one attempt, at different times and by the channel the person chose. Record "not yet reached" as its own outcome. Start the first conversation by listening | Largest loss is the unreached follow-up call (B). Phone at least as good as text or email (A). Listening call +10.7 points (A, blood). The one-day figure is untested |
| "Not now" with a chosen date of 3, 6 or 12 months | Tighten | The person picks any date, with 3, 6 and 12 months as suggestions. The pause is agreed in conversation where possible, not by email alone. The service makes contact about a week before the date, in plain factual words. That message offers 3 equal options: talk now, pause again, remove me. Record how many times a person has paused or been asked to wait. Treat a returning person as a priority | Reminder a week before, plain tone (A). Conversation works; email alone does not (A). Longer waits and repeated deferrals do worse (B). ## Gaps

- No fostering evidence is in these notes. Every transfer judgement is an inference.
- No study tests how fast a first reply must be, or how often to keep in touch during a long assessment.
- No study measures opt-outs, complaints or harm from frequent contact.
- No randomised trial of a personal call at the end of a waiting period. No test of a stated date without a reminder. No test of a pause the person chose themselves.
- No trial of a coordinator, advocate or named guide measured from the volunteer's side. No UK evaluation of peer buddy schemes for potential donors.
- No trial of any way of giving a refusal or of follow-up afterwards. Almost nothing on people who withdrew of their own accord.
- No register study of people stood down: asked, and then told they were not needed.
- No evaluation of a cooling-off period or of any work on ambivalence in registers.
- No study relating the length of gaps in contact to withdrawal.
- No England figures for return after deferral, for first-time donors giving again, or for how many people come forward as potential living donors.
- No evaluation showing any measure that closed an ethnic gap in availability or progression.
- No evidence on whether telling people at the outset that they may stop without explanation changes what they do or how they feel.

## Questions Kinderform could test openly

**Recommendation (Kinderform).** Each of these can be tested with services that volunteer. The method would be published first, and anything public would use synthetic examples. The outcomes would measure understanding and respect before anything else.

- Do people who are told at first contact that they can stop at any time, without explanation, report feeling better informed and less pressured?
- A plain reminder could come a week before a self-chosen "not now" date, offering talk, pause and remove as equal options. Does it leave people feeling respected? How many choose each option?
- Does an agreed contact rhythm work better for people than a fixed two-week rule? Judge this by one direct question about how the process left them.
- Suppose a service says "not now" or "no" by voice, with a reason and a later call. Compared with a letter, do people understand why and know what happens next?
- Does separating "not reached", "paused", "person's decision" and "service's decision" change what a service learns about where people leave?
- Does stating a reason beside each question at first contact change who completes the form, counted as group totals?
- Would people who enquire want access to someone independent of the service, and at what point?
- Does offering other real ways to help after a "no" feel like respect or like a brush-off?

## Conclusion

The most useful thing these fields offer Kinderform is a correction of emphasis. The draft standard puts its precision into speeds and intervals: 5 minutes, 1 day, 2 weeks. That is where the evidence is silent. The evidence is loudest about moments the draft treats lightly:

- the conversation in which someone is asked to wait
- the promise to call back on a date
- the way a "no" is given
- the year-long silence in which a willing person's details quietly go out of date

A standard that is exact about those moments, and openly modest about its clock times, would be both better evidenced and more honest.

The second lesson is that persuasion is not being sacrificed for principle. Some studies tested urgency, gifts, emotive wording and substitute good deeds against plain information and a kept promise. The plain version usually did as well or better. Kinderform is in a position these fields have not reached. It can test the open questions above with respect and understanding as the outcomes. And it can publish the null results as prominently as the rest.
