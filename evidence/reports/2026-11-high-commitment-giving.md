# Kept promises outperform persuasion in giving

> **Status:** desk review, version 1, 19 November 2026. It looks at blood donation, stem cell registers and the assessment of people who offer to donate a kidney. An AI-assisted research tool produced it. None of the evidence is about fostering, so every view on what carries over is our own. The figures have **not yet been checked by a person** against the original sources, and many were read from a summary of the study only. Check the source before you quote anything, starting with the list under "Check before public use". Everything we propose is for discussion and is not yet part of the OUCM standard, the open standard that the project behind kinderform.org is drafting. The [research log](../research-log.json) says what each review could mean for the project.

The evidence from blood donation, stem cell registries and living kidney donor assessment backs the shape of the OUCM draft standard but not its numbers. Three things are well supported: a "not now" ends most new relationships unless the service explains it in person at the time, agrees a date and gets back in touch itself shortly before that date; long silences go hand in hand with losing touch; and the largest loss in a long assessment comes right after the first form, when nobody manages to speak to the person. Three things are not supported: no study in these fields tests a five-minute acknowledgement, a one-working-day first contact or a two-week keeping-in-touch rhythm, so those figures are reasonable promises, not findings. A named guide is firmly established in guidance and valued in interviews, yet has never been trialled from the volunteer's side, and the United States experience shows that naming a role without defining it produces very uneven practice. Ambivalence is the strongest known predictor of stepping back in stem cell registries, which is exactly why OUCM must treat it as a normal part of a large decision and never measure it to score, rank or predict anyone. Emotive appeals, urgency, gifts and "another way to help" mostly failed or backfired when tested, so the honest approach OUCM already prefers is also the better-evidenced one. Almost all of this evidence measures whether people came back or went ahead; OUCM's aim is a well-informed decision and a respectful reply, so the direction of each finding matters more than its size.

## How to read this report

Every claim carries one of three labels. **Evidence** means a published study, with a grade: **A** randomised or quasi-experimental; **B** large observational or administrative; **C** qualitative or small survey; **D** guidance or opinion. **Observed practice** means what services or guidelines do or say, without a measured effect. **Recommendation (OUCM)** means this report's own judgement, including every judgement about whether a finding carries over to fostering. Those judgements are inferences. None of the notes behind this report contains fostering research, so no claim here is evidence about fostering itself.

Two cautions apply throughout. First, almost every blood donation figure and many kidney figures were read from the published abstract only, through a research index; full texts were not read. Second, Bailey et al. 2017 and the BOUnD study are two separate UK studies and must not be merged: Bailey et al. followed 805 people assessed as *directed* donors at seven UK centres in 2014 to 2016 ([Bailey et al. 2017](https://core.ac.uk/download/96781141.pdf)); BOUnD, by Mamode and colleagues, is the national study of *unspecified* donors (people offering a kidney to a stranger) across all 23 UK units ([Mamode et al. 2025](https://library.kab.ac.ug/Record/doaj-art-212fefbb094541b79514cbbc3d908c3e/Description)). Anything not yet checked, secondhand or internally inconsistent is marked where it appears and gathered in the "check before public use" list near the end.

## An hour, a procedure, a household: where the analogy holds and where it breaks

**Recommendation (OUCM).** The three fields differ from fostering in ways that limit what can be borrowed. Giving blood takes about an hour and can be repeated a few times a year. A stem cell donation is a single procedure, usually requested years after joining a register. Donating a kidney is one operation after a long assessment. Fostering changes a household for years and involves a child. A considered "no" is therefore likely to be more common in fostering, and more often the right answer, than in any of these fields.

**Recommendation (OUCM).** The purpose of the research also differs. Blood studies count "came back to donate" as success. Registry studies count "available when asked". The home-based education trials in kidney donation set out to increase the number of people who offer a kidney to a relative or friend. OUCM does not want more yeses; it wants people to decide well and to feel respected whichever way they decide. So this report borrows mechanisms (what silence does, what a kept promise does, how a refusal feels) and formats (a conversation at home with the household), and it does not borrow targets or effect sizes. A 10-point rise in people returning to give blood says nothing reliable about the size of any effect on a decision to foster.

**Recommendation (OUCM).** What does carry over is the position of the person. In all four settings a volunteer offers something large to an organisation that needs it, is asked intrusive questions, waits on other people's processes, and can be told "not you" or "not now". The parts of the evidence about how the organisation behaves (speed, clarity, follow-up, honesty, fairness) transfer far better than the parts about the volunteer's character. **Evidence (B).** A United States study that surveyed 7,905 blood donors in 2003 and tracked them for 12 months found that a convenient place and a good experience predicted actual return, while altruism, empathy and sense of social responsibility did not ([Schlumpf et al. 2008](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:b28067b5-85c4-4d87-b293-85ea5ef0e944/)). That is a reason to examine the process before questioning anyone's motivation.

## A "not now" ends most new relationships unless the service keeps its date

### The size of the harm

**Evidence (B).** Being told "not today" is followed by far fewer people coming back, and the drop is steepest for people at their first visit. In the United States, 25% of first-time donors temporarily deferred in 2000 returned within five years against 47% of eligible first-timers; among repeat donors it was 81% against 86% ([Custer et al. 2007](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:064cf715-953d-43c8-8369-6e7972cedb45/)). In Australia, after a six-month deferral for low haemoglobin, 20.9% of first-time donors returned within three years against 69.9% of those not deferred; among repeat donors 64.0% against 91.0% ([Hillgrove et al. 2011](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:3251bbfe-d55c-4225-8e12-6307ae9c167b/)). In the Netherlands, among all 343,825 donors with a whole-blood attempt in 2013 to 2015, first-time donors returned at 69% if deferred against 82% if not, and experienced donors at 92% against 96% ([Spekman et al. 2019](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:4a6bb6a6-82c9-48e9-ac44-39207c4717e8/)). In Iran, 51.77% of temporarily deferred volunteers returned over 4.8 years against 74.77% of eligible donors, and the median time to return was 1,467 days against 315 ([Maghsudlu et al. 2024](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:c123aba0-95f5-4d3d-802d-1ed7177944b2/)). In a Brazilian service with no follow-up activity at all, deferral was associated with a 50% lower likelihood of return across more than two million attempts over 26 years ([Ferreira et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:8b546132-e539-4a68-a392-19ed76c4af04/)).

**Evidence (B).** Three further patterns matter for the "not now" option. Longer waits do worse: across six United States blood centres in 2006 to 2008, the share returning after deferrals of up to a year never rose above 50% ([Custer et al. 2011](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:d1429cf8-fa7d-482c-98d1-b0044e5e08c2/)). Repeated deferrals compound: in Austrian data on more than 123,000 donors, each extra deferral strengthened the effect ([Clément et al. 2021](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:443452bd-6bf2-47f2-9ec3-b2bd14362265/); the abstract's conclusion contradicts its own results on which group is hit harder, so check the full text). People who have come back after a gap are fragile: Dutch "reactivated" donors returned at 76% if their visit went ahead and 61% if they were deferred, about the same as newcomers ([Spekman et al. 2019](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:4a6bb6a6-82c9-48e9-ac44-39207c4717e8/)). A softer message does not remove the problem: Canadian women advised, not required, to pause for six months returned at 77.5% over two years against 88.5% of those with normal results, and about a third came back early ([Goldman et al. 2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:cbb5531b-99e6-4ee8-9ba8-505f1d2ba0b0/)).

**Evidence (B/C).** Hurt feelings are not the whole story. In a pilot of 96 first-time donors (country not stated in the abstract, believed Germany), mood fell straight after deferral and 36.4% of deferred against 60.8% of non-deferred donors returned within 12 months, but the drop in mood did not statistically explain who stayed away ([Greffin et al. 2021](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:a03d4b63-8e98-43ce-a662-9b3efb073a7b/)). Hassle and lost momentum probably matter as much.

### What reduced the harm, and what did not

**Evidence (A).** A reminder when the wait is over works, and plain words work better than emotional ones. Australian Red Cross Lifeblood randomised 1,676 deferred donors to a reminder four weeks before, one week before or one week after their deferral ended, or to no contact. Any reminder doubled the odds of attempting to return within three months (odds ratio 2.01), and one week before was best. In a second study of 1,973 donors a plain, factual message beat an emotive one (odds ratio 1.38), and email did as well as text ([Gemelli et al. 2021](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:870c787e-5171-4777-b1e4-9a813c8b8102/)). The abstract does not give the underlying return rates, so no baseline can be reported.

**Evidence (A).** A conversation at the moment of "not now" works modestly; an email alone does not. In a three-arm cluster randomised trial at Lifeblood, a staff-guided conversation plus a brochure plus a follow-up email raised the odds of return within three months of the deferral ending (odds ratio 1.16, 95% confidence interval 1.00 to 1.33), with larger effects for novice donors (1.38) and for people who had been deferred before (1.55; the interval is printed as 1.15 to 1.55, which looks like a typing error). **The email-only arm showed no significant difference from usual practice** ([Gemelli et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:aaebd0fb-79af-420d-9d7d-480f3ee6c24f/)). A Dutch randomised field trial in ten centres found the same for extra information about the deferral, with effect sizes not given in the abstract ([Spekman et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:92bca49f-0c4f-4bdd-aebb-399d7e248bc7/)).

**Evidence (A), a null.** In that same Dutch trial, **offering deferred donors an "alternative good deed" did not increase return** ([Spekman et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:92bca49f-0c4f-4bdd-aebb-399d7e248bc7/)). Suggesting another way to help can be kind, but this trial gives no reason to think it keeps the original offer alive.

**Evidence (A and B).** Two lower-pressure measures also have support. Among 956 deferred novice donors (believed Quebec), those who made their own "if-then" plans for forgetting, fitting it in and transport had a 19% greater chance of returning within four years (p = 0.04) ([Godin et al. 2012](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:cf5a1452-f201-4c19-bf3c-6e14f2419019/)). And when Lifeblood asked eligibility questions at booking in July to December 2020, people were less likely to be deferred on the day (adjusted odds ratio 0.86; 0.79 for new donors), and those still deferred were more likely to return at 12 months (adjusted hazard ratio 1.12) ([Gemelli et al. 2025](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:6108457d-0540-4947-b4f9-82ee6d6cdb42/)). **Evidence (B, weak).** Personal calls at the end of a deferral have only uncontrolled support: two Indian programmes in 2026 report 42.9% and 49.4% of called donors later donating, with no comparison group ([Kaur et al. 2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:b95f71f6-e95e-48c2-8863-9796d07b8573/); [Bansal et al. 2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:379f6c5e-db2b-494f-9176-b9dfcc6a6660/)).

**Evidence (C).** Kidney donor research describes the same harm in people's own words. Australians judged ineligible spoke of a "suspended donor status", changeable criteria, and being "dismissed and discarded by the system" ([Ralph et al. 2019](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:d0c52a25-5500-4b2d-8839-940ab0fef3f1/)).

**Recommendation (OUCM).** Transfer is strong in direction and weak in size. Everyone who enquires about fostering is at the "first visit" stage, where a "not now" does most damage. The trial-supported ingredients are cheap and respectful: a person explains why at the time, a date is agreed, and the service keeps its promise in plain words about a week before. Two things do not transfer cleanly. In blood donation the service imposes the wait; OUCM's "not now" is mainly the person's own choice, and no study in these notes tests a self-chosen pause. And the if-then planning result is only acceptable where the person decides for themselves what would need to be true before they come back; scripted prompts used to push would breach OUCM's purpose.

## Silence predicts lost contact, and one message to an old list achieves little

### Recency of contact is the strongest thing a service controls

**Evidence (B).** On the British Bone Marrow Registry (now the NHS Stem Cell Donor Registry), across 10,258 verification requests in 2013 to 2018, 36% of people were not available when first asked. Among people the registry had been in touch with in the past six months, 20.6% were not available; at six months to two years, 32.4%; after more than two years, 47.4% (odds ratio 3.47, 95% confidence interval 3.13 to 3.86). Recency of contact was the single most influential feature in the authors' model, stronger than time on the register ([Li et al., journal believed to be Biology of Blood and Marrow Transplantation 2020, not yet checked](https://core.ac.uk/download/326512372.pdf)). This is an association: recently contacted people differ in other ways.

**Evidence (B).** Most of the loss is not a change of heart. At Anthony Nolan, of 7,542 confirmatory typing requests reported in 2013, 38.2% were cancelled for donor reasons. Of those, 36% were "no contact made", 34.1% medical, 19.4% personal and 7.9% overseas or emigrated ([Lown et al. 2013, conference abstract](https://bmt.confex.com/tandem/2013/webprogram/Paper2256.html)). On the NHS registry the split was medical 41.1%, ability to contact 29.8%, personal commitment 11.4% and other 17.6% ([Li et al.](https://core.ac.uk/download/326512372.pdf)). Three different things hide inside "dropped out": cannot be reached, circumstances changed, and a considered no.

**Evidence (A, quasi-experimental).** One well-identified study shows that a small, concrete check-in helps a little and sorts honestly. DKMS Germany invited registered people to send a fresh sample, say when they were temporarily unavailable, or both. Across 91,479 later requests, against a baseline availability of 77.1%, being invited raised availability by 2.5 to 3.2 percentage points; among those who took part the rise was 3.8 to 8.2 points. People who responded were more than 10 points more available than those who did not, which the authors read partly as a signal of who is still willing ([Haylock et al. 2022](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29857)). A university press release puts the strongest effect as a 37% relative cut in later unavailability; that figure is secondhand and should be checked against the journal article ([University of Tübingen](https://uni-tuebingen.de/en/university/news-and-publications/press-releases/press-releases/article/better-contact-increases-stem-cell-donors-availability/)). No figures show how many people used the "temporarily unavailable" option or came back after it.

### Re-contacting people after a long gap: mostly small effects and nulls

**Evidence (A), nulls.** A single message or call to an old list does little. In Guangzhou, 11,880 inactive donors were randomised to a phone call, a text or nothing and followed for a year: **there was no significant difference in re-donation between the three groups** by intention to treat, although calls helped among people actually reached ([Ou-Yang et al. 2020](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:dbe3b8e0-71f8-447e-80b4-16d6ac80f0c5/)). In Nigeria, 2,809 lapsed donors were randomised to text, WhatsApp, phone or nothing: 27.7% responded positively, almost all by phone, and **one person donated within 30 days (0.04%)** ([Ogbenna et al. 2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:43ae0c40-dc78-478a-abc7-ee6adb814251/)). Among 1,022 lapsed donors (believed Switzerland), overall return after ten months was 3%, and **adding a "save lives" message made no difference** ([Moussaoui et al. 2016](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:8880c7cb-ccf9-4564-bd04-376d89a106a7/)).

**Evidence (A and C).** What has some support is quieter. Among 7,000 donors lapsed for two years or more (believed Quebec), simply being sent a short questionnaire about their intentions raised registrations to give blood at 6 and 15 months against no contact (p < 0.05; absolute rates not in the abstract) ([Godin et al. 2013](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:54fa8d57-f711-4365-82c5-66b29ba9c324/)). In a German survey of 1,263 people who had not given for more than 36 months, 44.9% had not consciously decided to stop, only 16.9% had, and a quarter of the first group were willing to restart; they asked for flexible hours (29.1%), online booking (24.8%) and reminders (18.1%), and the authors suggest "pause options" ([Saltzmann et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:9c87188d-71c1-4c40-9d25-b00395f0872c/)).

### How much contact is too much: nobody has measured it

**Evidence (A and B).** At the intensities tested, more contact did no measurable harm. In England, the INTERVAL extension randomised 20,757 donors to more intensive or routine reminders and found slightly more blood collected (0.11 units a year in men, 0.06 in women) with no detectable difference in quality of life ([Kaptoge et al. 2019](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:fc8cf664-7a98-47d8-b0b1-338b6ac62fa5/)). In Australia, 36,418 donors invited into research were slightly more likely to donate afterwards than matched controls (relative risk 1.18 at one month) ([Kruse et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:4a65c47d-fcc6-4c61-a3ee-a35129ad629f/)). **But no study found measures opt-outs, complaints or harm from frequent contact**, and none tests how often to keep in touch during a long assessment.

**Evidence (C) and Observed practice (D).** What kidney donor research does show is that silence hurts. In BOUnD interviews with 35 people who offered a kidney to a stranger, those who did not go on reported dissatisfaction and lasting emotional effects "linked to lack of follow-up from transplant teams" ([Zuchowski et al. 2021](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:3e587fff-8088-42a1-b4ec-c1b2b4142f6e/)). Among young United States stem cell registrants, having spoken with registry staff only once or twice was independently associated with opting out ([Hamed et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36455856/)). UK kidney guidance sets a week-by-week pathway ending at week 18 ([BTS/Renal Association 2018](https://bts.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/BTS_LDKT_UK_Guidelines_2018.pdf)), yet people who donated in the 2014 to 2016 UK cohort spent a median of 308 days, about 44 weeks, in assessment ([Bailey et al. 2017](https://core.ac.uk/download/96781141.pdf)), and a 2025 survey in which most centres said they offered 18 weeks was self-reported and internally inconsistent ([Nightingale et al. 2025](https://www.frontierspartnerships.org/articles/10.3389/ti.2025.15341/full)). In Canada and Australia a donor's wait was often driven by someone else's process: each month's delay in the recipient's referral added 0.9 months ([Habbous et al. 2018](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:f29c658c-1913-4999-b563-4a154289999a/)).

**Recommendation (OUCM).** Transfer is high for the principle and nil for the number. The evidence supports keeping in touch, telling people when a wait is not about them, and measuring elapsed time from the person's side instead of trusting a service's description of itself. It does not support "every two weeks" over every three or four. The claim that delay causes people to withdraw is plausible and widely asserted but untested; say "associated with".

## The largest loss comes straight after the first form

**Evidence (B).** Where counting starts decides the headline. From a first form, 6% to 8% of people at two United States centres went on to donate a kidney; from the start of formal assessment, 15% to 20% in UK and United States multi-centre cohorts; from research consent or a screened assessment day, 45% (BOUnD) to 66% (Belfast, secondhand through a journal blog). At the Cleveland Clinic, of 3,001 first forms in 2016 to 2019, the largest single loss came immediately after the form, mainly because the person did not respond to the follow-up phone call (22.6%) ([Cholin et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:6baa6682-0f01-4c80-a62d-9a86cd416f39/)). At a rural United States centre, 63.0% of 1,776 self-referrals left after the first form, chiefly lost to follow-up (32.1%) or having changed their mind (24.6%) ([Dobbs et al. 2025](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:9deac9be-197b-4f43-a400-cc78003dcd79/)). In the UK directed-donor cohort, 15.2% donated and 15.0% withdrew, and those who withdrew did so early, after a median of 61 days ([Bailey et al. 2017](https://core.ac.uk/download/96781141.pdf)).

**Observed practice (D, press release).** NHS Blood and Transplant said in June 2025 that of people who had registered to give blood in England, "only 1 in 4 (24 per cent)" had gone on to donate ([NHSBT, 9 June 2025](https://www.blood.co.uk/news-and-campaigns/news-and-statements/nhs-in-england-calls-for-one-million-people-to-donate-blood-to-secure-the-nation-s-blood-supply/)). Even for a one-hour act with a national brand, most people who put their name down do not take the next step.

**Evidence (A).** Personal contact after a first step beats silence, by about ten percentage points in the better blood trials. In a randomised trial of 418 first-time donors, a call 9 to 14 weeks later that began by asking how the first experience had been raised six-month return from 54.3% to 65.0% ([Marsano et al. 2024](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:71db5d8f-ca7e-425b-b1d5-0a770bcd524f/)). A Quebec quasi-experiment found 48.3% against 38.0% within 12 months ([Godin et al. 2011](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:9510567e-8d76-43e8-806d-d165689318c3/)). A pooled analysis of seven trials gives a relative gain of 25% for any active contact, with **educational messages inconclusive**; it is a conference abstract and provisional ([Silva et al. 2025](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:acba8814-c3f7-4354-a296-a4f047806019/)). A person on the phone did as well as or better than text or email. **Evidence (A), nulls.** A T-shirt made no difference (20.5% against 20.6%), and in Iran a "motivational meeting" did no better than no contact (22% against 22.1%) ([Reich et al. 2006](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:d805065f-7ff6-4961-a1df-d366e0de4cba/); [Hashemi et al. 2019](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:2d722a8e-f4d6-4d53-a10f-d47ab8aaf066/)). Automated web versions of motivational interviewing showed no behavioural advantage ([Jensen et al. 2020](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:685b78f1-4ae1-4815-b961-65061d1bf1fb/)).

**Evidence (A), a weaker result than its reputation.** The celebrated "your blood was used" text has one positive non-randomised Australian study (70.3% against 62.6% returning within 12 months) ([Gemelli et al. 2017](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:90cf5776-f065-4136-9c3d-f7fa57112ad4/)) and one randomised trial of 20,365 donors (believed Denmark) that found **no significant effect among whole-blood donors** ([Fosgaard et al. 2020](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:dadee526-1695-4c3a-832f-47e08a2b74a4/)). The Swedish claim rests on press reporting and NHS Blood and Transplant has published no evaluation of its own scheme ([NHSBT, 14 June 2016](https://www.blood.co.uk/news-and-campaigns/news-and-statements/blood-donors-texted-when-their-blood-goes-to-hospitals/)). It should not be cited as proven.

**Recommendation (OUCM).** Transfer is high for the shape: the gap between a form and a first conversation is where people silently disappear, and "we could not reach this person" should be recorded as its own outcome, never folded into "withdrew". Nothing in these notes tests how fast the first reply must be. The five-minute and one-working-day promises rest on respect and common sense, and OUCM should say so.

## Being turned away well has five parts, none of them medical

**Evidence (C).** People turned down as kidney donors commonly experience it as a personal failure, and what made it worse was the handling, not the decision. Interviews with 27 Australians described "devastating loss and disappointment", being "ill prepared for rejection", and "having the donor role stolen" ([Ralph et al. 2019](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:d0c52a25-5500-4b2d-8839-940ab0fef3f1/)). In a United States survey of declined candidates (64 replies from 193, a 33.2% response), 50% felt disappointed and 34% sad, 72% felt proud to have considered donating, **none regretted starting**, and 23% wanted a follow-up call after the decision ([Calkins et al. 2025](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:49aba195-5d11-4572-aada-80ee0cffd119/)).

**Evidence (B/C).** Standard questionnaires miss this. At the University of Pennsylvania, 43 people turned down in 2010 to 2013 had similar quality-of-life, depression and financial stress scores to 128 donors, yet **32% said their life was worse after the centre's decision against 7% of donors** (p < 0.01) ([Reese et al. 2018](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:67aa9b04-5370-437e-bd67-101d2337d9f3/)). One direct question found what the scales did not.

**Observed practice (D).** UK guidance already states what is owed: a policy must be in place for people found unsuitable; they "must be identified at the earliest possible stage"; a second opinion "must always be available"; a follow-up appointment is arranged to discuss results; and the need for emotional support "must be anticipated" ([BTS/Renal Association 2018](https://bts.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/BTS_LDKT_UK_Guidelines_2018.pdf)). BOUnD names support for people who do not go ahead as a priority and recommends preparing people from the very beginning "for all possible outcomes" ([Zuchowski et al. 2021](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:3e587fff-8088-42a1-b4ec-c1b2b4142f6e/); [Mamode et al. 2025](https://library.kab.ac.ug/Record/doaj-art-212fefbb094541b79514cbbc3d908c3e/Description)). **Evidence (B).** Being asked and then not needed is routine: 5.5% of Anthony Nolan requests were cancelled for transplant centre reasons ([Lown et al. 2013](https://bmt.confex.com/tandem/2013/webprogram/Paper2256.html)). No registry study found measures how that feels or whether those people are available next time.

**Recommendation (OUCM).** Transfer is high. Put together, the evidence describes five parts of a decent "no": say at the start how often people do not go through and that it is not a judgement of their worth; give the decision by voice, with time; give the real reason and what it means for the person; offer a route to a review; and get in touch again afterwards, because the reaction comes later. No trial has tested any of the five. Naming other real ways to help answers the loss of identity that people describe, but the Dutch null result means it should be offered as a kindness, not counted on to keep the fostering offer alive, and never used as a brush-off.

## A named guide is established practice with no trial behind it

**Observed practice (D).** Transplant services have ended up with three roles around the volunteer. A coordinator organises and keeps in touch: UK guidance says most centres have designated staff who are a main source of support ([BTS/Renal Association 2018](https://bts.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/BTS_LDKT_UK_Guidelines_2018.pdf)). An independent person protects the volunteer's interests: in the UK every donor is interviewed separately by an Independent Assessor accredited by the Human Tissue Authority, and United States rules require an independent living donor advocate who "protects the donor's right to withdraw" ([Hays et al. 2015](https://www.notifylibrary.org/sites/default/files/GUIDANCE%20DOC%20AST_The%20independent%20living%20donor%20advocate_a%20guidance%20document%20from%20the%20AST%E2%80%99s%20LDCOP.pdf)). And a peer who has done it before offers support, a role Ontario donors asked for ([Getchell et al. 2017](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:8a500c9d-09de-4aa4-acd3-8239dd6634be/)).

**Evidence (C and B).** Volunteers say the quality of contact shapes everything: for 18 Danish potential donors, experience "largely depended on the quality of their communication and interaction" with staff ([Agerskov et al. 2015](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:a6b5a60b-e2f0-424a-87d4-884aafc62959/)). In BOUnD, withdrawal was less likely in high-volume, well-staffed centres ([Mamode et al. 2025](https://library.kab.ac.ug/Record/doaj-art-212fefbb094541b79514cbbc3d908c3e/Description)).

**Evidence (B and A), cautions.** Naming a role does not deliver it. A national survey of 120 United States advocates found wide variation in background, training and practice; only 18% thought "not being part of the donor team" necessary for independence and 53% attended donor selection meetings (figures quoted secondhand through Hays et al.) ([Steel et al. 2012](https://scholars.mssm.edu/en/publications/a-national-survey-of-independent-living-donor-advocates-the-need--2/)). The large effects reported for navigator and champion programmes (hazard ratios of 5.8 to 9.3) measure patients finding donors among self-selected participants and must not be quoted as the effect of a guide for the volunteer ([Locke et al. 2019](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:7ec63a9c-969b-4b87-9709-70de6cbdae85/); [King et al. 2020](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:f26b480d-eaf8-4756-871c-08629db782d3/)). The one randomised test of adding a peer mentor, among 319 Black patients in 2018 to 2020, found **no significant difference in transplants within a year** (0% usual care, 5% house calls, 6% house calls plus mentor; p = 0.07) and no clear added benefit from the mentor ([Rodrigue et al. 2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:662d2c1a-bc32-4c0a-84ea-11292a3a0e46/)). No controlled study of a coordinator, a named nurse or an advocate measured from the volunteer's side was found.

**Recommendation (OUCM).** Transfer is high for the named contact and the peer, and moderate for the independent role. Keep the named journey guide, be honest that it rests on guidance and interviews, and define it by duties instead of a job title: what the guide must do, by when, and what happens when they are away. The independent role raises a harder point. An agency that needs carers has a structural interest similar to a transplant team that needs a kidney, and transplant practice answers that with someone whose only loyalty is to the volunteer. OUCM should at least require that the guide's stated job is to support the person's decision either way. Whether anything independent exists for people at the enquiry stage in England should be checked against Department for Education and Ofsted sources before it is asserted.

## Ambivalence is normal, predictive, and not to be scored

**Evidence (B).** In interviews with 1,067 United States stem cell registrants contacted about a possible match, ambivalence was the strongest predictor of opting out in every ethnic group, with adjusted odds ratios of 14.1 to 22.2 in three groups and 94.6 among Hispanic registrants, a figure too imprecise to quote as exact ([Switzer et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3578960)). A 2023 study of 935 registrants aged 18 to 30 found the same, and its model classified 86.6% of people correctly ([Hamed et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36455856/)). Reported secondhand through the registry's summary, joining "because it was easy" was associated with later opting out, while having medical concerns was associated with continuing ([NMDP summary](https://network.nmdp.org/research/research-publications/factors-associated-with-young-adults-opting-out-of-donation-for-hct)). One reading is that voiced worries get answered and silent doubt does not; the paper does not test that.

**Evidence (B, secondhand).** Pressure appears to backfire. A 2025 systematic review reports, from earlier work it cites, that feeling pressured when contacted about a match, less contact with staff and staff not explaining the process were each associated with dropping out, while receiving information was associated with staying ([Forbes et al. 2025](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/182833/1/British%20J%20Health%20Psychol%20-%202024%20-%20Forbes%20-%20Factors%20influencing%20unrelated%20stem%20cell%20donation%20a%20mixed%E2%80%90methods%20integrated.pdf)).

**Observed practice (D).** The researchers who found the ambivalence effect say what to do with it: address the specific worries, offer a "cooling-off" period after which the person gets back in touch themselves, and use brief motivational interviewing aimed at comfort with the decision, "not at encouraging donation" ([Switzer et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3578960)). None of these has been evaluated. **Evidence (A), mixed.** Motivational interviewing in blood donation has one positive small trial (odds ratio 2.48 among 215 donors), one trial of 484 with no overall behavioural effect, and no benefit from automated versions; a 2026 systematic review of 36 studies calls the evidence "conflicting" ([Sinclair et al. 2010](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:58227acd-06e0-436b-b1b0-e4148f7e913b/); [France et al. 2017](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:c7992c4b-c4eb-4f7c-8941-a9776c0a67a1/); [Boult et al. 2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:79c5d7a6-db05-4926-9df1-93a7d8ead50e/)).

**Observed practice (D).** The clearest ethical model is consent renewed at each stage. The World Marrow Donor Association standards say adult donation "must be voluntary" and "a donor must be free to withdraw at any time" ([WMDA 2024](https://wmda.info/storage/2024/01/2024-WMDA-Stnds_20240101-final.pdf)). UK kidney guidance says a person may withdraw "without having to provide an explanation", that this "should not be regarded as a failure", and that the assessment team's "primary responsibility" is to support the donor in deciding ([BTS/Renal Association 2018](https://bts.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/BTS_LDKT_UK_Guidelines_2018.pdf)).

**Evidence (B) and Observed practice (D).** Suspicion of an unusual offer is not supported. BOUnD found no difference between people donating to a stranger and people donating to someone they know in outcomes, regret or withdrawal (hazard ratio 1.12, 95% confidence interval 0.75 to 1.67), although the stranger-donors reported fewer positive perceptions of the experience (79.9% against 86.2%) ([Mamode et al. 2025](https://library.kab.ac.ug/Record/doaj-art-212fefbb094541b79514cbbc3d908c3e/Description)). In Israel, 23.4% of 598 unrelated candidates were disqualified in 2008 to 2016, 41% of them on mental health grounds using criteria such as "ego strength" ([Ashkenazi et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:d6fa5395-0d16-4d4c-a0b6-daa6319b4ca2/)): a picture of where vague motive-testing ends up.

**Recommendation (OUCM).** Transfer is high as a principle and forbidden as a tool. Doubt at enquiry is probably wiser than certainty for a decision as large as fostering. Services should make room for people to voice it, answer worries straight, and say at the start that stopping is always allowed and is not a failure. The so-called "medical alibi" in kidney donation, where a team gives a general reason so a reluctant person can step back without blame, is summarised in the notes from background knowledge and unread sources, so treat it as not yet checked. Its honest form is worth testing: one truthful, non-specific form of words ("this is not going ahead at the moment"), agreed with the person and used for everyone, with nothing invented and any safeguarding concern recorded truthfully.

## Equity: the gaps open where the system loses touch or asks without explaining

**Evidence (B).** On the NHS stem cell registry, people from most minority ethnic groups were roughly two to four times as likely as White British registrants to be unavailable when asked (for example Black African odds ratio 2.95, Black Caribbean 2.30, Asian Pakistani 2.15), and the authors say the gap was mainly due to inability to contact them; people with no recorded ethnicity were also far less available (2.41) ([Li et al.](https://core.ac.uk/download/326512372.pdf)). Subgroup percentages in that paper's table do not add up, so rely on the odds ratios. Anthony Nolan data show the same pattern ([Lown et al. 2013](https://bmt.confex.com/tandem/2013/webprogram/Paper2256.html)). This points at stale records and thin relationships more than at attitudes.

**Evidence (B).** In the UK, a study of 981 donors and non-donors found trust lower in every domain among ethnic minority respondents; for ethnic minority non-donors, trust in *individuals* predicted willingness, while for White non-donors it was trust in the blood service ([Ferguson et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:476ad961-85fa-4083-bd5c-5b4dfda777a0/)). Eligibility questions fell unevenly: 8% to 9.3% of ethnic minority donors were deferred on travel grounds in 2015 to 2019 against 1.7% of White donors, and questions asked without explanation produced discouraging word of mouth through perceived discrimination ([Ferguson et al. 2024](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:3a211c85-e708-4276-a1fb-a1b30aa6b795/)). Because deferral sharply reduces return, the two effects compound.

**Evidence (B).** Looking step by step shows where gaps open. At one United States kidney centre, African American candidates were about half as likely to get from referral to donation (adjusted hazard ratio 0.47), with the gap at two steps that require time off, travel and household support, and none at first referral or at the approval decision ([Kumar et al. 2018](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:6ebffcd5-0a6b-4932-9ae6-7214a035ed5b/)). In the UK cohort, non-white ethnicity was associated with withdrawal (odds ratio 2.98, 95% confidence interval 1.05 to 8.44) on very few people in a cohort that was 92.8% white, and people from the most deprived fifth of areas were about half as likely to donate (odds ratio 0.49, p = 0.05), with the trend across all groups consistent with chance ([Bailey et al. 2017](https://core.ac.uk/download/96781141.pdf)).

**Evidence (B) and Observed practice (D).** Cost is a real barrier. A systematic review found about 80% of donors had a financial loss, with lost income the largest share ([Fu et al. 2020](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:4d1200df-6f23-4d75-a737-bf32a5743bdb/)). NHS England's policy is that there should be "no financial incentive or disincentive", but people assessed and found unsuitable can claim travel and parking only ([NHS England 2021](https://www.england.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2017/08/Reimbursement-of-Expenses-for-Living-Donors-A06PA-Dec-2021.pdf)).

**Evidence (A), a possible backfire.** More information is not always the answer. In a United States trial of a patient education programme (a preprint, not peer reviewed), there was no overall effect, and in exploratory analysis the programme was associated with *lower* completion among people who had experienced discrimination in health care (hazard ratio 0.56) while helping those who had not (1.32) ([Velez-Bermudez et al. 2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:10d4f5ad-9556-4cf6-b27a-205616094e50/)). **Evidence (A/B, weak).** A 2019 review found only eight intervention studies on blood donation in minority ethnic communities, all rated low quality ([Makin et al. 2019](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:c98531c7-608f-4d5a-a55b-660716cc8b8f/)). No evaluation found shows any registry change closing the availability gap.

**Recommendation (OUCM).** Transfer is high as a method. Before anyone concludes that a group is "less committed", check whether the service lost touch with them or never answered their questions. The standard should let a service see, in aggregate, the step at which different groups of people leave, and should require that every early question comes with a stated reason or is dropped from first contact. Likely fostering equivalents (questions on housing tenure, immigration status or time in the UK; old rules people believe still apply) are this report's assumptions and need checking against current practice. Trust runs through people, so who replies matters. The standard should also ask services to say at enquiry who bears the costs of assessment and what happens to them if the person is not approved. All of this must use aggregate figures and synthetic examples only, and must never rank an individual.

## What OUCM should not adopt

**Recommendation (OUCM).** Several things that appear in this literature are ruled out by OUCM's purpose, and most of them also failed when tested.

| Do not adopt | Why |
|---|---|
| Any ambivalence scale, "risk factor" count or availability prediction used to score, rank, filter or prioritise people who enquire | A firm project rule. The registry literature proposes exactly this ([Li et al.](https://core.ac.uk/download/326512372.pdf) suggest a prediction score to choose between donors). Ambivalence may only prompt a conversation the person controls. Treating a reply to a check-in as a "signal" of commitment, as the DKMS authors do, is also out |
| Urgency, guilt or loss framing ("a child is waiting", "would you want someone there for a child in your family?") | A loss-framed text worked in a 14-day blood emergency in China ([Ou-Yang et al. 2021](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:c59d0af8-b26c-40b0-9861-42a9088f3218/)); emotive reminders did worse than plain ones and "save lives" added nothing. Reciprocity framing edges towards guilt, and its authors estimate real behaviour at about a tenth of stated willingness ([Ferguson et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:9a9a5fa0-1185-4f1c-a0e6-9ae53b216763/)) |
| Gifts, rewards or incentives for enquiring or continuing | A T-shirt did nothing; gift cards shifted timing with no lasting effect, and a surprise reward was followed by *less* donation ([Lacetera, Macis and Slonim 2014](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:35dfe955-1861-41d1-856c-faa2e5a3f7f3/)); a small cash payment almost halved women's willingness in one Swedish experiment ([Mellström and Johannesson 2008](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:cafe42a9-2924-4249-a986-86a5fa377deb/)); a review of 71 papers found no incentive that is liked, effective and safe ([Chell et al. 2018](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:b620173f-b09a-46e5-be6f-d798f42bf95b/)). None of this settles anything about fostering allowances, which carers need to do the job |
| Asking for an explicit commitment or pledge early | It raised first attendance by 11.5 points in one blood trial ([Wevers et al. 2014](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:e375dd97-2e85-452d-8909-c54f96b2fcdf/)), which is persuasion, not information |
| Home visits or family sessions designed to increase offers | The kidney trials aim to raise the number of people who offer; UK families named coercion and family pressure as risks ([Bailey et al. 2021](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:2f7ca797-c12a-4645-84fa-e6192bfd6e18/)) |
| A check-in where "yes, still interested" is the easy answer | "Pause" and "remove me" must be offered as equal options, in the same place and with the same effort, or the check-in becomes a nudge |
| Invented reasons to help someone step back | An honest general statement only; never a false record |
| Counting a friendly reply as a decision | In Nigeria 27.7% responded warmly and one person donated ([Ogbenna et al. 2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:43ae0c40-dc78-478a-abc7-ee6adb814251/)) |
| Extra motive-testing for people whose offer seems unusual | Not supported by BOUnD; needs a stated reason, a consistent method and an explanation to the person |

## Proposed changes to the OUCM standard

Every row is a **Recommendation (OUCM)**. "Basis" names the strongest evidence and its grade; where the basis is principle alone, it says so.

| Element | Verdict | Proposed wording or change | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acknowledge within 5 minutes | Confirm, and label honestly | Keep. State in the standard that this is a courtesy promise with no trial behind it. The acknowledgement should say what happens next, when, and that enquiring commits the person to nothing | Principle. Nearest evidence: silent loss after the first form (B) |
| A person makes first contact within 1 working day | Tighten | Keep the promise. Add: more than one attempt, at different times and by the channel the person chose; record "not yet reached" as its own outcome; the first conversation starts by listening | Largest loss is the unreached follow-up call (B); phone at least as good as text or email (A); listening call +10.7 points (A, blood). The one-day figure is untested |
| "Not now" with a chosen date of 3, 6 or 12 months | Tighten | The person picks any date, with 3, 6 and 12 months as suggestions. The pause is agreed in conversation where possible, not by email alone. The service makes contact about a week before the date, in plain factual words. That message offers three equal options: talk now, pause again, remove me. Record how many times a person has paused or been asked to wait. Treat a returning person as a priority | Reminder a week before, plain tone (A); conversation works, email alone does not (A); longer waits and repeated deferrals do worse (B); returners are fragile (B); many lapses are unintended (C). ## Check before public use

- **Abstract only.** Nearly all blood donation figures, and many kidney figures, come from abstracts. Read the full text before quoting any of them publicly.
- **"Believed" countries.** Greffin 2021 (Germany), Godin 2012 and 2013 and Germain 2015 (Quebec), Fosgaard 2020 (Denmark), Moussaoui 2016 (Switzerland), Wevers 2014 (Netherlands) are inferred from author affiliations. Marsano 2024 does not state a country.
- **Internally inconsistent.** Clément 2021 (conclusion contradicts results); Gemelli 2022 (interval printed as 1.15 to 1.55 around 1.55); Li et al. (ethnicity subgroup percentages do not add up; journal and year not yet checked); Nightingale 2025 (76.2% of centres offering 18 weeks against "only 50%" elsewhere in the paper; self-reported); Haylock 2022 (working paper says 91,479 requests and 77.1%, press release says 91,670 and "75%"); Cholin 2022 (188 donors from 3,001 forms is 6.3% by this report's arithmetic, but the paper reports 7.5%, so the denominator needs checking).
- **Press releases and secondhand.** The NHSBT "1 in 4" figure (June 2025) and its 2016 text-message announcement; the Tübingen 37% figure; the Swedish text-message claim (press reporting only, no controlled evaluation found); the NMDP summary of Hamed 2023; the findings attributed to Switzer et al. 2004 (through Forbes 2025 only); Steel 2012 figures (through Hays 2015); the Belfast one-day model (through a journal blog; it is the source of the 66% upper figure for people going on to donate after a screened assessment day).
- **Conference abstracts and preprints.** Lown 2013; Silva 2025; Velez-Bermudez 2026 (not peer reviewed).
- **From background knowledge, not yet checked.** The "medical alibi" debate; the exact wording of the KDIGO 2017 guideline and United States policy on withdrawal and statements of unsuitability.
- **Partly read documents.** The UK kidney guideline was read from the December 2017 consultation text; check quotations against the final 2018 edition. The statement that the WMDA standards contain nothing on keeping in touch or on pressure comes from an extracted summary; this report does not rely on it, and it should be checked before being said publicly.
- **Fostering parallels.** Every statement here about how fostering services in England work is an assumption. Check against Department for Education and Ofsted definitions and sources before publishing.
- **Two studies, not one.** Bailey et al. 2017 (directed donors, seven UK centres) and BOUnD (Mamode and colleagues, unspecified donors, 23 UK units).

## Gaps

- No fostering evidence is in these notes. Every transfer judgement is an inference.
- No study tests how fast a first reply must be, or how often to keep in touch during a long assessment.
- No study measures opt-outs, complaints or harm from frequent contact.
- No randomised trial of a personal call at the end of a waiting period; no test of a stated date without a reminder; no test of a pause the person chose themselves.
- No trial of a coordinator, advocate or named guide measured from the volunteer's side; no UK evaluation of peer buddy schemes for potential donors.
- No trial of any way of giving a refusal or of follow-up afterwards; almost nothing on people who withdrew of their own accord.
- No registry study of people stood down after being asked.
- No evaluation of a cooling-off period or of any work on ambivalence in registries.
- No study relating the length of gaps in contact to withdrawal.
- No England figures for return after deferral, for first-time donors giving again, or for how many people come forward as potential living donors.
- No evaluation showing any measure that closed an ethnic gap in availability or progression.
- No evidence on whether telling people at the outset that they may stop without explanation changes what they do or how they feel.

## Questions OUCM could test openly

**Recommendation (OUCM).** Each of these can be tested with services that volunteer, with the method published first, synthetic examples in anything public, and outcomes that measure understanding and respect before anything else.

- Do people who are told at first contact that they can stop at any time, without explanation, report feeling better informed and less pressured?
- Does a plain reminder a week before a self-chosen "not now" date, offering talk, pause and remove as equal options, leave people feeling respected? How many choose each option?
- Does an agreed contact rhythm work better for people than a fixed two-week rule, judged by one direct question about how the process left them?
- When a service says "not now" or "no" by voice with a reason and a later call, compared with a letter, do people understand why and know what happens next?
- Does separating "not reached", "paused", "person's decision" and "service's decision" change what a service learns about where people leave?
- Does stating a reason beside each question at first contact change who completes the form, in aggregate?
- Would people who enquire want access to someone independent of the service, and at what point?
- Does offering other real ways to help after a "no" feel like respect or like a brush-off?

## Conclusion

The most useful thing these fields offer OUCM is a correction of emphasis. The draft standard puts its precision into speeds and intervals (five minutes, one day, two weeks), which is where the evidence is silent. The evidence is loudest about moments the draft treats lightly: the conversation in which someone is asked to wait, the promise to call back on a date, the way a "no" is given, and the year-long silence in which a willing person's details quietly go out of date. A standard that is exact about those moments and openly modest about its clock times would be both better evidenced and more honest.

The second lesson is that persuasion is not being sacrificed for principle. Where urgency, gifts, emotive wording and substitute good deeds were tested against plain information and a kept promise, the plain version usually did as well or better. OUCM is in a position these fields have not reached: it can test the open questions above with respect and understanding as the outcomes, and publish the nulls as prominently as the rest.
