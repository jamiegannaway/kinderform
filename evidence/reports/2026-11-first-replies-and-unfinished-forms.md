# First replies and unfinished forms: what the evidence says

> **Status: first draft.** Desk review, 26 November 2026. It was made with an AI-assisted search of peer-reviewed papers and the web. See [how we made this](https://www.kinderform.org/about#how). None of the figures has been checked by a person yet, and several were read secondhand (see [Gaps and limits](#6-gaps-and-limits)). No study here is about fostering, so every view on what carries over is our own. Everything we propose is for discussion. It is not yet part of the Kinderform standard, the open standard that the project behind kinderform.org is drafting. The [research log](../research-log.json) says what each review could mean for the project.

Grades:

- **A:** randomised or strong quasi-experimental
- **B:** controlled comparison, review of comparisons, or robust official statistics
- **C:** descriptive, survey or qualitative
- **D:** guidance, anecdote, a single site, or read secondhand

## In short

1. **Invite, don't lecture.** In UK mediation calls, people turned the service down more often when it was explained in abstract terms. They agreed more often when asked if they would be "willing" to take the next step (grade C). A first reply about fostering should lead with the person, their offer and the next step.
2. **Don't leave the work to the person.** Callers to three English GP surgeries sometimes had to push for a next step. It happened most at the surgery with the worst satisfaction scores (grade C). A first reply should end with what happens next, who does it and when.
3. **One word can matter, but test it locally.** In a US trial, "Is there something else...?" drew out more unmet concerns than "Is there anything else...?" (grade A). A UK study found no difference.
4. **Progress bars are not a fix.** Across 32 experiments, a steady progress bar did not reliably stop people giving up a survey. Bars that look slow at the start made it worse (grade A).
5. **Be honest about time.** People are less likely to start a form they are told is long (grade A). Once they have started, honest estimates help them finish.
6. **Help beats information.** Hands-on help to finish an application raised college enrolment by 8.1 percentage points. Information alone did nothing (grade A). Reminders to people who left a form unfinished added 1 to 3 points.
7. **Show the real work.** In experiments, showing people the work done for them raised trust (grades A and B). Showing a service that did nothing gained nothing.

We found no study of first calls or first replies to people who enquire about fostering. We also found no published figures for fostering enquiry forms started against those sent.

## 1. How first calls and first replies are worded

Conversation analysis studies recordings of real talk, turn by turn. It links small choices of words to whether callers take up a service, or feel they have to push for it. Most of this evidence comes from one UK research programme and describes what happens (grade C). In these settings the caller has a problem. Someone who enquires about fostering is making an offer. So the findings carry over as principles, not scripts.

| Finding | Source | Grade | What carries over to fostering |
|---|---|---|---|
| About 200 calls to 5 UK community mediation services. Callers often did not know what mediation is. They turned it down more when it was described as an "impartial" service. Call-takers who described the steps in concrete terms, with empathy, did better. | Stokoe, *Negotiation Journal*, 2013 (UK) | C | Medium. People who enquire about fostering often lack a clear picture too. But mediation callers usually want something else, such as a neighbour punished. People who enquire about fostering are making an offer. |
| Asking whether a caller would be "willing" to mediate drew more agreement than other ways of inviting them. This held even after earlier resistance. | Sikveland and Stokoe, *Conflict Resolution Quarterly*, 2016 (UK); restated in Stokoe, *LSE Public Policy Review*, 2026 | C | Medium. A yes or no invitation to an information session or visit, framed as the person's choice, may carry over. The word itself is untested in fostering. Overusing it could sound scripted. |
| Callers rarely ask outright. When one fell silent, the call-taker offered an easy way out. The caller then decided mediation would not work. | Stokoe, *LSE Public Policy Review*, 2026 (UK) | C | Medium. Staff may mean well by listing early reasons someone might not be suitable. Be honest, but do not offer reasons to stop before they come up. |
| The Ministry of Justice redesigned its 2014 family mediation video and leaflets as a result of this research. Officials reported a good return through reach. | Stokoe, *LSE Public Policy Review*, 2026, quoting officials | D | Low to medium. Findings like these can be turned into written materials. Reach is not uptake, so do not cite this as evidence of effect. |
| Callers work to show their problem fits the service, for example by saying what they have already tried. | Edwards and Stokoe, *Research on Language and Social Interaction*, 2007 (UK) | C | Medium. People who enquire may work to show they are "suitable". Welcoming the offer before any question may help them feel less judged. This is untested. |
| In 447 calls to 3 English GP surgeries, callers had to push for a next step in two places. One was when no alternative was offered. The other was when the call ended without a summary of next steps. It happened in 15 of 150, 28 of 149 and 46 of 148 calls. More of it went with lower patient satisfaction. | Stokoe, Sikveland and Symonds, *British Journal of General Practice*, 2016; Sikveland, Stokoe and Symonds, *Patient Education and Counseling*, 2016 (England) | C | High. Both moments happen in fostering first calls. Offering another way and summarising the next step are low risk. |
| In police crisis negotiations, people rejected proposals framed as "talk" more often than "speak". Some well-placed challenges helped more than rapport alone. | Sikveland and Stokoe, *Research on Language and Social Interaction*, 2020; Sikveland, Kevoe-Feldman and Stokoe, *Applied Linguistics*, 2020 (UK) | C | Low. The stakes are very different. Only the general point carries over: specific actions matter more than general rapport. |
| Every Metropolitan Police negotiator was trained from this research in 2017. The aim was to describe what experts already do, not to prescribe. | Albury, McCabe, Patel and Stokoe, *Patient Education and Counseling*, 2025 (UK) | D | Medium, as a method. Build fostering guidance from what experienced staff already do in real calls. |
| A training method plays real recordings one turn at a time. It was tested in a small trial with student teachers. | Sikveland, Moser, Solem and Skovholt, *Teaching and Teacher Education*, 2023 (Norway) | A design, but a feasibility trial; read from the title and listing only | Medium. A possible training format. It needs consented recordings of real calls, which raises consent and privacy questions. |
| Asking "Is there something else you want to address today?" cut unmet concerns by 78% compared with not asking. "Anything else" made no difference. Neither made visits longer. | Heritage, Robinson, Elliott, Beckett and Wilkes, *Journal of General Internal Medicine*, 2007 (US) | A | Medium. It fits the end of a first call. But see the next row. |
| A small UK study found "any" and "some" made no difference to concerns raised, visit length or satisfaction. | Feasibility study on GPs drawing out patient concerns, *Patient Education and Counseling*, 2018 (UK) | B | Medium. Wording effects may not travel between countries and settings. |
| An Australian children's helpline answers with its name, not "how can I help?", because its role is to listen. Callers then give both a problem and a reason for calling. | Danby, Baker and Emmison, 2005; Emmison and Danby, *Research on Language and Social Interaction*, 2007 (Australia) | C | Low to medium. An opening can match the service's purpose. A fostering service could open with "we're glad you got in touch". |
| A review of 37 studies of recorded helpline calls describes common practice in openings, advice and closings. Some call-takers give callers space: "take your time". | Review of conversation analysis and health helplines, 2019 (UCL preprint); Hepburn and Potter, 2007 (UK) | C, read in part | Medium. "Take your time" may suit a first call where people describe their family. |

Written first replies are rarely studied in any service. | This review | none | The closest sources remain the adoption first-call research in the library: Wilson, Katz and Geen (2005), and Chanmugam and others (2016). |

### What carries over, and what needs care

Likely to carry over:

- End every first call or reply with a summary of the next step: what happens, who does it and by when.
- If the person's request cannot be met, offer another way straight away. For example, offer a call if they cannot come to the next information evening.
- Invite the next step with a plain yes or no question about the person's own choice.
- Describe the process in concrete terms: what happens, when, and who they will meet.
- Near the end, ask "Is there something else you'd like to ask?". The UK evidence on this wording is mixed.

Needs care:

- **Motivation differs.** Mediation and GP callers want something for themselves. People who enquire about fostering are offering something.
- **Services must assess suitability.** Not offering an early way out must never mean hiding honest facts about the assessment.
- **Specific words** such as "willing", "speak" and "some" come from other settings. Rules built on them risk sounding scripted.
- **Crisis and police evidence** comes from very different stakes. Only its general point carries over.

## 2. People who start a form and do not finish it

Most of this evidence comes from survey research, where giving up part-way is called "breakoff". Someone offering to foster may have stronger reasons to finish than someone answering a survey.

| Finding | Source | Grade | What carries over to fostering |
|---|---|---|---|
| A review of 32 experiments: a progress bar moving at a steady speed did not reliably reduce people giving up. Bars that moved fast first helped. Bars that moved slowly first made it worse. With a small promised reward, a steady bar made it worse. | Villar, Callegaro and Yang, *Social Science Computer Review*, 2013 | A | High. An enquiry form is short, so a bar is unlikely to help. A bar that runs fast first misstates progress, so we rule it out. |
| In two experiments, 21.8% gave up when early progress looked slow and 11.3% when it looked fast. 14.4% gave up with a steady bar and 12.7% with no bar. | Conrad, Couper, Tourangeau and Peytchev, *Interacting with Computers*, 2010 (US) | A | High. The first pages matter most, so they should be quick and easy. |
| Progress bars helped when the real time matched the time promised in the invitation. | Yan, Conrad, Tourangeau and Couper, *International Journal of Public Opinion Research*, 2011 (US), read secondhand | A | High. If a form says "about 3 minutes", it should take about 3 minutes. |
| People were told a survey would take 10, 20 or 30 minutes. The longer the time stated, the fewer started and finished. One report gives 75%, 65% and 62% starting. Another gives 69.9% for 20 minutes. | Galesic and Bosnjak, *Public Opinion Quarterly*, 2009 (Croatia); start rates read secondhand | A | High. Add the job applicant study already in the library (Hardy and others, 2017). The lesson: keep forms short and state the time honestly. |
| Other trials found little or no effect of stated length. A Dutch postal invitation trial found none. A UK study found 12% gave up against 13%. | Survey trial reported on surveyinsights.org, 2021 (Netherlands); "Surely shorter is better?", *Survey Research Methods* (UK) | A and B | Medium. What is asked, and how, matters at least as much as length. |
| A review of 14 studies of surveys on phones. Fewer people gave up with designs made for phones, shorter surveys, simpler designs and a choice of how to answer. Email invitations did better than text messages. Grids, drop-downs, images, sliders and progress bars went with more people giving up. | Mavletova and Couper, in *Mobile Research Methods*, 2015 (Russia and US) | B | High. Many people will enquire on a phone. Use plain text boxes and radio buttons, and offer a phone call as another route. |
| In a trial on phones, text invitations led more people to give up than email: 10.5% against 5.7%. | Mavletova and Couper, *Journal of Survey Statistics and Methodology*, 2014 (Russia) | A | Medium. Sending a link to a long form by text may lose more people. |
| Pages with more questions, sliders or long open questions raised the risk of giving up. The total number of questions did not. A bar that barely moved more than doubled the risk. | Peytchev, *Public Opinion Quarterly*, 2009 (US) | B | High. Few items per page, and no long free-text questions at the first step. |
| Applying 20 web form guidelines to real company forms led to faster completion and fewer failed attempts to send. One summary reports 78% sent without error at the first try, against 42%. | Seckler, Heinz, Bargas-Avila, Opwis and Tuch, CHI conference, 2014 (Switzerland); percentages secondhand | B | High. Clear labels, explained formats, and keeping what people typed after an error are cheap to do. |
| GOV.UK advises one thing per page, merging pages only when research supports it. People are "generally very tolerant" of clicking through pages if the questions are easy and clearly relevant. | GOV.UK Service Manual and Design System; GDS user research blog, 2015 | D | High. This is widely used in UK public services. |
| The GOV.UK Carer's Allowance team removed progress indicators and saw no change in completion. | GDS user research blog, 2015 | D, one service, no figures published | High. It fits the experiments above. |
| Low-income families were offered help to complete the US student aid form. College enrolment rose 8.1 percentage points, from 34.2%, for school leavers. It rose 1.5 points for independent adults. Information alone had no effect. | Bettinger, Long, Oreopoulos and Sanbonmatsu, *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2012 (US) | A | Medium to high. The closest thing for fostering is offering a call back to finish the enquiry together. |
| 7,138 young people with idle online job applications got email reminders or nothing. 14.74% finished without a reminder. Reminders added 1.3 to 1.8 points, not clearly significant. 88.4% of finished applications were sent within a week of starting. | Bhanot and Heller, *Journal of Behavioral Public Administration*, 2022 (US) | A | High, as a caution. One kind follow-up within days makes sense. Repeated chasing is unlikely to be worth it. |
| 87,394 households applied for health insurance but chose no plan. A reminder letter raised enrolment by 1.3 points, from 8.1%. In a later trial, a phone call raised it by 2.7 points, from 12%. | Domurat, Menashe and Yin, *American Economic Review*, 2021; Myerson and others, *Health Affairs*, 2022 (US) | A | Medium. Small but real effects, with a call doing better than a letter. |
| Two-way texts tailored to the state of a student's aid form raised completion by about 6 points by the end of the messages. By late summer the gap was no longer significant. | Page, Castleman and Meyer, *Educational Evaluation and Policy Analysis*, 2020 (US) | A | Medium. Two-way contact helps more than one-way reminders, but the effect can fade. |
| In five experiments, people sometimes preferred a website that made them wait but showed the work being done. They preferred it to instant results. | Buell and Norton, *Management Science*, 2011 (US) | A | Medium. People may value a "what happens next" page that shows the real steps. The effect is on how people see the service, not on whether they go on. |
| Customers rated quality higher when they and the cooks could see each other. One summary reports 22.2% higher. | Buell, Kim and Tsay, *Management Science*, 2017 (US); detail secondhand | A | Low to medium. |
| Showing citizens the work done on their requests raised trust and engagement. One study used Boston's app for reporting problems. Showing a government that did nothing did not raise trust. | Buell, Porter and Norton, *Manufacturing & Service Operations Management*, 2021 (US); percentages secondhand | A and B | High. An acknowledgement that says what the service has done and will do, with dates, should build trust. That holds only if the service then does it. |
| Ofsted figures for England: 160,635 enquiries and 10,145 applications in 2020–21 (6%). 125,195 and 8,010 in 2022–23 (6%). Councils saw 12% of enquiries become applications, independent agencies 5% (March 2024 release). Sources disagree on later years, so check Ofsted's own tables. | Ofsted, *Fostering in England*, 2021 to 2025; Department for Education, *Renewing Fostering*, 2026 | B | Context only. Services count an "enquiry" in different ways, so the rates do not compare between services. |
| Not found: published English figures on fostering enquiry forms started against those sent. Services define an "enquiry" in different ways, from a website visit to a first conversation. Since 2023–24, Ofsted counts an enquiry only if it is "actionable". That means it gives names and contact details, and it is counted per household. | Department for Education and Ipsos, *Foster carer recruitment and conversion rates*, RR1366, 2023; Ofsted notes on changes to its figures | C | A real gap that Kinderform could help fill, with care for privacy. |

## 3. What it could mean for the Kinderform standard

All of this is for discussion. None of it is proven practice in fostering. The [research log](https://www.kinderform.org/research/log) tracks each idea.

### First calls and first replies: principles, not scripts

- **Welcome the offer first.** Thank the person and recognise what they are offering, before any question about suitability.
- **Describe the next step in concrete terms.** For example: "The next step is a 20-minute call with someone who has fostered, to answer your questions." Not: "We are committed to a rigorous, child-centred assessment."
- **Invite with a yes or no question about their choice.** For example: "Would you like a chat with one of our team next week?" Make it as easy to say no as yes.
- **If you can't do what they asked, offer another way straight away.**
- **End with the next step:** what happens, who does it and by when.
- **Near the end, ask "Is there something else you'd like to ask?"** and leave room for the answer.
- **Be honest early** about how long the assessment takes and what checks it involves. Do not volunteer reasons the person might fail.
- **Build guidance from what good staff already do,** using recordings made with consent, not words borrowed from other settings.

### The acknowledgement and "what happens next"

- Send an acknowledgement at once. Say what the service received, who will reply, by when, and what the reply will involve.
- Show real work, never invented busyness. No added delays or fake "processing" screens.
- If the service misses the date it gave, send a short, honest update.

### The reference form

- Keep the first step to a few easy questions: a name, a way to get in touch, the preferred way and time, and an optional "anything you'd like us to know".
- One thing per page, or one short page, tested with users. Prefer radio buttons and text boxes to grids, sliders and drop-downs.
- Say honestly how long it takes, and check that with real users.
- No progress bar on a short form. Show "Question 3 of 5" only when the count stays true. Never show progress that misleads.
- Make it work well on a phone, and offer a call on every page.
- Never clear what someone typed after an error.
- Saving and coming back matters more for the longer application later on. We found no experiments on it.

### When someone stops part-way

- Only if the person gave contact details and agreed to be contacted: send one kind follow-up within a few days, offering help by phone. Then stop.
- Offer help to finish, not more information.
- Make "not now" easy to say.

### What to measure: systems and pages, never staff or people

- Forms started against forms sent, as weekly totals for each page. Count in a way that does not identify or follow anyone across sites.
- The page where unfinished forms stop, as totals, to find confusing questions.
- Time from enquiry to the first reply from a person, and the share of replies made by the date promised.
- Whether a first call ended with an agreed next step. Record it as yes or no, to judge the process, not to rate staff.
- One shared definition of an enquiry. Ofsted's "actionable" definition is a ready starting point.
- Never build anything that scores, ranks or predicts which people will apply.

## 4. Cautions

- **Scripts can feel manipulative.** Using "willing" or "some" to persuade would feel like a sales tactic. The research describes what works in conversations. It is not a licence to engineer agreement. The 2013 mediation paper talks of "converting" callers to keep funding. That framing does not fit fostering.
- **Do not hide the hard parts.** Honesty early protects children and carers later.
- **No misleading progress bars,** even though bars that run fast first reduced quitting in experiments. They work by misleading people.
- **No theatre.** Fake waiting or animations to look busy would be dishonest, and would damage trust if found out.
- **Follow-up can become pressure.** One message, then stop.
- **Recordings need consent and care.** People may share sensitive family details. Record only with consent, keep as little as possible and store it securely. Do not reproduce long transcripts.
- **Judge systems, not people.** A measure such as "next step agreed" is for improving processes and pages. It must never rank or discipline staff.

## 6. Gaps and limits

- **Nothing on fostering.** We found no study of first calls or written first replies about fostering. Every view in section 1 on what carries over is our inference from mediation, GP surgeries, helplines and policing.
- **Little on written replies.** Emails and letters are rarely studied in any service. Most of this research is about phone calls.
- **Few experiments on wording.** Apart from Heritage and others (2007), most findings describe what happens and come from one research programme. No randomised trial has tested the "willing" finding.
- **No figures on unfinished fostering forms** in England, and services define an "enquiry" in different ways.
- **Little on other form features.** We found little evidence on saving and coming back, checking answers as people type, or marking required questions. We did not review commercial sources such as the Baymard Institute. They would be grade D.
- **Nothing on voicemail or call-back wording.**
- **Figures read secondhand or from abstracts.** Check these against the full texts before quoting them:
  - Galesic and Bosnjak's start rates
  - Yan and others (2011)
  - Buell, Kim and Tsay's 22.2%
  - Buell, Porter and Norton's figures for trust and support
  - Seckler and others' 78% and 42%
  - the training trial with student teachers
- **A survey is not an enquiry form.** Someone offering to foster may have stronger reasons to finish, so effects may be smaller or different.
- **Mostly American trials.** Most trials in section 2 are from the US. English public services may differ.
