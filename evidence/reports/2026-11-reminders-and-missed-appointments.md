# Clear, kind replies outperform clever nudges

> **Status:** desk review, version 1, 19 November 2026. It looks at reminders, first replies and missed appointments in health, courts, jobcentres, education, talking therapies and volunteering. An AI-assisted research tool produced it. None of the evidence is about fostering, so every view on what carries over is our own. See [how we made this](https://www.kinderform.org/about#how). Everything we propose is for discussion. It is not yet part of the Kinderform standard, the open standard that the project behind kinderform.org is drafting. The [research log](../research-log.json) says what each review could mean for the project.

The evidence from health, courts, jobcentres, education, talking therapies and volunteering supports the direction of all 3 Kinderform rules. It contradicts none of them. But it does not test any of the 3 time limits. We found no study in any field that compares:

- a 5-minute acknowledgement (the message that says an enquiry has arrived) with a slower one
- first contact from a person within 1 working day with contact within 2 or 3 days
- a recovery call within 2 working days of a missed event with a later one

What the evidence does show is this:

- What a first message says, and who it is from, matter more than how fast it arrives.
- Reminders for a booked event are worth a few percentage points, not a transformation.
- The people a service most easily loses are lost through silence, wrong contact details and quiet closure, not through lack of interest.

The strongest single results in this research work through felt obligation, penalty or guilt. Kinderform should not copy them. They include:

- a named jobcentre adviser's "I've booked you a place"
- a hospital text stating what a missed slot costs
- a guilt-worded reminder in Israel

Published effects shrink sharply at scale: 126 government trials averaged 1.4 percentage points, against 8.7 in published academic studies ([DellaVigna and Linos, 2022](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27594)).

So the honest case for a quick, kind and considered reply is respect and an informed choice. A modest gain in attendance comes as a side effect. The standard should keep its 3 time limits as service commitments. It should say plainly that they are commitments, not thresholds drawn from evidence. It should also tighten the rules on what the acknowledgement must contain and on what must never follow a missed event.

**How to read this report.** Evidence grades follow the research notes:

- **A**: randomised or quasi-experimental. In a randomised trial, chance decides who gets which version. A quasi-experimental study compares similar groups that chance did not choose.
- **B**: large observational or administrative. Observational studies watch what happens without changing anything. Administrative data are the records services keep anyway.
- **C**: qualitative, such as interviews.
- **D**: guidance or opinion.

Every claim carries one of 3 labels:

- **Evidence**: a finding from a cited study.
- **Observed practice**: what a service, charity or public body does or advises, with no proof of effect.
- **Recommendation (Kinderform)**: this report's own judgement.

"pp" means percentage points, an absolute change. For example, a rise from 10% to 12% is 2 pp. We give the starting rate each time. Every judgement about whether a finding carries over to fostering is this report's, not the source's. All of the evidence comes from other fields. None of it was gathered from people enquiring about fostering. Almost all of it measures attendance, which is not Kinderform's aim.

**Terms used in this report.**

- **Abstract**: the short summary at the start of a research paper.
- **Base**: the number of people a percentage is worked out from.
- **Cochrane review**: a systematic review by Cochrane, an international network that reviews health research.
- **Cohort study**: a study that follows a large group of people over time.
- **Confidence interval (CI)**: the range the true figure probably lies in. A 95% CI is the usual kind.
- **d**: a standard measure of the size of an effect, so that different studies can be compared. A value of 0 means no effect.
- **Exploratory**: an analysis the study did not plan in advance, so it is less certain.
- **Meta-analysis**: a study that pools the results of earlier studies into one estimate.
- **N**: the number of people in a study.
- **Nudge**: a small change to a message or design that aims to change what people do. A nudge unit is a government team that tests nudges.
- **Odds ratio, risk ratio**: 2 ways of comparing how likely an outcome is in one group against another. A value of 1 means no difference. Above 1 means more likely, and below 1 means less likely.
- **p**: the p-value. It shows how likely a difference this large would be by chance alone, if there were no real effect.
- **Publication bias**: studies with striking results are more likely to be published, so published effects look bigger. It is also called selective publication.
- **RCT**: randomised controlled trial. In a cluster RCT, chance decides which groups of people, such as clinics, get which version.
- **Regression discontinuity**: a study that compares cases just before and just after a cut-off, such as the date a form changed.
- **Relative change**: a change given as a share of the starting rate. A rise from 10% to 12% is 2 pp, or a relative rise of 20%.
- **Significant**: unlikely to be down to chance alone. The usual test is p below .05. "Not significant" means chance could explain the difference. "10% level only" means p was below .10 but not below .05, which is weaker.
- **Standard error**: a measure of how precise an estimate is. The smaller it is, the more precise the estimate.
- **Systematic review**: a review that finds and sums up the studies on a question, using set methods.
- **Talking therapies**: NHS psychological therapy services in England, mainly for anxiety and depression.
- **Working paper**: a research paper shared before it is published in a journal.

## The three rules hold as commitments, and none of the time limits has been tested

| Kinderform draft rule | Verdict | What the evidence says | Strongest grade behind the direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acknowledge within 5 minutes | Not contradicted; untested | No trial of acknowledgement speed exists in any field searched. Evidence on first messages is about what they say and who they are from. | None for speed; A for content and sender |
| A person makes first contact within 1 working day | Direction supported; threshold untested | Shorter waits go with higher attendance in large NHS datasets. In 2 small trials, very fast first appointments pointed the same way. The results were not statistically significant. Nothing supports a longer wait. | B (A, weak and inconclusive) |
| Call within 2 working days of a missed event, offering a one-to-one | Direction supported; threshold untested | Guidance and qualitative research favour prompt, active, supportive follow-up and oppose automatic closure. We found no trial of a recovery call, a second offer or any follow-up window. | D and C |

**Recommendation (Kinderform).** Keep all 3 limits. In the standard, describe them as "service commitments in line with the evidence on direction". Do not describe any of them as proven. Claiming more than the evidence shows would be exactly the kind of thing that harms trust with the sector.

## Nobody has tested a five-minute reply, but what the reply says is well evidenced

**Evidence (none found).** No study in health, courts, welfare, education or volunteering tests an instant acknowledgement against a slower one. None tests whether people read an automated instant reply as cold. Wherever Kinderform writes about these 2 claims, it should mark them as not backed by evidence.

**Evidence (A, quasi-experimental; USA, 2020).** The best evidence on a first written message is the redesign of the New York City court summons. The redesign moved the date and place to the top. It gave the form a plain heading that said what it was. It brought the key information to the front. Across 323,922 summonses, this cut missed court dates by **6.2 pp from a starting rate of about 47%** ([Fishbane, Ouss and Shah, 2020](https://psych.wustl.edu/files/psych/fishbane20.pdf)). It cost nothing extra to print.

The same source reports linked online experiments (N = 725). In these, the share who remembered the date correctly rose from 19% to 38%. For the place, it rose from 26% to 46%. The redesign reached everyone. The text reminders in the same study reached only the roughly 11% who had given a mobile number. The part of the redesign that moved the penalty to the front does not carry over to fostering, where there is no penalty.

**Evidence (A; UK, 2019, fieldwork 2013).** Bedford Jobcentre invited 1,224 claimants by text to recruitment events. Attendance was:

- **10.5%** with a standard message
- 14.8% when the claimant's first name was added (not significant)
- **17.4%** when the adviser's name was added as sender (p < .05)
- **26.8%** with "I've booked you a place. Good luck" signed by the adviser (p < .001) ([Sanders and Kirkman, 2019](https://journal-bpa.org/index.php/jbpa/article/download/24/36/463))

The authors' own cautions matter:

- It was one site, with about 300 people in each group.
- About a quarter of texts were undelivered.
- Each group's message added something to the one before, so the named sender cannot be separated from the wording.
- The effect was larger than in comparable studies.
- We found no study that repeats it.

A separate English trial on court fines also found that a named text did better: the average payment was £20.87 against £14.73. But that figure comes from a J-PAL summary, and from a debt-enforcement setting ([J-PAL summary of Haynes et al., 2013](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/assessing-effectiveness-alternative-text-messages-improve-collection-delinquent-fines)). J-PAL is the Abdul Latif Jameel Poverty Action Lab. Naming a professional made no significant difference in a far larger Israeli hospital trial ([Berliner Senderey et al., 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0234817)). The fair reading: a real named sender is honest, courteous and probably helpful, but adding the recipient's first name alone does little.

**Evidence (A, old and small trials; 2001 and about 2013).** A short "orientation" message tells people what will happen and who they will see. It is the best-supported low-cost step. Across 4 trials in the Cochrane review, any prompt reduced missed appointments (risk ratio 0.80, 95% CI 0.65 to 0.98) ([Cochrane CD002085, 2001](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD002085_prompts-encourage-appointment-attendance-people-serious-mental-illness)).

A review looked at first mental health appointments ([Psychiatric Services systematic review](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ps.201200540); author and year not yet checked, see the check list). Orientation letters raised attendance from **57% to 82%** in one trial. In another, they raised it from **68% to 74%**. In 2 further trials, they made no significant difference. The same review found that asking people to fill in an extra questionnaire before a first appointment did not help. In one trial, it lowered attendance from **83% to 73%**. That is directly relevant to long fostering enquiry forms and supports the draft standard's short first form.

**Evidence (D; England, 2025).** The Department for Education's Get Into Teaching team found that people often ignored the confirmation page after signing up for an adviser. The team has published no figures ([DfE design histories, updated 15 October 2025](https://design-histories.education.gov.uk/get-into-teaching/improving-our-mailing-list-and-adviser-sign-up-funnels-to-provide-more-personalised-guidance)). "What happens next" on a web page is not enough. The message itself has to carry it.

**Evidence (A; UK and USA, 2018 to 2022).** Messages cannot help if they do not arrive. The only English court-reminder trial found no effect on attendance (odds ratio 1.06, p = 0.661). A later check found **62% of phone numbers were unusable** ([Chivers and Barnes, 2018](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s41887-018-0023-5)). In a rural Californian court, about 47% of numbers were wrong or incomplete ([Owens and Sloan, 2022](https://bpb-us-e2.wpmucdn.com/faculty.sites.uci.edu/dist/9/540/files/2022/09/Shasta_County-4.pdf)). About 25% of the Bedford texts were undelivered.

This gives the 5-minute acknowledgement a practical job that does not depend on any persuasion effect. It proves the channel (such as email or text) works while the person is still at hand to correct a mistyped number or address.

**Evidence (B; England, 2022).** An instant sign-up followed by nothing is itself a reason people leave. NHS England surveyed 11,107 NHS Volunteer Responders. Of these, 9% never received a task alert or a shift within their travel distance ([NHS England, December 2022](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/nhs-volunteer-responders-volunteer-survey-and-engagement-meeting-results/)).

**Recommendation (Kinderform).** Confirm the 5-minute rule as a courtesy and a delivery check. Tighten what the message must contain:

- a plain first line saying what it is ("We've received your enquiry about fostering")
- the name of the real person or team who will be in touch, and by when
- what the first conversation involves and how long it usually takes
- how to change the time or channel
- a sentence saying every step is the person's choice

An automated acknowledgement should say it is automatic and name the person who will follow up. It should never pretend that a person typed it. This matters for trust. It also matters because the trial evidence is about real senders taking real actions.

## One working day is the right direction, on a scale of days not hours

**Evidence (B; England, 2023, data from 2017).** A study looked at 49,923 people referred to 7 English talking therapy services ([Saxon et al., 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9903253/)). The median wait to assessment (the middle value) was **9 days for those who attended and 15 days for those who did not**. Shorter waits went with attending, even after allowing for other factors.

Telephone assessments happened sooner: a median of 7 days, against 19 for face to face. After allowing for other factors, phone or face to face made no difference to attending the assessment (odds ratio 1.03). But a telephone assessment was followed by higher attendance at the first treatment session (odds ratio 1.28). The individual practitioner accounted for about 11.5% of the variation in whether people came back. This is association, not proof of cause. And it distinguishes a week from a fortnight, not the same day from the next.

**Evidence (A, small and inconclusive; USA, 1990 and 1999).** There were 2 trials of very fast first appointments. Both pointed the same way, but neither reached significance ([Psychiatric Services systematic review](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ps.201200540)). A same-day appointment gave **66% against 41%** attendance. An appointment within 48 hours gave **67% against 57%** for a wait of about 5 days.

**Evidence (B; England, 2022, data from 2010 to 2014).** People who referred themselves to talking therapies were far more likely to attend assessment than those referred by a GP (family doctor). The odds ratio was about 3.4, in a model of 45,332 referrals ([Sweetman et al., 2022](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/192581/7/Risk_factors_for_initial_appointment_non_attendance_at_Improving_Access_to_Psychological_Therapy_IAPT_services_A_retrospective_analysis.pdf)). Of all 97,020 referrals, 66% attended. Use only the assessment figures. The paper's figures for attending treatment disagree with each other. What carries over is that someone who comes forward unprompted is at a high point of willingness. So the main risk is the service's delay.

**Evidence (B and C; Great Britain and England and Wales, 2019 to 2024).** In volunteering, a growing minority find the way in slower than they expected: **14% in 2019 and 20% in 2023**. Among 18 to 24 year olds, the share rose from 22% to 33% ([NCVO Time Well Spent 2019](https://www.ncvo.org.uk/news-and-insights/news-index/time-well-spent-national-survey-volunteer-experience/volunteer-experience/); [NCVO 2023](https://www.ncvo.org.uk/news-and-insights/news-index/time-well-spent-2023/volunteer-experience-impact/)). In 2019, 23% of those who had a formal interview expected a quicker process, against 13% of those who had an informal chat. These are people who did get through, so the figures understate the problem.

The closest comparison with fostering is special constable recruitment. Special constables are volunteer police officers. Becoming one is a vetted, months-long route into a helping role, run by a public body. A qualitative study of 63 applicants found people waiting months with no feedback ([Birmingham City University open access copy](https://www.open-access.bcu.ac.uk/15897/1/Improving_the_Application_and_Assessment_Experiences_of_Special_Constable_Candidates_in_England_and_Wales_-_Accepted%281%29.pdf)). They had "to keep prompting for updates". In one case, a person took up a different volunteer role during the wait. We found no study that times a reply to a volunteer enquiry and follows who goes on to volunteer.

**Observed practice (D; England, 2023).** NHS Employers independently recommends the same components as the Kinderform draft, for people waiting to start a job ([NHS Employers, March 2023](https://www.nhsemployers.org/articles/reducing-delay-between-job-offer-and-start-date)). They are a single named contact, an agreed rhythm of contact and stated timescales. NHS Employers publishes no figures on their effect.

**Recommendation (Kinderform).** Confirm the 1-working-day rule and tighten its wording. One description of the rule is "a person makes first contact". The project's earlier evidence report describes it as a "first human attempt". These are different clocks. The standard should:

- define the rule as a first attempt by a named person within 1 working day
- keep the separate limit for a conversation actually held
- measure both from interaction records (the record of each contact)

The finding about individual practitioners supports saying something about the quality of the first conversation. Any check of it should look across the whole service and never score individual staff.

## Reminders buy a few percentage points, and the best-known wins shrink or vanish at scale

### Where reminders help, and by how much

**Evidence (A; international, 2013 and 2016).** Text reminders reliably raise attendance when nothing was being sent before. The 2013 Cochrane review (8 trials, 6,615 people) reports attendance of **67.8% with no reminder and 78.6% with a text**. That is about 10.8 pp (the note-taker's arithmetic). Texts did as well as phone calls (risk ratio 0.99). In 2 studies, texts cost 55% to 65% less per attendance ([Cochrane review, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24310741/)).

A later meta-analysis found no-shows of **15% against 21%**, about 6 pp ([Robotham et al., 2016](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/6/10/e012116.full)). Studies that sent 2 or more reminders did better than those that sent one. But the paper gives 2 different figures for how much better (see the check list).

**Evidence (A; USA, 2020 to 2025).** Court reminders show the same pattern and the same dependence on the starting point. New York texts cut missed dates by about **8 pp from 37.9%** ([Fishbane et al., 2020](https://psych.wustl.edu/files/psych/fishbane20.pdf)). In one group, the texts only helped people plan, with no mention of consequences. That group still achieved **6.0 pp**.

A county court trial of 31,372 people cut missed dates by **8.3 to 8.6 pp from about 21%** ([Emanuel and Ho, 2024](https://nataliaemanuel.github.io/ne_website/Emanuel_Ho_FTA.pdf)). Its texts included practical help, such as how to rearrange and free childcare. In Santa Clara County, the starting rate was low. There, warrants at the first date fell from **12.1% to 9.7%**, about 2.5 pp ([Chohlas-Wood et al., 2025](https://www.5harad.com/papers/court-reminders.pdf)). A warrant here is a court order issued after a missed date. The plan-only group is the one that matters for fostering, because it worked without any threat.

**Evidence (A; England, 2015).** Where a reminder already exists, rewording buys less. At Barts Health in London, the best wording cut missed appointments from **11.1% to 8.4%**, about 2.7 pp ([Hallsworth et al., 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0137306)).

**Evidence (A and D; Israel and England).** Clearer messages produce more advance cancellations. In the Israeli trial, they rose from 17.2% to about 26 to 27%. In one Barts group, they rose from 8.8% to 10.5%. For fostering, a person who says "not now" is better served than one who drifts away. So this is a good outcome.

NHS England's guidance treats two-way reply, out-of-hours phone lines and choice of time as a minimum standard ([NHS England, Reducing did not attends](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/reducing-did-not-attends-dnas-in-outpatient-services/)). The same page says reminders cut missed appointments "by up to 80%". That claim is a best-case case study from a supplier. Do not quote it as a typical effect.

### Nulls, backfires and shrinkage deserve the same weight

**Evidence (A; USA, 2022).** Across all 126 trials run by 2 US government nudge units, the average effect was **1.4 pp**. The trials covered over 23 million people. Published academic nudge trials averaged **8.7 pp**. Selective publication can account for the whole gap, and small studies make it worse ([DellaVigna and Linos, 2022](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27594)).

These trials are mostly letters, emails and texts from public bodies. That is what a fostering service sends. So 1.4 pp is the most defensible single planning figure. It covers all kinds of nudge, not reminders alone.

A re-analysis of a widely cited meta-analysis corrected for publication bias ([Maier et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9351501)). After that correction, the average nudge effect falls from d = 0.43 to d = 0.04. Reminders and similar help come to 0.01. But real variation between interventions remains.

**Evidence (A; USA, 2019 to 2021).** The clearest worked example is summer text messages to school leavers heading for college. Local trials reported gains of about 4 to 7 pp in some sites, and nothing in Boston. These site figures are secondhand, from a [NEBHE summary](https://www.nebhe.org/post/can-text-messages-mitigate-summer-melt) (NEBHE is the New England Board of Higher Education).

National and state bodies sent the same approach to more than 800,000 students. Enrolment changed by **+0.3 pp (standard error 0.2)**. The study ruled out effects above 0.5 pp, and no subgroup of students benefited. Framing, channel, timing and layout made no difference. Only 11.6% took up an offer of one-to-one advice by text. The authors' untested explanation is that the earlier messages came from local partners the students knew ([Bird et al., 2019](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26158.pdf)). That is encouraging for a local fostering team. It is also a warning: do not expect a national or mass-mail layer to do the same job.

**Evidence (A; USA, 2023).** A pragmatic trial, run in everyday conditions, covered about 88,500 appointments at a US veterans' service. In it, 4 nudge versions of the reminder letter did nothing. The odds ratios for missing were 1.14 in primary care (starting rate 11.9%) and 1.20 in mental health (starting rate 18.0%). Neither was significant, and both were in the wrong direction. The authors concluded that more intensive support is needed, not changes to messages ([Teo et al., 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11606-023-08131-5)).

**Evidence (A; various).** Alongside the wins sit other results that found no effect, or the opposite effect:

- When 3 small trials in mental health services were pooled, text reminders showed no clear effect ([Robotham et al., 2016](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/6/10/e012116.full)).
- At Barts, putting the cancellation number in the text did not reduce missed appointments. Nor did a social-norm line, which tells people what most others do.
- At Barts, an empathy appeal did worse than the best message (10.7% against 8.2%).
- Staffed two-way texting added nothing over one-way texts in the county court trial.
- In one trial, phone reminders before a first mental health appointment helped (83% against 66%). In another, they made no difference. In a third, they went with lower attendance (48% against 76%).
- Homeless defendants in Shasta County gained nothing from texts. Their starting miss rate was about 86.5%.
- A less punitive school absence notice helped by only 0.07 days in a month across 131,312 families ([Lasky-Fink et al., 2021](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/using-behavioral-insights-improve-school-administrative-communications-case-truancy-0)).

**Evidence (B; England, 2010).** One design choice backfires in a way attendance figures hide. A Sheffield NHS psychotherapy service required people to opt in before it gave them a slot ([Houghton et al., 2010](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-psychiatrist/article/effects-of-optin-letters-in-a-national-health-service-psychotherapy-service/ED4A36AF22253CE707B7F80258950D68)). Among those booked, non-attendance fell from 20% to 15% (not significant). But the share of all referrals who attended a first appointment fell from **80% to 65%**, because one in five never opted in. People with anxiety were about twice as likely not to reach the service. Small opt-in trials elsewhere gave mixed results. In fostering, the equivalent is "reply to confirm or we will close your enquiry".

**Evidence (B and D).** People like self-booking, and it is reasonable to offer ([Zhao et al., 2017](https://www.jmir.org/2017/4/e134)). But the evidence that it raises attendance is weak, and mostly from studies with no comparison group. And it suits people who are confident online. No randomised trial compares self-booked first appointments with allocated ones. We found no head-to-head trial of reminder timing either.

**Recommendation (Kinderform).** Add a simple reminder rule for booked events:

- a confirmation at booking
- a reminder about a week ahead
- a reminder the day before

Each goes in the person's chosen channel. Each gives the date, time, place and how long it lasts, and a one-step way to rearrange or say "not now". Present the expected gain as "a few percentage points at most, and possibly nothing where personal follow-up is already good". Each service should measure its own results before and after, and not assume a published effect.

## After a missed event, the evidence backs an open door more than any deadline

**Evidence (none found).** We found no randomised or quasi-experimental study of a personal call, a second offer or a text rebooking link after a missed first appointment. The New York court study randomised follow-up texts after a missed date, but published no results. This is the largest gap behind any of the 3 rules. The project's earlier evidence report gives the basis of the 2-working-day rule as "Erie County via Sebba". That source is not in the notes for this report, and we did not re-check it here.

**Observed practice (D; England).** The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) has guidance for a much higher-risk group ([NICE QS188, statement 4](https://nice.org.uk/guidance/qs188/chapter/quality-statement-4-follow-up-after-any-missed-appointment)). The page we read does not show its publication date. The guidance says follow-up after a missed appointment "should happen promptly and actively, especially after the first missed appointment". It says this should be by phone, text or visit, including outside routine hours. It also says services should not automatically discharge people (end their care). Its 24-hour timescale applies where there are concerns about risk. It cannot justify a number of days for someone enquiring about fostering. NHS England says people should be reviewed and contacted before any discharge ([NHS England, Reducing did not attends](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/reducing-did-not-attends-dnas-in-outpatient-services/)).

**Evidence (C; UK, 2026).** Co-design work involved people who repeatedly miss care, through 61 interviews and a 16-member advisory group. Co-design means shaping a service together with the people who use it. The work supports:

- follow-up framed as support, not reprimand
- personal two-way contact
- flexible times
- a named coordinator

It also identifies having to be referred again after a miss as a barrier in itself. The package has not yet been tested in practice ([Lindsay et al., 2026](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/389588/3/389588.pdf)).

Interviews with clinical psychologists describe ([Pickersgill, 2026](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/85D9947C21C9CC72287C29DF6027EC8E/core-reader)):

- letters saying "write to us within two weeks or we close your case"
- some people discharged before any contact
- staff reading a miss as lack of "readiness"

In a very small London audit, reasons for missing included ([LSBU open research copy](https://openresearch.lsbu.ac.uk/download/81c9ad811935e163b2676e96c1a1645bbd65818ab183b59562155fa0639c6412/776431/non%20attendance%20at%20psychological%20therapy%20appointments%20proof.pdf)):

- not knowing about the appointment
- forgetting
- a family crisis
- illness
- not being able to get through to the service

**Evidence (A, lab; USA, 2020).** People wrongly assume a missed obligation is deliberate. In online experiments, people were prompted to think of a miss as a possible mistake. Their support for reminders then rose from 63% to 82% ([Fishbane et al., 2020](https://psych.wustl.edu/files/psych/fishbane20.pdf)). In the county court trial, the authors concluded that a large share of missed dates are mistakes ([Emanuel and Ho, 2024](https://nataliaemanuel.github.io/ne_website/Emanuel_Ho_FTA.pdf)).

**Evidence (A; USA, 2023).** Tone has trial support in a voluntary setting. In 2 field experiments with 117,073 renters, some outreach told people their situation was not their fault. This raised requests for rental assistance by about 18% over information alone ([Lasky-Fink and Linos, 2023](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/improving-delivery-social-safety-net-role-stigma-0)). That is a relative change, and the starting rate was not in the notes. The outcome was requests for help, not attendance.

**Recommendation (Kinderform).** Confirm the call within 2 working days as a commitment consistent with "promptly and actively". Tighten what surrounds it:

- The call should assume a slip or a clash, not bad faith.
- If there is no answer, the service leaves a short personal voicemail. It also sends a message in the person's chosen channel with 3 equal options: another date, a one-to-one conversation, or "not for now".
- The service treats a first miss as normal, and a first miss never closes an enquiry.
- No service asks a person to confirm their interest as a condition of staying on its records.
- If the person shares what got in the way, the service records it as a code. That way, the service can fix venues, times and wording.
- The service records "not now" as a good outcome.

## Who is not reached, and by what

**Evidence (A).** Texts and emails reach only people whose details are right and who have a stable phone. Only about 11% of New York summons recipients gave a number ([Fishbane et al., 2020](https://psych.wustl.edu/files/psych/fishbane20.pdf)). Those who did were already more likely to attend. People in neighbourhoods with more Black and Hispanic residents were less likely to give one. The Barts authors describe the patients they could reach as a minority who may differ from the rest ([Hallsworth et al., 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0137306)). Homeless defendants gained nothing. Among people who were reached, poorer New York neighbourhoods gained more (12.5 pp against 6.4 pp; exploratory). The pattern is two-sided. Reminders narrow gaps among people who can be reached. They widen the gap between those who can be reached and those who cannot.

**Evidence (B; Scotland, 2017 and 2019).** People's circumstances shape who misses. Among 550,083 Scottish general practice patients, repeated missing was 2.27 times as likely in the most deprived group ([Ellis et al., 2017](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/88220)). It was also somewhat more likely at ages 16 to 30. The linked finding is that repeated missing goes with much higher death rates among people with mental health conditions ([McQueenie et al., 2019](https://doaj.org/article/8726f50dfd414d0e89238a59bd35dfdd)). That finding is not relevant to fostering. It should not appear in Kinderform public writing as if it were. Only the framing point carries over.

**Evidence (B; England).** In talking therapies, people in the least deprived fifth were more likely to attend assessment (odds ratio 1.51) ([Saxon et al., 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9903253/)). Several minority ethnic groups were less likely to attend (for example, odds ratio 0.80 for Black against White patients) ([Sweetman et al., 2022](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/192581/7/Risk_factors_for_initial_appointment_non_attendance_at_Improving_Access_to_Psychological_Therapy_IAPT_services_A_retrospective_analysis.pdf)). Opt-in steps lose people with anxiety. In volunteering, younger adults are the least tolerant of delay. In 2019, 34% of minority ethnic volunteers (base 177) agreed there was too much bureaucracy, against 24% overall ([NCVO, 2019](https://www.ncvo.org.uk/news-and-insights/news-index/time-well-spent-national-survey-volunteer-experience/volunteer-experience/)).

**Evidence (C and D).** Reminders do not remove practical barriers such as transport, childcare, work clashes, limited hours or mistrust of services ([Lindsay et al., 2026](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/389588/3/389588.pdf); [NHS England](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/reducing-did-not-attends-dnas-in-outpatient-services/)). About 22% of Santa Clara clients needed messages in Spanish or Vietnamese, and got them ([Chohlas-Wood et al., 2025](https://www.5harad.com/papers/court-reminders.pdf)). None of the trials we read reported reminder effects by deprivation, ethnicity, language or disability. No figures exist for the effect of translated reminders.

**Recommendation (Kinderform).** Fix the main page, form and first message before adding text reminders, because those reach everyone. Offer at least one route that needs no smartphone. Treat a bounced or undelivered message as the service's task to resolve through another channel. Let people rearrange outside office hours without phoning. Provide plain English and the languages the local population needs. Do not borrow health services' tools for predicting who will miss. Nothing in Kinderform scores, ranks or predicts people who enquire.

A reply to confirm attendance can prompt a courtesy call to someone who has not replied. But the service must never use it to rate a person or to decide who is worth following up. In Santa Clara, the gap in warrants between people who confirmed and those who did not was 2.9% against 16.8%. That gap was self-selection, not an effect: the people who chose to confirm already differed from those who did not.

## High-performing techniques Kinderform should not adopt

Almost every trial in this research counts attendance as success. Kinderform's aim is a well-informed decision and a person who feels respected. The project's guiding sentence is that everyone who offers to care for a child deserves a quick, kind and considered reply. Against that sentence, a message that raises attendance by making someone feel pushed is a failure. None of the trials measured how recipients felt. A fostering service also holds power over approval. Techniques that are mild between equals feel different when they come from a body that will later assess you.

| Technique | What it achieved | Why Kinderform should not use it |
|---|---|---|
| Guilt wording | **Evidence (A).** Missed appointments 14.2% against 21.1% in Israel ([Berliner Senderey et al., 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0234817)) | Works by making the person feel bad. A person offering to care for a child would be treated as a problem to manage. |
| Stating what a missed slot costs the service | **Evidence (A).** 8.4% against 11.1% at Barts ([Hallsworth et al., 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0137306)) | A pressure message suited, at most, to a clinic slot someone else could use. It has no place in a voluntary offer. |
| Stating a penalty or consequence | **Evidence (A).** 8.9 pp against 6.0 pp for planning alone in New York ([Fishbane et al., 2020](https://psych.wustl.edu/files/psych/fishbane20.pdf)) | There is no penalty for missing a fostering session, and implying one would be manipulative. The non-threatening message kept about two thirds of the effect. |
| "Your absence will be recorded" | **Evidence (A).** 9.6%, no better than the best message at Barts | A veiled threat, and it did not work. |
| Reciprocity: "I've done this for you" to create a debt | **Evidence (A).** 26.8% against 10.5% in Bedford; the authors name reciprocity as the mechanism ([Sanders and Kirkman, 2019](https://journal-bpa.org/index.php/jbpa/article/download/24/36/463)) | Felt obligation is not informed choice. Keep the honest parts: a real named person, and a place held only when the person asked for one. |
| Social-norm lines ("9 out of 10 attend") | **Evidence (A).** No effect on missed appointments at Barts; 3.3 pp in Israel | Inconsistent, and it works through pressure to conform. |
| Opt-in or "confirm or we close" | **Evidence (B).** People seen fell from 80% to 65% of referrals ([Houghton et al., 2010](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-psychiatrist/article/effects-of-optin-letters-in-a-national-health-service-psychotherapy-service/ED4A36AF22253CE707B7F80258950D68)) | Improves the service's figures while quietly dropping less confident people. |
| Closing after one miss | **Evidence (C, D).** No measured benefit; harm falls on people with the most complicated lives | Makes people start again and reads a slip as lack of commitment. |
| Being vague about what is optional | **Evidence (C).** Work coaches at the Department for Work and Pensions (DWP) did this to raise attendance. Some claimants attended from fear and found it stressful ([DWP, 2017](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a8229cced915d74e340211c/2015-esa-trials-a-synthesis.pdf)) | Raises attendance and damages trust. |
| Written commitments, attitude or "strengths" exercises | **Evidence (A, bundled, team's own report; D critique).** Reported as 15 to 20% more people off benefits at 13 weeks in the Loughton pilot ([Behavioural Insights Team](https://www.bi.team/?p=5287)). Criticised as coercive under sanctions ([Friedli and Stearn, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4484497)) | Messages must never test, score or steer a person's attitude. Practical journey details are help; a commitment device is not. |
| Predicting who will miss | **Observed practice (D).** Used in parts of the health sector | Breaks Kinderform's rule against scoring, ranking or predicting people. |

**Recommendation (Kinderform), and a disagreement to record.** The project's earlier evidence report lists messages that say "I have done something for you" among the "strongest transferable mechanisms". It also cites reciprocity, the pull to return a favour, as a basis for the contact rhythm. This report disagrees with that framing. The named sender and the concrete next step should stay, because they are honest and courteous. Reciprocity as a design goal should come out of the standard's stated basis. That is because it builds a sense of debt into a message to someone weighing a life decision. If that were noticed, it would harm trust with carers and with the sector.

## Gaps

- No study tests acknowledgement speed. None tests whether an instant automated reply feels cold.
- No trial tests any follow-up after a missed first appointment, or any follow-up window. None tests closing after one miss against a more tolerant policy.
- No head-to-head trial compares reminder timings, self-booked with allocated slots, or evening and weekend availability.
- No trial reports reminder effects by deprivation, ethnicity, language or disability.
- No trial measures trust, felt respect or felt pressure as an outcome. None follows people who attended after a reminder and later withdrew. So whether reminders bring in people who were not ready is unknown.
- We found no evidence on how many reminders become unwelcome.
- We found nothing citable on Samaritans, Girlguiding or school governor enquiries, or on Get Into Teaching adviser outcomes. Nor did we find anything citable on DWP appointment reminders, or on screening and vaccination reminders.

We did not read:

- the 2024 "missingness" realist review (a review of what works, for whom and why)
- current NHS Talking Therapies national figures
- the full texts of Sanders et al. (2021), Castleman and Page (2015) and Mertens et al. (2022)

All of the evidence is from fields other than fostering.

## Questions Kinderform could test openly

Each of these could be:

- pre-registered, with the method published first
- run with a willing service or hub
- published whatever the result

Services would see their own results privately before anything is made public. None of the tests involves scoring or predicting people, and none uses pressure wording in any group.

1. Compare an acknowledgement that names the person who will call, and says when, with a generic one. Does it change the share of people who have a first conversation? Does it change their own rating of "I knew what would happen next"?
2. Does a first attempt on the same working day against the next working day change anything people notice or value?
3. After a missed session, compare a call plus a 3-option message with a letter alone. Does it change how many people rebook, how many say "not now", and how many simply go quiet?
4. Do 1, 2 or 3 reminders differ in attendance, rearrangement and opt-outs, and at what point do people find them unwelcome?
5. Does a choice of session times, including evenings and weekends, change who attends, analysed with consent by group?
6. What share of acknowledgements bounce or go undelivered, and how many people are reached through a second channel?
7. Do people who say "not now" and are contacted on the date they chose feel respected, and do some return?

## Conclusion

The evidence from nearby fields changes the argument for Kinderform's rules more than it changes the rules. Speed limits that looked like evidence-based thresholds turn out to be reasonable promises that nobody has tested. The well-evidenced parts are plain content, a known local sender, working contact details and an open door after a miss. The techniques with the largest published effects are the ones a project built on respect has to refuse. The evidence from trials at scale means little is lost by refusing them. The realistic prize from any message was always a few percentage points.

That leaves fostering services with an advantage the health service lacks. They hear from few enough people to do the personal follow-up that hospital guidance recommends and cannot afford. The standard's event records also make it possible to measure what no trial in this research measured:

- whether a person who offered to care for a child got a clear reply
- whether they knew what would happen next
- whether they felt free to say no

Testing those questions openly would fill gaps that matter well beyond fostering.
