# Clear, kind replies outperform clever nudges

> **Status:** desk review, version 1, 19 November 2026. It looks at reminders, first replies and missed appointments in health, courts, jobcentres, education, talking therapies and volunteering. An AI-assisted research tool produced it. None of the evidence is about fostering, so every view on what carries over is our own. The figures have **not yet been checked by a person** against the original sources, and many were read from a summary of the study only. Check the source before you quote anything, starting with the list under "Check before public use". Everything we propose is for discussion and is not yet part of the OUCM standard, the open standard that the project behind kinderform.org is drafting. The [research log](../research-log.json) says what each review could mean for the project.

The evidence from health, courts, jobcentres, education, talking therapies and volunteering supports the direction of all three OUCM rules and contradicts none of them, but it does not test any of the three time limits. No study found in any field compares a 5-minute acknowledgement with a slower one, a 1-working-day human contact with a 2- or 3-day one, or a 2-working-day recovery call with a later one. What the evidence does show is that the content and sender of a first message matter more than its speed, that reminders for a booked event are worth a few percentage points and not a transformation, and that the people a service most easily loses are lost through silence, wrong contact details and quiet closure, not through lack of interest. The strongest single results in this literature (a named jobcentre adviser's "I've booked you a place", a hospital text stating what a missed slot costs, a guilt-worded reminder in Israel) work through felt obligation, penalty or guilt, and OUCM should not copy them. Published effects shrink sharply at scale: 126 government trials averaged 1.4 percentage points against 8.7 in published academic studies ([DellaVigna and Linos, 2022](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27594)). The honest case for a quick, kind and considered reply is therefore respect and an informed choice, with a modest attendance gain as a side effect. The standard should keep its three time limits as service commitments, say plainly that they are commitments and not evidence-derived thresholds, and tighten what the acknowledgement must contain and what must never follow a missed event.

**How to read this report.** Evidence grades follow the research notes: **A** randomised or quasi-experimental; **B** large observational or administrative; **C** qualitative; **D** guidance or opinion. Every claim carries one of three labels: **Evidence** (a finding from a cited study), **Observed practice** (what a service, charity or public body does or advises, with no proof of effect), or **Recommendation (OUCM)** (this report's own judgement). "pp" means percentage points, an absolute change; the starting rate is given each time. Every judgement about whether a finding carries over to fostering is this report's, not the source's. All of the evidence comes from other fields. None of it was gathered from people enquiring about fostering, and almost all of it measures attendance, which is not OUCM's aim.

## The three rules hold as commitments, and none of the time limits has been tested

| OUCM draft rule | Verdict | What the evidence says | Strongest grade behind the direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acknowledge within 5 minutes | Not contradicted; untested | No trial of acknowledgement speed exists in any field searched. Evidence on first messages is about what they say and who they are from. | None for speed; A for content and sender |
| A person makes first contact within 1 working day | Direction supported; threshold untested | Shorter waits go with higher attendance in large NHS datasets. Two small trials of very fast first appointments pointed the same way and were not statistically significant. Nothing supports a longer wait. | B (A, weak and inconclusive) |
| Call within 2 working days of a missed event, offering a one-to-one | Direction supported; threshold untested | Guidance and qualitative research favour prompt, active, supportive follow-up and oppose automatic closure. No trial of a recovery call, a second offer or any follow-up window was found. | D and C |

**Recommendation (OUCM).** Keep all three limits. Describe them in the standard as "service commitments in line with the evidence on direction", and do not describe any of them as proven. Overclaiming here would be exactly the kind of thing that harms trust with the sector.

## Nobody has tested a five-minute reply, but what the reply says is well evidenced

**Evidence (none found).** No study in health, courts, welfare, education or volunteering tests an instant acknowledgement against a slower one, and none tests whether an automated instant reply is read as cold. Both claims should be marked as unevidenced wherever OUCM writes about them.

**Evidence (A, quasi-experimental; USA, 2020).** The best evidence on a first written message is the redesign of the New York City court summons. Moving the date and place to the top, giving the form a plain heading that said what it was, and bringing the key information to the front cut missed court dates by **6.2 pp from a baseline of about 47%** across 323,922 summonses, at no extra printing cost ([Fishbane, Ouss and Shah, 2020](https://psych.wustl.edu/files/psych/fishbane20.pdf)). In linked online experiments (N = 725), correct recall of the date rose from 19% to 38% and of the place from 26% to 46% (same source). The redesign reached everyone; the text reminders in the same study reached only the roughly 11% who had given a mobile number. The part of the redesign that moved the penalty to the front does not carry over to fostering, where there is no penalty.

**Evidence (A; UK, 2019, fieldwork 2013).** At Bedford Jobcentre, 1,224 claimants were invited by text to recruitment events. Attendance was **10.5%** with a standard message, 14.8% when the claimant's first name was added (not significant), **17.4%** when the adviser's name was added as sender (p < .05), and **26.8%** with "I've booked you a place. Good luck" signed by the adviser (p < .001) ([Sanders and Kirkman, 2019](https://journal-bpa.org/index.php/jbpa/article/download/24/36/463)). The authors' own cautions matter: one site, about 300 people per arm, about a quarter of texts undelivered, arms built up additively so the named sender cannot be separated from the wording, an effect larger than comparable studies, and no replication found. A separate English court-fines trial also found a named text did better (average payment £20.87 against £14.73), but that figure comes from a J-PAL summary and from a debt-enforcement setting ([J-PAL summary of Haynes et al., 2013](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/assessing-effectiveness-alternative-text-messages-improve-collection-delinquent-fines)). Naming a professional made no significant difference in a far larger Israeli hospital trial ([Berliner Senderey et al., 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0234817)). The fair reading is that a real named sender is honest, courteous and probably helpful, and that inserting the recipient's first name alone does little.

**Evidence (A, old and small trials; 2001 and about 2013).** A short "orientation" message that tells people what will happen and who they will see is the best-supported low-cost step. Across four trials in the Cochrane review, any prompt reduced missed appointments (risk ratio 0.80, 95% CI 0.65 to 0.98) ([Cochrane CD002085, 2001](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD002085_prompts-encourage-appointment-attendance-people-serious-mental-illness)). In a review of first mental health appointments, orientation letters raised attendance from **57% to 82%** in one trial and from **68% to 74%** in another, with two further trials showing no significant difference ([Psychiatric Services systematic review](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ps.201200540); author and year not yet checked, see the check list). The same review found that asking people to fill in an extra questionnaire before a first appointment did not help and in one trial lowered attendance from **83% to 73%**. That is directly relevant to long fostering enquiry forms and supports the draft standard's short first form.

**Evidence (D; England, 2025).** The Department for Education's Get Into Teaching team found that people often ignored the confirmation page after signing up for an adviser; no figures are published ([DfE design histories, updated 15 October 2025](https://design-histories.education.gov.uk/get-into-teaching/improving-our-mailing-list-and-adviser-sign-up-funnels-to-provide-more-personalised-guidance)). "What happens next" on a web page is not enough; the message itself has to carry it.

**Evidence (A; UK and USA, 2018 to 2022).** Messages cannot help if they do not arrive. The only English court-reminder trial found no effect on attendance (odds ratio 1.06, p = 0.661), and a later check found **62% of phone numbers were unusable** ([Chivers and Barnes, 2018](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s41887-018-0023-5)). About 47% of numbers were wrong or incomplete in a rural Californian court ([Owens and Sloan, 2022](https://bpb-us-e2.wpmucdn.com/faculty.sites.uci.edu/dist/9/540/files/2022/09/Shasta_County-4.pdf)) and about 25% of the Bedford texts were undelivered. This gives the 5-minute acknowledgement a practical job that does not depend on any persuasion effect: it proves the channel works while the person is still at hand to correct a mistyped number or address.

**Evidence (B; England, 2022).** An instant sign-up followed by nothing is itself a reason people leave. In NHS England's survey of 11,107 NHS Volunteer Responders, 9% never received a task alert or a shift within their travel distance ([NHS England, December 2022](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/nhs-volunteer-responders-volunteer-survey-and-engagement-meeting-results/)).

**Recommendation (OUCM).** Confirm the 5-minute rule as a courtesy and a delivery check, and tighten what the message must contain: a plain first line saying what it is ("We've received your enquiry about fostering"); the name of the real person or team who will be in touch and by when; what the first conversation involves and how long it usually takes; how to change the time or channel; and a sentence saying every step is the person's choice. An automated acknowledgement should say it is automatic and name the person who will follow up. It should never pretend to have been typed by a person, both for trust and because the trial evidence is about real senders taking real actions.

## One working day is the right direction, on a scale of days not hours

**Evidence (B; England, 2023, data from 2017).** Among 49,923 people referred to seven English talking therapy services, the median wait to assessment was **9 days for those who attended and 15 days for those who did not**, and shorter waits were independently associated with attending ([Saxon et al., 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9903253/)). Telephone assessments happened sooner (median 7 days against 19 face to face); after adjustment the mode made no difference to attending the assessment (odds ratio 1.03) but was followed by higher attendance at the first treatment session (odds ratio 1.28). The individual practitioner accounted for about 11.5% of the variation in whether people came back. This is association, not proof of cause, and it distinguishes a week from a fortnight, not the same day from the next.

**Evidence (A, small and inconclusive; USA, 1990 and 1999).** Two trials of very fast first appointments pointed the same way without reaching significance: a same-day appointment gave **66% against 41%** attendance, and an appointment within 48 hours gave **67% against 57%** against about five days ([Psychiatric Services systematic review](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ps.201200540)).

**Evidence (B; England, 2022, data from 2010 to 2014).** People who referred themselves to talking therapies were far more likely to attend assessment than those referred by a GP (odds ratio about 3.4 in a model of 45,332 referrals; 66% of all 97,020 referrals attended) ([Sweetman et al., 2022](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/192581/7/Risk_factors_for_initial_appointment_non_attendance_at_Improving_Access_to_Psychological_Therapy_IAPT_services_A_retrospective_analysis.pdf)). Use only the assessment figures; the paper's treatment-attendance figures disagree with each other. The carry-over is that someone who comes forward unprompted is at a high point of willingness, so the main risk is the service's delay.

**Evidence (B and C; Great Britain and England and Wales, 2019 to 2024).** In volunteering, a growing minority find the way in slower than they expected: **14% in 2019 and 20% in 2023**, and among 18 to 24 year olds 22% rising to 33% ([NCVO Time Well Spent 2019](https://www.ncvo.org.uk/news-and-insights/news-index/time-well-spent-national-survey-volunteer-experience/volunteer-experience/); [NCVO 2023](https://www.ncvo.org.uk/news-and-insights/news-index/time-well-spent-2023/volunteer-experience-impact/)). In 2019, 23% of those who had a formal interview expected a quicker process against 13% of those who had an informal chat. These are people who did get through, so the figures understate the problem. The closest analogue to fostering, a vetted and months-long route into a helping role run by a public body, is special constable recruitment, where a qualitative study of 63 applicants found people waiting months with no feedback, having "to keep prompting for updates", and in one case taking up a different volunteer role during the wait ([Birmingham City University open access copy](https://www.open-access.bcu.ac.uk/15897/1/Improving_the_Application_and_Assessment_Experiences_of_Special_Constable_Candidates_in_England_and_Wales_-_Accepted%281%29.pdf)). No study found times a reply to a volunteer enquiry and follows who goes on to volunteer.

**Observed practice (D; England, 2023).** NHS Employers independently recommends the same components as the OUCM draft for people waiting to start a job: a single named contact, an agreed rhythm of contact and stated timescales; it publishes no effect sizes ([NHS Employers, March 2023](https://www.nhsemployers.org/articles/reducing-delay-between-job-offer-and-start-date)).

**Recommendation (OUCM).** Confirm the 1-working-day rule and tighten its wording. One description of the rule is "a person makes first contact"; the project's earlier evidence report describes it as a "first human attempt". These are different clocks. The standard should define the rule as a first attempt by a named person within 1 working day, keep the separate limit for a conversation actually held, and measure both from interaction records. The practitioner finding supports saying something about the quality of the first conversation, assessed across the whole service and never by scoring individual staff.

## Reminders buy a few percentage points, and the best-known wins shrink or vanish at scale

### Where reminders help, and by how much

**Evidence (A; international, 2013 and 2016).** Text reminders reliably raise attendance when nothing was being sent before. The Cochrane review (8 trials, 6,615 people) reports attendance of **67.8% with no reminder and 78.6% with a text**, about 10.8 pp (the note-taker's arithmetic), with texts doing as well as phone calls (risk ratio 0.99) at 55% to 65% lower cost per attendance in two studies ([Cochrane review, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24310741/)). A later meta-analysis found no-shows of **15% against 21%**, about 6 pp ([Robotham et al., 2016](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/6/10/e012116.full)). Studies sending two or more reminders did better than those sending one, though the paper gives two different figures for how much (see the check list).

**Evidence (A; USA, 2020 to 2025).** Court reminders show the same pattern and the same dependence on the starting point. New York texts cut missed dates by about **8 pp from 37.9%**; the arm that only helped people plan, with no mention of consequences, still achieved **6.0 pp** ([Fishbane et al., 2020](https://psych.wustl.edu/files/psych/fishbane20.pdf)). A county court trial of 31,372 people cut missed dates by **8.3 to 8.6 pp from about 21%** with texts that included practical help such as how to rearrange and free childcare ([Emanuel and Ho, 2024](https://nataliaemanuel.github.io/ne_website/Emanuel_Ho_FTA.pdf)). In Santa Clara County, where the starting rate was low, warrants at the first date fell from **12.1% to 9.7%**, about 2.5 pp ([Chohlas-Wood et al., 2025](https://www.5harad.com/papers/court-reminders.pdf)). The plan-only arm is the relevant one for fostering because it worked without any threat.

**Evidence (A; England, 2015).** Where a reminder already exists, rewording buys less. At Barts Health in London the best wording cut missed appointments from **11.1% to 8.4%**, about 2.7 pp ([Hallsworth et al., 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0137306)).

**Evidence (A and D; Israel and England).** Clearer messages produce more advance cancellations: from 17.2% to about 26 to 27% in the Israeli trial, and from 8.8% to 10.5% in one Barts arm. For fostering, a person who says "not now" is better served than one who drifts away, so this is a good outcome. NHS England's guidance treats two-way reply, out-of-hours phone lines and choice of time as a minimum standard ([NHS England, Reducing did not attends](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/reducing-did-not-attends-dnas-in-outpatient-services/)). The same page's claim that reminders cut missed appointments "by up to 80%" is a best-case supplier case study and must not be quoted as a typical effect.

### Nulls, backfires and shrinkage deserve the same weight

**Evidence (A; USA, 2022).** Across all 126 trials run by two US government nudge units, covering over 23 million people, the average effect was **1.4 pp**; published academic nudge trials averaged **8.7 pp**. Selective publication, made worse by small studies, can account for the whole gap ([DellaVigna and Linos, 2022](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27594)). These trials are mostly letters, emails and texts from public bodies, which is what a fostering service sends, so 1.4 pp is the most defensible single planning figure. It covers all kinds of nudge, not reminders alone. A re-analysis of a widely cited meta-analysis found that the average nudge effect falls from d = 0.43 to d = 0.04 after correcting for publication bias, with reminders and similar help at 0.01, while real variation between interventions remains ([Maier et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9351501)).

**Evidence (A; USA, 2019 to 2021).** The clearest worked example is summer text messages to school leavers heading for college. Local trials reported gains of about 4 to 7 pp in some sites and nothing in Boston (site figures are secondhand, from a [NEBHE summary](https://www.nebhe.org/post/can-text-messages-mitigate-summer-melt)). When national and state bodies sent the same approach to more than 800,000 students, enrolment changed by **+0.3 pp (standard error 0.2)**, effects above 0.5 pp were ruled out, and no subgroup benefited. Framing, channel, timing and layout made no difference. Only 11.6% took up an offer of one-to-one advice by text. The authors' untested explanation is that the earlier messages came from local partners the students knew ([Bird et al., 2019](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26158.pdf)). That is encouraging for a local fostering team and a warning against expecting a national or mass-mail layer to do the same job.

**Evidence (A; USA, 2023).** In a pragmatic trial covering about 88,500 appointments at a US veterans' service, four nudge versions of the reminder letter did nothing: odds ratios for missing were 1.14 in primary care (baseline 11.9%) and 1.20 in mental health (baseline 18.0%), neither significant and both in the wrong direction. The authors concluded that more intensive support, not message changes, is needed ([Teo et al., 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11606-023-08131-5)).

**Evidence (A; various).** Other nulls and reversals sit alongside the wins. Text reminders showed no clear effect in mental health services in three small pooled trials ([Robotham et al., 2016](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/6/10/e012116.full)). At Barts, putting the cancellation number in the text and a social-norm line did not reduce missed appointments, and an empathy appeal did worse than the best message (10.7% against 8.2%). Staffed two-way texting added nothing over one-way texts in the county court trial. Phone reminders before a first mental health appointment helped in one trial (83% against 66%), made no difference in another and went with lower attendance in a third (48% against 76%). Homeless defendants in Shasta County gained nothing from texts, from a baseline miss rate of about 86.5%. A less punitive school absence notice helped by only 0.07 days in a month across 131,312 families ([Lasky-Fink et al., 2021](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/using-behavioral-insights-improve-school-administrative-communications-case-truancy-0)).

**Evidence (B; England, 2010).** One design choice backfires in a way attendance figures hide. When a Sheffield NHS psychotherapy service required people to opt in before being given a slot, non-attendance among those booked fell from 20% to 15% (not significant), but the share of all referrals who attended a first appointment fell from **80% to 65%** because one in five never opted in; people with anxiety were about twice as likely not to reach the service ([Houghton et al., 2010](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-psychiatrist/article/effects-of-optin-letters-in-a-national-health-service-psychotherapy-service/ED4A36AF22253CE707B7F80258950D68)). Small opt-in trials elsewhere gave mixed results. In fostering the equivalent is "reply to confirm or we will close your enquiry".

**Evidence (B and D).** Self-booking is liked and reasonable to offer, but the evidence that it raises attendance is weak and mostly uncontrolled, and it suits the digitally confident ([Zhao et al., 2017](https://www.jmir.org/2017/4/e134)). No randomised trial compares self-booked with allocated first appointments, and no head-to-head trial of reminder timing was found.

**Recommendation (OUCM).** Add a simple reminder rule for booked events (a confirmation at booking, a reminder about a week ahead and one the day before, in the person's chosen channel, each with date, time, place, how long it lasts and a one-step way to rearrange or say "not now"). Present the expected gain as "a few percentage points at most, and possibly nothing where personal follow-up is already good". Each service should measure its own before and after and not assume a published effect.

## After a missed event, the evidence backs an open door more than any deadline

**Evidence (none found).** No randomised or quasi-experimental study was found of a personal call, a second offer or a text rebooking link after a missed first appointment. The New York court study randomised follow-up texts after a missed date but published no results. This is the largest gap behind any of the three rules. The project's earlier evidence report gives the basis of the 2-working-day rule as "Erie County via Sebba"; that source is not in the notes for this report and was not re-checked here.

**Observed practice (D; England).** NICE guidance for a much higher-risk group says follow-up after a missed appointment "should happen promptly and actively, especially after the first missed appointment", by phone, text or visit, including outside routine hours, and that services should not automatically discharge people ([NICE QS188, statement 4](https://nice.org.uk/guidance/qs188/chapter/quality-statement-4-follow-up-after-any-missed-appointment); publication date not shown on the page read). Its 24-hour timescale applies where there are concerns about risk and cannot justify a number of days for someone enquiring about fostering. NHS England says people should be reviewed and contacted before any discharge ([NHS England, Reducing did not attends](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/reducing-did-not-attends-dnas-in-outpatient-services/)).

**Evidence (C; UK, 2026).** Co-design work with people who repeatedly miss care (61 interviews and a 16-member advisory group) supports follow-up framed as support and not reprimand, personal two-way contact, flexible times and a named coordinator, and identifies having to be re-referred after a miss as a barrier in itself. The package has not yet been tested in practice ([Lindsay et al., 2026](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/389588/3/389588.pdf)). Interviews with clinical psychologists describe "write to us within two weeks or we close your case" letters, some people discharged before any contact, and staff reading a miss as lack of "readiness" ([Pickersgill, 2026](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/85D9947C21C9CC72287C29DF6027EC8E/core-reader)). In a very small London audit, reasons for missing included not knowing about the appointment, forgetting, a family crisis, illness and not being able to get through to the service ([LSBU open research copy](https://openresearch.lsbu.ac.uk/download/81c9ad811935e163b2676e96c1a1645bbd65818ab183b59562155fa0639c6412/776431/non%20attendance%20at%20psychological%20therapy%20appointments%20proof.pdf)).

**Evidence (A, lab; USA, 2020).** People wrongly assume a missed obligation is deliberate. In online experiments, support for reminders rose from 63% to 82% once people were prompted to think of a miss as a possible mistake ([Fishbane et al., 2020](https://psych.wustl.edu/files/psych/fishbane20.pdf)). In the county court trial the authors concluded that a large share of missed dates are mistakes ([Emanuel and Ho, 2024](https://nataliaemanuel.github.io/ne_website/Emanuel_Ho_FTA.pdf)).

**Evidence (A; USA, 2023).** Tone has trial support in a voluntary setting. In two field experiments covering 117,073 renters, outreach that told people their situation was not their fault raised requests for rental assistance by about 18% over information alone (a relative change; the baseline rate was not in the notes) ([Lasky-Fink and Linos, 2023](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/improving-delivery-social-safety-net-role-stigma-0)). The outcome was requests for help, not attendance.

**Recommendation (OUCM).** Confirm the call within 2 working days as a commitment consistent with "promptly and actively", and tighten what surrounds it. The call should assume a slip or a clash, not bad faith. If there is no answer, the service leaves a short personal voicemail and sends a message in the person's chosen channel with three equal options: another date, a one-to-one conversation, or "not for now". A first miss is treated as normal and never closes an enquiry. No service asks a person to confirm their interest as a condition of staying on its records. If the person shares what got in the way, it is recorded as a code, so the service can fix venues, times and wording. "Not now" is recorded as a good outcome.

## Who is not reached, and by what

**Evidence (A).** Texts and emails reach only people whose details are right and who have a stable phone. Only about 11% of New York summons recipients gave a number; those who did were already more likely to attend, and people in neighbourhoods with more Black and Hispanic residents were less likely to give one ([Fishbane et al., 2020](https://psych.wustl.edu/files/psych/fishbane20.pdf)). The Barts authors describe the patients they could reach as a minority who may differ from the rest ([Hallsworth et al., 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0137306)). Homeless defendants gained nothing. Among people who were reached, poorer New York neighbourhoods gained more (12.5 pp against 6.4 pp; exploratory). The pattern is two-sided: reminders narrow gaps among people who can be reached and widen the gap between those who can and cannot.

**Evidence (B; Scotland, 2017 and 2019).** Missing is patterned by circumstance. Among 550,083 Scottish general practice patients, repeated missing was 2.27 times as likely in the most deprived group and somewhat more likely at ages 16 to 30 ([Ellis et al., 2017](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/88220)). The linked finding that repeated missing goes with much higher mortality among people with mental health conditions ([McQueenie et al., 2019](https://doaj.org/article/8726f50dfd414d0e89238a59bd35dfdd)) is not relevant to fostering and should not appear in OUCM public writing as if it were; only the framing point carries over.

**Evidence (B; England).** In talking therapies, people in the least deprived fifth were more likely to attend assessment (odds ratio 1.51) ([Saxon et al., 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9903253/)), and several minority ethnic groups were less likely to (for example odds ratio 0.80 for Black against White patients) ([Sweetman et al., 2022](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/192581/7/Risk_factors_for_initial_appointment_non_attendance_at_Improving_Access_to_Psychological_Therapy_IAPT_services_A_retrospective_analysis.pdf)). Opt-in steps lose people with anxiety. In volunteering, younger adults are the least tolerant of delay, and in 2019, 34% of minority ethnic volunteers (base 177) agreed there was too much bureaucracy against 24% overall ([NCVO, 2019](https://www.ncvo.org.uk/news-and-insights/news-index/time-well-spent-national-survey-volunteer-experience/volunteer-experience/)).

**Evidence (C and D).** Reminders do not remove practical barriers such as transport, childcare, work clashes, limited hours or mistrust of services ([Lindsay et al., 2026](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/389588/3/389588.pdf); [NHS England](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/reducing-did-not-attends-dnas-in-outpatient-services/)). About 22% of Santa Clara clients needed Spanish or Vietnamese messages, which were provided ([Chohlas-Wood et al., 2025](https://www.5harad.com/papers/court-reminders.pdf)). No trial read reported reminder effects by deprivation, ethnicity, language or disability, and no effect sizes exist for translated reminders.

**Recommendation (OUCM).** Fix the main page, form and first message before adding text reminders, because those reach everyone. Offer at least one route that needs no smartphone. Treat a bounced or undelivered message as the service's task to resolve through another channel. Let people rearrange outside office hours without phoning. Provide plain English and the languages the local population needs. Health services' tools for predicting who will miss must not be borrowed: nothing in OUCM scores, ranks or predicts people who enquire. A reply to confirm attendance can prompt a courtesy call to someone who has not replied, but it must never be used to rate a person or decide who is worth following up; in Santa Clara the gap between people who confirmed and those who did not (2.9% against 16.8% warrants) was self-selection, not an effect.

## High-performing techniques OUCM should not adopt

Almost every trial in this literature counts attendance as success. OUCM's aim is a well-informed decision and a person who feels respected, so a message that raises attendance by making someone feel pushed is a failure against the guiding sentence. None of the trials measured how recipients felt. A fostering service also holds power over approval, and techniques that are mild between equals feel different from a body that will later assess you.

| Technique | What it achieved | Why OUCM should not use it |
|---|---|---|
| Guilt wording | **Evidence (A).** Missed appointments 14.2% against 21.1% in Israel ([Berliner Senderey et al., 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0234817)) | Works by making the person feel bad. A person offering to care for a child would be treated as a problem to manage. |
| Stating what a missed slot costs the service | **Evidence (A).** 8.4% against 11.1% at Barts ([Hallsworth et al., 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0137306)) | A pressure message suited, at most, to a clinic slot someone else could use. It has no place in a voluntary offer. |
| Stating a penalty or consequence | **Evidence (A).** 8.9 pp against 6.0 pp for planning alone in New York ([Fishbane et al., 2020](https://psych.wustl.edu/files/psych/fishbane20.pdf)) | There is no penalty for missing a fostering session, and implying one would be manipulative. The non-threatening message kept about two thirds of the effect. |
| "Your absence will be recorded" | **Evidence (A).** 9.6%, no better than the best message at Barts | A veiled threat, and it did not work. |
| Reciprocity: "I've done this for you" to create a debt | **Evidence (A).** 26.8% against 10.5% in Bedford; the authors name reciprocity as the mechanism ([Sanders and Kirkman, 2019](https://journal-bpa.org/index.php/jbpa/article/download/24/36/463)) | Felt obligation is not informed choice. | Social-norm lines ("9 out of 10 attend") | **Evidence (A).** No effect on missed appointments at Barts; 3.3 pp in Israel | Inconsistent, and it works through pressure to conform. |

| Written commitments, attitude or "strengths" exercises | **Evidence (A, bundled, team's own report; D critique).** Reported as 15 to 20% more people off benefits at 13 weeks in the Loughton pilot ([Behavioural Insights Team](https://www.bi.team/?p=5287)); criticised as coercive under sanctions ([Friedli and Stearn, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4484497)) | Messages must never test, score or steer a person's attitude. Practical journey details are help; a commitment device is not. |
| Predicting who will miss | **Observed practice (D).** Used in parts of the health sector | Breaks OUCM's rule against scoring, ranking or predicting people. |

**Recommendation (OUCM), and a disagreement to record.** The project's earlier evidence report lists messages that say "I have done something for you" among the "strongest transferable mechanisms" and cites reciprocity as a basis for the contact rhythm. This report disagrees with that framing. The named sender and the concrete next step should stay because they are honest and courteous. Reciprocity as a design goal should come out of the standard's stated basis, because building a sense of debt into a message to a person weighing a life decision would harm trust with carers and with the sector if it were noticed.

## Gaps

No study tests acknowledgement speed, and none tests whether an instant automated reply feels cold. No trial tests any follow-up after a missed first appointment, any follow-up window, or one-miss closure against a more tolerant policy. No head-to-head trial compares reminder timings, self-booked with allocated slots, or evening and weekend availability. No trial reports reminder effects by deprivation, ethnicity, language or disability. No trial measures trust, felt respect or felt pressure as an outcome, or follows people who attended after a reminder and later withdrew, so whether reminders bring in people who were not ready is unknown. No evidence was found on how many reminders become unwelcome. Nothing citable was found on Samaritans, Girlguiding or school governor enquiries, on Get Into Teaching adviser outcomes, on DWP appointment reminders, or on screening and vaccination reminders. The 2024 "missingness" realist review, current NHS Talking Therapies national figures and the full texts of Sanders et al. (2021), Castleman and Page (2015) and Mertens et al. (2022) were not read. All of the evidence is from fields other than fostering.

## Questions OUCM could test openly

Each of these could be pre-registered, run with a willing service or hub, and published whatever the result, with the method published first and services seeing their own results privately before anything is made public. None involves scoring or predicting people, and none uses pressure wording in any arm.

1. Does an acknowledgement that names the person who will call and says when, against a generic one, change the share of people who have a first conversation and their own rating of "I knew what would happen next"?
2. Does a first attempt on the same working day against the next working day change anything people notice or value?
3. After a missed session, does a call plus a three-option message, against a letter alone, change how many people rebook, how many say "not now", and how many simply go quiet?
4. Do one, two or three reminders differ in attendance, rearrangement and opt-outs, and at what point do people find them unwelcome?
5. Does a choice of session times, including evenings and weekends, change who attends, analysed with consent by group?
6. What share of acknowledgements bounce or go undelivered, and how many people are reached through a second channel?
7. Do people who say "not now" and are contacted on the date they chose feel respected, and do some return?

## Conclusion

The adjacent evidence changes the argument for OUCM's rules more than it changes the rules. Speed limits that looked like evidence-based thresholds turn out to be reasonable promises that nobody has tested; the well-evidenced parts are plain content, a known local sender, working contact details and an open door after a miss. The techniques with the largest published effects are the ones a project built on respect has to refuse, and the scale-up evidence means little is lost by refusing them: the realistic prize from any message was always a few percentage points.

That leaves fostering services with an advantage the health service lacks. They hear from few enough people to do the personal follow-up that hospital guidance recommends and cannot afford. The standard's event records also make it possible to measure what no trial in this literature measured: whether a person who offered to care for a child got a clear reply, knew what would happen next, and felt free to say no. Testing those questions openly would fill gaps that matter well beyond fostering.
