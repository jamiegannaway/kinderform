# Fostering and adoption intake interactions: what the evidence says works, and a refined Kinderform open standard

> **Status:** desk review, version 1, 14 November 2026. An AI-assisted research tool produced it. Most figures have **not yet been checked by a person** against the original sources, so check the source before you quote anything. Everything we propose is for discussion and is not yet part of the Kinderform standard. See [how we made this review](#how-we-made-this-review).

## Summary

**What we looked at.** This review looks at what happens after someone first gets in touch to ask about fostering or adoption. We call that first contact an enquiry. We call the contacts that follow, up to a formal application, "intake interactions". These contacts may come from a person or from a digital tool. A service is a council, hub or agency that finds and approves foster carers or adopters.

The review covers first replies, calls, follow-ups, information events, home visits and contact with experienced carers and adopters. It also covers hand-offs between teams, digital and AI tools, and conversations when someone withdraws. We focus on England, with shorter notes on Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and other countries. We then propose changes to the open standard that Kinderform, the project behind kinderform.org, is drafting.

**What we found.**

1. **The best-evidenced approach is to be responsive and build a relationship, not to perfect a script for the first call.** That means:
   - a prompt, warm first reply from a person
   - one named person who keeps in touch
   - early contact with experienced carers or adopters
   - honest information about the process
2. **Speed matters most for the very first reply. After that, keeping in touch matters more.** People hoping to foster or adopt describe the first enquiry as the moment that decides whether they feel welcome. Not being followed up is a documented reason for dropping out. In one Australian study, a quarter of people who enquired and did not go on never got a follow-up.
3. **Speed on its own is not enough.** England's hubs are groups of councils that recruit foster carers together. They became much faster at initial visits, the first home visit from a social worker. But in a controlled comparison, they did not lead to more approvals than areas without hubs.
   - Foster with North East is a "pathfinder" hub, set up early to test the approach. Its share of initial visits held within 5 days rose from 29% to 50%. Yet its controlled evaluation found no measurable gain in applications or approvals.
4. **The weak points now are hand-offs, gaps in contact during assessment, and expectations that do not match reality.** A hand-off is when one team passes a person on to another.
5. **The approaches most likely to work here are personal and two-way.** They include a named worker, follow-up calls, "a safe space to ask questions", and contact with other carers and adopters. They also include messages that say "I have done something for you".
   - In a Behavioural Insights Team (BIT) trial, text messages from a named adviser raised attendance from 10.5% to 26.8%. The messages mentioned an appointment booked for the person.
   - The Department for Education (DfE) ran its own trial, with Kantar. Presenting fostering and adoption as linked options (a "continuum") raised people's stated intent, and so did offering a "safe space". That trial measured intentions, not what people actually did.

**How sure we are.**

- We found no randomised or quasi-experimental study of any single step after an enquiry in fostering or adoption. These would be grade A, our strongest grade (see [how we grade the evidence](#how-we-grade-the-evidence)).
- The strongest controlled study looked at a whole hub model, not single steps, and found no gain in approvals.
- The only other grade A evidence comes from tests of message wording, which measured intentions, and from fields such as jobcentres and sales.
- Several widely quoted figures on drop-out come from studies from 1990 to 2008.
- A person checked a few citations by hand on 14 November 2026. Most figures have not yet been checked by a person against the original source.

**What we suggest.** The Kinderform draft standard should keep its rules on fast first replies, and add rules on continuity, hand-offs and honesty:

- Keep the acknowledgement within 5 minutes, and a first attempt by a person to make contact within 1 working day.
- Make this rule mandatory: "warm hand-off within 2 working days, first contact from the receiving team within 5".
- Add a named "journey guide": one member of staff who stays with each person from enquiry to approval.
- Add a regular "keeping in touch" rhythm during assessment.
- Offer contact with an experienced carer or adopter at the first conversation.
- Record every contact as an `Interaction` event, so services can measure what they cannot measure now.
- Then run an open trial of the follow-up and hand-off rules, with its plan published before it starts. We suggest this because there is no randomised evidence on intake interactions in fostering or adoption.

## How we grade the evidence

We give each piece of evidence a grade:

- **A:** randomised or quasi-experimental. In a randomised trial, chance decides who gets the new approach. A quasi-experimental study compares similar groups that were not chosen by chance.
- **B:** large observational studies, or administrative data (the records services keep anyway)
- **C:** qualitative, such as interviews, focus groups and case studies
- **D:** practice guidance or opinion

We also label each claim:

- **Evidence:** what studies and data show
- **Observed practice:** what services and suppliers do, or say they do, in their own documents
- **Recommendation (Kinderform):** what we propose

## Many people stop before approval, and fewer are coming forward to adopt

In fostering, plenty of people enquire, but many stop at each step on the way to approval. In adoption, fewer people are coming forward as well. So adoption services have to help people keep going, and avoid putting off the shrinking number who come forward.

### Fostering in England, 2024–25: almost 150,000 enquiries, but 7,365 new foster carers (Evidence, B)

The Department for Education's plan, *Renewing Fostering: homes for 10,000 more children* (February 2026), says "Interest in fostering is not the problem".

- In 2024–25 there were almost 150,000 enquiries (149,795, on Community Care's reading of the data). But only 7,365 foster carers were newly approved.
- The department notes that enquiry counts are "likely to include multiple enquiries from individuals, and… variations in how fostering services define enquiries."
- It says people who express an interest wait "weeks or months for a reply".
- In councils, 59% of fostering assessments take over 6 months.
- The consultation published alongside the plan estimates the effect of delay. A delay of 1 week for each household adds up to 4,430 weeks of fostering capacity put back, in total.

Ofsted inspects children's services in England and publishes official statistics on fostering. Its *Fostering in England 2024–25* shows (Evidence, B):

- Services received 8,290 applications, and 5,370 were completed by 31 March 2025. *(Maintainer check, 14 November 2026: both figures match Ofsted's main findings, published 26 November 2025.)*
- Of the completed applications, 2,050 were approved, 3,290 withdrawn and 30 rejected.
- Applicants withdrew 2,175 applications and services withdrew 1,115.

**A correction to our earlier notes.** The "61% withdrawal" figure is 61% of *completed* applications (3,290 of 5,370), not of all applications received.

**Reasons applicants gave for withdrawing:**

| Reason | Share |
|---|---|
| Change in household circumstances (not financial) | 36% |
| The process was not as expected | 20% |

Ofsted's main findings for 2024–25 also record "other or unknown reason" at 35%. This is a catch-all category, not a sign that applicants gave no reason.

**Earlier figures:**

- Ipsos, for the Department for Education (2023), reports that only 6% of enquiries reached application in 2021–22.
- In 2023–24, Ofsted recorded 8,485 applications, 1,815 approvals, 3,670 withdrawals and 50 rejections.

End-to-end hubs take people from enquiry through assessment to approval, not just the first steps. The Department for Education's own booklet on these hubs (March 2026) says:

> "Only 14% of initial visits currently result in approval, with prospective carers often disengaging during lengthy assessments."

### Adoption in England: fewer people are registering, and fewer are approved (Evidence, B)

The Coram Institute and Adoption England published *Every Day is Everything* in September 2026, covering 2021/22 to 2025/26:

- Adopter registrations fell 15%, from 3,740 in 2021/22 to 3,180 in 2025/26. The figure includes all agencies, including voluntary adoption agencies (charities that approve adopters).
- Approvals fell 25%, from about 2,990 to 2,240 (figures rounded to the nearest 10).
- At 31 March 2026 there were 3,010 children and 1,400 approved families: 1,610 more children than families.
- Sufficiency means how far the number of adopters meets the need. If registrations fall a further 15%, it could drop from 41% to about 13% of need by 2030.

Coram also publishes headline measures for the Adoption and Special Guardianship Leadership Board. For the third quarter of 2025/26, they show only 25% of adoptive families were approved within 6 months of registration.

## What happens after someone enquires, and where people are lost

### Fostering in England: 7 steps from enquiry to assessment (observed practice, with evidence on timing)

The main early loss is that services cannot reach the person who enquired. In Foster with North East, the main sticking point later on was the hand-off from the hub to the council.

Assessment has 2 stages: Stage 1 is mainly checks and references, and Stage 2 is the full assessment of the household. An independent agency is a charity or company that finds and supports foster carers.

| Step | What happens | Who | How | Usual timing (where published) | Where people are lost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Enquiry received | Automatic acknowledgement; recorded in a contact database or case system | Hub (over 70% of councils by October 2026, per the Department for Education), council recruitment team, or independent agency | Web form, phone, email, events | Immediate to same day | Not reaching the person who enquired is the main early loss |
| 1. First call ("initial conversation") | Screening call: why they want to foster, spare room, household, work, consent for checks; correcting common myths; next steps | Recruitment officer or adviser; in Foster with North East, a hub fostering social worker | Phone (most common); some evenings and weekends | Ipsos case-study services reported 1–2 working days; Doncaster procedure: next working day | Essex analysis: most "no further action" closures happen soon after first contact, often because the service cannot reach the person |
| 2. Information | Information pack (often digital); common questions and answers; offer of an information session or a "speak to a carer" | Recruitment team | Email or post; online sessions and drop-ins | Packs within 5 working days in several procedures (Redbridge, Nottinghamshire); Doncaster makes a courtesy call 5 days after the pack | Silence after the pack; Redbridge sends a follow-up letter at 2 weeks |
| 3. Contact with an experienced carer (optional) | Buddy or mentor; carer at events | Experienced carer | Phone, in person | Derby (Foster for East Midlands): mentor offered on the first call; Department for Education end-to-end model: buddy "from the point of completion of enquiry" | How many people take it up varies by council (Foster with North East) |
| 4. Expression of interest and initial home visit | Expression of interest form; home visit to explore suitability and "right time" | Social worker (led by the hub, the council, or both) | In person or online | Common standard is within 5 working days of request (Nottinghamshire, Doncaster, Wales); Foster with North East: 50% within 5 days in 2025 | Some see the visit as "invasive"; "not the right time" |
| 5. Hand-off to the assessing service | Transfer from hub to council; a social worker is allocated | Hub → council | Email or system, plus a call | Foster with North East evaluation recommends first council contact within 5 working days | Main sticking point in Foster with North East: communication "ceased or diminished" |
| 6. Application → Stage 1 and Stage 2 | Application form; checks; Skills to Foster (preparation training); "keeping in touch" calls | Assessing social worker; hub | Mixed | Department for Education target: initial visit to approval under 200 days / 6 months | 61% of completed applications withdrawn; 20% "process not as expected" |

**Enquiries from relatives and friends need a separate route.** Some enquiries come from a relative or friend who wants to care for a particular child. These are kinship or connected-persons enquiries. They arrive the same way as other enquiries, but follow a different legal route, with an urgent assessment linked to that child. Foster with North East's training groups included kinship carers alongside other applicants. The Department for Education's end-to-end booklet has a separate section on kinship.

**Recommendation (Kinderform):** add a kinship flag at the first conversation. It should send the enquiry to the kinship or connected-persons team within 1 working day. Do not put it through the follow-up message sequence.

### Adoption in England has formal steps, with time limits in statutory guidance

Statutory guidance is guidance from government that agencies must follow, and in adoption it sets time limits for several steps. Most enquiries go to regional adoption agencies, where councils work together on adoption.

A registration of interest is the formal request that starts the adoption process. Early Permanence is a route where a child lives with carers approved as both foster carers and adopters. The carers may go on to adopt the child.

| Step | What happens | Who | Time limit or usual timing | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Enquiry | Acknowledgement; first call | Regional adoption agency (most), voluntary adoption agency, a few councils | Statutory guidance: detailed information within **10 working days** of a request | Many regional adoption agencies require an information event before a registration of interest |
| 1. Information event | Usually online, often required before a registration of interest | Regional adoption agency team, with adopters as speakers | Held regularly | Adopt South 2025/26: 653 enquiries → 428 households at online information events → 189 registrations of interest → 171 into assessment → 86 approved |
| 2. First visit or conversation | Explores readiness and interest in Early Permanence | Social worker | Varies | Adopt South: 64% of approved adopters wished to be considered for Early Permanence |
| 3. Registration of interest | Registration of interest form | Regional adoption agency | Decision to accept within **5 working days** | Adopt South reports being the only regional adoption agency meeting the 5-day timescale from enquiry to registration of interest |
| 4. Stage 1 (pre-assessment) | Checks, references, preparation training | Regional adoption agency | **2 months** | Fast track (approved foster carers, previous adopters): decision within **4 months** of registration of interest |
| 5. Stage 2 (assessment) | Home study | Social worker | **4 months** | Adoption UK Barometer 2026 (England, 2,445 of 3,078 UK respondents): only 39% said approvals ran "smoothly with no delays", and 61% reported delays. More than half found the process so challenging they considered dropping out. 87% said their social worker understood and supported them |

The Adoption Barometer is an annual survey by Adoption UK.

**How adoption differs from fostering:**

- The steps are formal: the registration of interest, and time limits set in statutory guidance.
- Information events are often a gateway: people attend one before they can go further.
- Adopters actively choose between agencies. The Wales Barometer reports that 92% knew they had a choice. How promptly the agency replied, and the quality of the information event, were among the reasons for that choice.
- Fear of rejection is very strong, especially after infertility, and among single and same-sex applicants (Kantar 2022).
- Adopting from another country is a separate, specialist route, and services should point people elsewhere for it.

### Wales publishes reply times; we found none for Scotland or Northern Ireland

**Wales.** Foster Wales is a national network of the 22 council fostering services.

- Foster Wales Bridgend promises contact "within one working day". It aims to complete an initial visit and pre-assessment screening within 5 working days.
- Bridgend, Merthyr Tydfil and Rhondda Cynon Taf share a regional "front door", where people get in touch. It sets a follow-up call within 24 hours and an initial visit within 5 working days.
- The AFA Cymru *National Fostering Framework Good Practice Guide* tells staff to "always offer prospective applicants a home visit within five working days".
- The Adoption Barometer shows that people hoping to adopt in Wales report more positively than the UK average. 44% said their approval ran smoothly, against 39% across the UK, and 91% said their social worker was supportive.

**Scotland and Northern Ireland.** We found no published standards for replying to enquiries that we could compare. The Scotland Barometer reports that "recommendations from others with experience of adopting" (21%) was the most-reported influence on choice of agency. This is a gap Kinderform could fill through voluntary data sharing.

### Speed matters most for the first reply, but speed alone did not raise approvals

**Recommendation (Kinderform):** keep the fast first reply. It is cheap, and Essex's data shows that people the service cannot reach are the largest early loss. But do not promise that speed alone will mean more approvals.

**Evidence:**

- Delay and lack of follow-up clearly cause people to drop out (rows 8, 10 and 11).
- The Department for Education now names slow replies as a national failure.
- We do not know how big the effect of speed is within fostering.
- The best test so far (row 1) found big gains in speed at the initial-visit stage, but no measurable difference in approvals.
- The Harvard Business Review figures show the chances of success falling fast with delay (row 15). They come from commercial sales, where buyers compare vendors within hours.
- In fostering, people often think about it for years: Ipsos cites 2–5 years from first thinking about it to approval.

**What this means.** The evidence on speed applies most strongly to 2 moments:

- making contact while the person is still emotionally "open" and reachable
- not losing them to another agency, which Ipsos documents

It applies only weakly to the later decision to apply, which depends on whether the household is ready. Changes in household circumstances account for 36% of withdrawals by applicants.

### The tone of the first call matters, especially to people who fear being judged

**Recommendation (Kinderform):** a first call that has a structure but feels like a conversation. It should cover:

1. welcome and thanks
2. their questions first ("there are no wrong questions; asking doesn't count against you")
3. 2 or 3 myths about who can foster or adopt that apply to them
4. an honest picture of the time the role takes, with examples, plus the support on offer
5. where relevant, the range of options: fostering, adoption and other roles (the continuum)
6. an agreed next step with a date

**Evidence (C):**

- Kantar found that a "cold, curt, critical, or overly intrusive" first call makes people doubt themselves.
- People who do not "fit the mould" fear judgement most. These include minority ethnic, single and same-sex applicants, and people with difficult histories.
- Effective agencies help people see past difficulties as strengths.
- Ipsos describes the tension between screening ("so we don't waste our time and their time") and going "at the enquirer's pace".

**Who should make the call?** We found no evidence comparing social workers with advisers. Foster with North East uses hub fostering social workers for the screening conversation. AdoptUSKids' checklist asks whether the person who replies is "family-oriented" and "trained to address expectations and fears families typically present".

**Give honest information before people commit.** Researchers call this a "realistic job preview", and there are no fostering trials of it.

- Ipsos participants argued that honest information early would let people "opt themselves out early".
- The Fostering Network and the Centre for Evidence and Implementation found that people withdraw late, once the reality hits them.
- Kantar's statistical analysis of what drives confidence found that people feel more confident when they believe support will be there. So honesty should come with support ("challenge and support").
- Messages that stressed only the challenge weakened the intent to foster when people saw them a second time.

### Offer a choice of information events, and call people who miss one

**Recommendation (Kinderform):**

- Offer events both online and in person.
- Do not make an event something people must attend before they can foster.
- Always make a "missed event" recovery call within 2 working days.

**Observed practice and evidence:**

- Adoption agencies often use online information events as a gateway. Adopt South's published figures show that about 66% of people who enquired attended. About 44% of those who attended went on to send a registration of interest.
- Sebba reports a finding from Erie County. Staff phoned people within 3 days of a missed information meeting and offered a one-to-one meeting. This brought in people who would otherwise not have gone on, though Sebba gives no effect size.
- Foster with North East found that digital and travel requirements put some participants off training and buddying, and recommends non-digital options.

### Contact with experienced carers and adopters looks helpful, but has not been tested

**Recommendation (Kinderform):** offer peer contact at the first conversation. Peer contact means contact with an experienced carer or adopter. Give it a clear purpose, for example a 30-minute "what the first year is really like" call. Record whether it was offered, accepted and held.

**Evidence:**

- Word of mouth produces the strongest enquiries (Ipsos, C).
- Knowing other foster families is the most common route in (Rodger et al., B).
- Foster with North East's buddy scheme "gave honest, practical insight", but how many took it up varied (C).
- The Centre for Excellence in Child and Family Welfare in Australia published a rapid review in 2024. It notes that "some studies" show buddies help during accreditation, the Australian approval process (D).
- There is no trial evidence, and a practitioner's critique warns against relying on buddies as a way to teach people (D).

### People value one named contact who keeps in touch

**Recommendation (Kinderform):** a named journey guide. Send a "what happens next" timeline after every change of stage.

**Evidence:**

- Ipsos's earlier work on Foster with North East, and the Year 3 report, show that people value continuity and "keeping in touch" calls (C).
- The Department for Education now *requires* end-to-end hubs to provide a "dedicated consistent 'journey guide' from enquiry to approval". This is a named person, aimed at the points where most people drop out (D, policy).
- In adoption, Barometer respondents describe periods of "next to no contact" between their Stage 1 and Stage 2 workers (C).

### When people say "not now", let them choose when to hear from you again

This review calls a series of follow-up messages to people who are not ready yet "nurture".

**Recommendation (Kinderform):** let the person choose when to be contacted again, and send fewer, more relevant messages. See [what we propose for the Kinderform standard](#what-we-propose-for-the-kinderform-standard).

**Evidence:**

- Essex looked at people it had contacted whose enquiries were later closed. Over half said it "wasn't the right time" (B, one council).
- Kantar recommends keeping people "warm until circumstances change". It also suggests offering other roles, such as respite care (short breaks) or mentoring (C).
- If no visit is booked, Derby's procedure keeps in "regular contact over a period of one month, via email, text and phone calls".
- There is no evidence on how often to send messages. Some companies sell "dormant database reactivation", which means getting back in touch with old contacts. Their claims are marketing, not evidence.

### Hand-offs between teams are the best-documented weak point in England's hub model

**Evidence:** of all the current weak points in England's hub model, the hand-off is the most clearly documented (row 2). The evaluation recommends:

- joint messages that explain the change
- first contact from the council within 5 working days
- clear responsibility for questions about fees, support and assessment

The Department for Education's end-to-end model aims partly to "reduce handover". The adoption research flags "jurisdictional and interjurisdictional issues" for 26% of US families. These include problems when families deal with more than one area or state.

### When people withdraw, ask why briefly and leave the door open

**Recommendation (Kinderform):** a short, optional exit conversation, plus one question with a set list of reasons. Make a clear "door stays open" offer.

**Evidence:**

- Ofsted records the reasons for withdrawal in set categories.
- Ipsos recommends gathering more qualitative data on why people do not go on "in the least resource intensive way".
- The Spanish review recommends exit interviews.
- Withdrawal is not always a failure. An informed decision not to foster can be a good outcome (CoramBAAF commentary; Ipsos).

### People who do not "fit the mould" fear being judged

**Recommendation (Kinderform):**

- Record, with consent, the demographics of people who stop at each stage.
- Train the people who make first calls on myths that affect particular groups. Examples are renting, being single, being LGBTQ+, age, working, disability and convictions.
- Check early closures by service, to see whether some groups are closed more often than others.

**Evidence:**

- Kantar found that fear of judgement was concentrated among people who do not "fit the mould".
- Kantar reported that some services seemed to lack cultural awareness, which turns questions into "dead ends". It also found rigid assumptions about matching by faith.
- Ipsos reports that progress from enquiry differs by gender, age and ethnicity. But it notes that the ethnicity differences "cannot be reliably" estimated.
- Foster with North East saw no change in the diversity of people enquiring, despite targeted work.
- In adoption, You Can Adopt, the national adoption campaign, prioritises Black and Black Mixed Heritage adopters.

## Which ideas from behavioural science are likely to work here?

Behavioural science studies how small changes, such as who sends a message or how it is worded, change what people do. The table shows which ideas have been tested, and how likely they are to work in fostering and adoption.

| Idea | Evidence in fostering or adoption | Evidence from other fields | How likely to work here |
|---|---|---|---|
| Messenger and reciprocity ("I've booked this for you" from a named person) | Kantar: follow-up calls shift power; Foster with North East values named contacts (C) | Behavioural Insights Team, Bedford: 10.5% → 26.8% attendance (A) | **High**: use named senders and pre-booked slots |
| Specific plans and commitment (implementation intentions: a specific next step, date and place) | None | Behavioural Insights Team and Department for Work and Pensions, jobcentres: positive stepped-wedge effect (A) | **High** for booking visits and events |
| Safe-space and permission framing | Kantar trial: best for adoption intent (A, intentions) | n/a | **High** for the wording of first replies |
| Continuum framing (foster ↔ adopt ↔ respite care or mentoring) | Kantar trial: best for fostering intent (A, intentions) | n/a | **High**; also helps offer other routes to people who say "not now" |
| Honest challenge framing | Kantar: the first lift fades for fostering when people see it again (A, intentions) | Research on realistic job previews (not reviewed here) | **Medium**: always pair with support |
| Social norms / "you have been chosen" | None | HM Revenue and Customs email: 1.6% → 2.4% click-through (A) | **Low to medium**: risks feeling manipulative in a life-changing decision |
| Reminders before events | Erie County calls after missed meetings (C) | Behavioural Insights Team text message evidence broadly positive; but an Army email before assessment had no effect (A) | **Medium**: use for attendance, not for the decision itself |

**We found 2 trials on fostering or adoption recruitment itself:**

- the message test by the Department for Education and Kantar (fieldwork in 2020)
- an older Behavioural Insights Team trial in West Sussex with *existing* carers, on resilience training, which is not about enquiries

We found no trial on following up enquiries by Foundations (the What Works Centre for Children and Families) or by Nesta. This is a real gap.

## Digital tools and AI can speed up first replies, but should not replace the conversation

**The most important digital fact for Kinderform: a national tool is coming, and an open standard could shape it.** The Department for Education has said that:

- new hubs should follow government data, digital and technical standards "rather than investing in new digital solutions at this time"
- the department "will develop digital tools… that streamline the recruitment and onboarding of prospective foster carers"
- hubs will connect to these tools "when ready in 2027"

**Rules to keep a person in charge (Recommendation, Kinderform):**

1. AI (artificial intelligence) may draft acknowledgements, summaries, follow-up emails and call notes, but a named person must approve anything personalised.
2. Bots may answer factual common questions from an approved knowledge base, with links to sources. They must never decide whether someone is eligible. They must hand over to a person on any disclosure, sign of distress or "can I with my…" question.
3. Every message that AI helped with is logged as an `Interaction` with `ai_assisted=true`.
4. No automatic re-contact unless the person opts in.

AI can rightly save time in the *first 5 minutes and the first working day*. It can send instant, warm, specific acknowledgements, and prepare summaries for staff before calls. The gain does not come from replacing the conversation.

**What services and suppliers do now (observed practice):**

- **Specialist enquiry systems.** Council Advertising Network has an Enquiry Management System for councils, designed with 10 council fostering teams. It argues that general contact-management software made for retail fits poorly. The procedures we read show enquiry records kept in Mosaic and LCS, which are case recording systems.
- **Weekly dashboards.** Foster with North East shares weekly PDF dashboards. The evaluators found that teams used this data in day-to-day work, but not yet for planning.
- **Chatbots.** Suppliers sell chatbots to fostering agencies that offer web chat and WhatsApp at any hour (for example fostering.ai). We found no published evaluation.
- **A warning from another public service.** Wandsworth and Richmond ran an AI chatbot pilot for adult unpaid carers (Tovie AI). Local press reported that it produced corrupted text and inaccurate advice about services. The council says social workers reviewed the assessments.
- **Using data to find carers in the USA.** The Contingent, in Oregon, uses data and AI to find likely foster parents by ZIP code (postcode area). It then contacts them by text and email (source: a news report).
- **AI search.** Commentary notes that people thinking about fostering now use general AI assistants to compare services.

**Risks:**

- Wrong advice about who can foster or adopt is the biggest risk, because myths about eligibility are themselves a barrier.
- Someone telling a bot about a safeguarding concern, such as a risk of harm.
- UK data protection law (UK GDPR): bots may collect special category data: sensitive data, such as health, that the law protects more strictly. They may also collect data about household members before those people consent.
- Losing the personal touch, which Kantar shows people read as a preview of "the system".

**Good design worth reusing:**

- Foster with North East's "keeping in touch" calls and regional buddy scheme.
- Adopt South's published figures for each stage, and its 5-day performance from enquiry to registration of interest.
- Oklahoma's Bridge Resource Support Center (2010) made follow-up calls at 10 and 30 days and gave consistent information. It cut the time from enquiry to a worker's follow-up.
- AdoptUSKids' checklist *Is your response system family friendly?*, which includes experienced families designing the enquiry process and checking how services reply.
- Essex's analysis of "no further action" closures by stage and channel.

**Problems that published sources document:**

- Waits of weeks or months for a reply (Department for Education 2026).
- Gaps in communication at the hand-off from hub to council (Foster with North East evaluation).
- "Next to no contact" between adoption Stage 1 and Stage 2 workers (Adoption Barometer).
- Agencies failing to follow up, in older studies (Keogh & Svensson; Geen et al.).

**A note on secret shopping.** AdoptUSKids' checklist suggests checking the enquiry process with a "secret shopper method". Kinderform should prefer test enquiries arranged with services, which agree to them and know about them, rather than deceptive mystery shopping.

## What can we learn from other countries?

- **USA.** "Customer service" is an established way to frame recruitment (AdoptUSKids and its National Resource Center for Diligent Recruitment). Some states publish standards for replying (Florida: 3 business days). The strongest data comes from the study of barriers to adoption (row 10). **Lesson:** treat responsiveness and emotional support as core to the quality of a service. Track each step from enquiry to licensure, the US approval process.
- **Australia.** Randle and colleagues ran a social marketing programme to encourage fostering. They found that 40% of 897 people who were not carers knew nothing about foster care. A notable share had simply never been asked. Past failures to follow up are well documented. **Lesson:** ask people directly, and follow up personally.
- **Spain.** Recruitment has not been evaluated enough. The 2022 *Zerbitzuan* review calls for tools to evaluate the recruitment and contact process, and for exit interviews with families who drop out. Only 7% of foster families repeat the experience (Del Valle et al.). A small Canary Islands study (sample of 26) found these main routes in:
  - campaigns (42.3%)
  - friends or relatives who knew the programme (26.9%)
  - friends or relatives who fostered (23.1%)

  **Lesson:** word of mouth matters, and so do systems for evaluation.
- **Ireland and New Zealand.** We looked at Tusla, Ireland's Child and Family Agency, and Oranga Tamariki, New Zealand's Ministry for Children. We could not confirm any evidence or standards on intake interactions, so we list them as gaps.

## What we propose for the Kinderform standard

These are proposals, and we have not adopted them.

### The draft standard for first replies: keep the fast start, change follow-up, and add 5 new rules

The draft standard sets times and steps for replying to enquiries and following up. The table shows each part of the current draft, our verdict, the revised rule and what it is based on.

| Part of the draft | Verdict | Revised rule | Based on |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acknowledgement within 5 minutes | **Confirm** | Within 5 minutes, 24 hours a day, 7 days a week. Must name the person who will call and when; a written promise of a time slot for the call | Low cost; Kantar (how the first reply feels); Behavioural Insights Team (named messenger) |
| First attempt by a person within 1 working day | **Confirm** | Within 1 working day; includes an evening or Saturday option | Department for Education hub standard (call within 24 hours); Wales 1 working day; Ipsos (evening and weekend contact) |
| First conversation within 5 working days | **Confirm** | Unchanged | Essex (people lost because they could not be reached) |
| 3 attempts over 5 working days across channels | **Tighten** | 3 attempts at different times of day across 2 or more channels, the last offering self-booking; then a "we'll keep your place" message | Evidence on persistence from other fields; Derby practice |
| Follow-up messages (nurture) on days 2, 7 and 21, set by how strongly the person intends to go on (intent tier) | **Change** | Day 2: personal "what happens next" (named sender). Day 7: offer of a story from, or a call with, an experienced carer or adopter. Day 21: check-in call. Then follow only the rhythm the person chooses. No more than 1 message a week unless the person replies | No evidence on how often; avoid contacting too often; evidence on reciprocity and peers |
| "Not now", with a re-contact date the person chooses (3, 6 or 12 months) | **Confirm and extend** | Add an "other ways to help" offer (respite care, mentoring, the adoption ↔ fostering continuum) and an opt-in newsletter | Kantar (keep warm, continuum); Essex ("not the right time") |
| One-question withdrawal survey | **Extend** | One coded question plus an optional 10-minute exit conversation; "door stays open" | Ipsos; Spanish review |
| *New:* named journey guide | **Add** | Assigned at first conversation; continues through assessment | Department for Education end-to-end requirement; evidence on continuity |
| *New:* warm hand-off | **Add** | Joint message within 2 working days naming the new contact. Receiving team's first contact within 5 working days. The sending team stays responsible until contact is confirmed | Foster with North East Year 3 recommendation |
| *New:* recovery after a missed event | **Add** | Call within 2 working days offering a one-to-one meeting | Erie County, via Sebba |
| *New:* kinship route | **Add** | Send to the kinship team within 1 working day; no follow-up messages (nurture) | Different legal route |
| *New:* keeping-in-touch rhythm | **Add** | At least every 2 weeks from expression of interest to the approval panel, or whenever there is a gap with no activity | Foster with North East; Adoption Barometer (gaps in contact) |

### A draft Interaction record would log every contact

We propose a new record, `Interaction`, to sit alongside the Enquiry record. Each time a service and a person are in touch, the service adds one `Interaction`. This would let services measure what they cannot measure now. The draft fields are below.

```
Interaction {
  interaction_id: uuid
  enquiry_id: uuid                  # FK to Enquiry
  sequence_no: int
  type: enum [acknowledgement, call_attempt, conversation, sms, email, whatsapp,
              letter, info_pack, info_session_booked, info_session_attended,
              info_session_missed, home_visit, peer_contact_offered, peer_contact_held,
              nurture, keeping_in_touch, handoff_sent, handoff_received,
              not_now_agreed, recontact, withdrawal_conversation, exit_survey]
  channel: enum [phone, video, in_person, email, sms, whatsapp, post, web_chat, portal]
  direction: enum [outbound, inbound]
  actor_role: enum [system, recruitment_adviser, social_worker, journey_guide,
                    peer_carer, peer_adopter, hub, la, raa, ifa, vaa, ai_agent]
  actor_org_id: string              # ODS/Ofsted URN where available
  ai_assisted: bool
  human_approved_by: string|null
  occurred_at: datetime
  duration_seconds: int|null
  outcome: enum [no_answer, voicemail, connected, completed, booked, attended,
                 missed, declined, opted_out, bounced]
  topics: [eligibility_myth, finances, process_timeline, support, kinship,
           adoption_continuum, disclosure, other]
  next_step_agreed: string|null
  next_contact_due_at: datetime|null
  recontact_preference: enum [3m, 6m, 12m, custom, none]|null
  handoff_to_org_id: string|null
  handoff_acknowledged_at: datetime|null
  enquirer_feedback_score: int(1-5)|null   # "felt welcomed / able to ask anything"
  consent_basis: enum [consent, public_task, legitimate_interest]
}
```

**How the record is designed:**

- It is event-sourced: each contact is added as a new event. The status of an enquiry should be *worked out* from its Interactions, not edited by hand.
- It lines up `actor_org_id` and service fields with organisation and service identifiers in the style of Open Referral's Human Services Data Specification (HSDS).
- It uses Ofsted's definition of an "actionable enquiry".
- In `actor_role`, `la` means council, `raa` regional adoption agency, `ifa` independent agency and `vaa` voluntary adoption agency.
- Store topics as codes, not free text, to limit special category data: the sensitive personal data that the law protects more strictly.
- *Maintainer note:* the existing schema is pseudonymous: it holds no names or contact details. To keep that design, `human_approved_by` should hold a role or a local reference, never a name. And `next_step_agreed` should be a coded value, not free text. This would need settling before any change to the schema.

### 5 patterns that services could reuse

1. **First call (15–25 minutes).**
   - Thank them, and give them permission: "Nothing you ask counts against you."
   - Ask "What made you get in touch now?" Their questions come first.
   - Correct the 2 or 3 myths that apply to them.
   - Give an honest picture of a typical week, together with the support on offer.
   - Present the options along the continuum.
   - Offer peer contact.
   - Agree a next step with a date. This is an implementation intention: a specific plan for what, when and where.
   - The named worker sends a summary by text or email within an hour.
   - *Basis:* Kantar; Behavioural Insights Team; Ipsos.
2. **"What happens next" messages.** After every change of stage, the named guide sends a short message. It covers:
   - what just happened
   - what happens next
   - who will contact them, and by when
   - how long it usually takes
   - one thing they can do now

   *Basis:* evidence on continuity and transparency; Barometer complaints about delays.
3. **"Not now".** Support their decision ("timing matters, and this is a good decision"). Ask when and how they would like you to get back in touch. Offer other ways to help. Then get back in touch on that date with one personal message. *Basis:* Essex; Kantar.
4. **Warm hand-off.** Send one three-way message that introduces the new named contact. Pass on a summary so the person does not have to repeat their story. The sending team checks that first contact has happened. It escalates if contact has not happened within 5 working days. *Basis:* Foster with North East Year 3.
5. **Withdrawal conversation.** Thank them. Ask one question with a set list of reasons, and offer an optional chat. Say what they could do if things change. Offer to keep their record (with consent), and leave the door open. *Basis:* Ipsos; Spanish review; CoramBAAF on "good" withdrawals.

### Adoption needs some changes to the patterns

- Make the first contact for adoption clearly a **safe space**, which was Kantar's best framing for adoption. Acknowledge infertility and fear of rejection when the person raises them.
- Tie the timing of information events and registrations of interest to the time limits in statutory guidance. These are information within 10 working days, and a decision on a registration of interest within 5 working days.
- Make Early Permanence and fostering-to-adopt visible early, since 64% of Adopt South's approved adopters wished to be considered for Early Permanence.
- Pair people who want to adopt with an experienced adopter before they register their interest.
- When an agency declines someone at registration of interest, procedures require it to give written reasons and point them to other agencies. Make this a coded `Interaction`.

## What we still do not know, and how we could find out

**What is missing:**

- There is no evidence on whether any single step after an enquiry causes better results in fostering or adoption. This covers timing, number of attempts, peer contact, how often to follow up, and journey guides.
- There is no national data on reply times, or on how people experience making an enquiry.
- Data for Scotland, Northern Ireland, Ireland and New Zealand is weak.
- There is no analysis of whether early closures affect some groups more than others.
- There are no evaluations of chatbots or AI in this field.

**Questions Kinderform could answer openly:**

1. Does a first reply from a named sender, with a pre-booked slot, raise the share of people who have a first conversation? A hub could test this with a randomised controlled trial, with each person put into a group by chance.
2. Does offering peer contact at the first call raise the rate of expressions of interest and applications? Does it reduce withdrawals for the reason "process not as expected"?
3. Does a warm hand-off reduce withdrawals between hub and council? This could use a stepped-wedge design across the councils in a hub.
4. What rhythm of follow-up brings back the most people who said "not now", without raising opt-outs?

**An outline for an evaluation with its plan published in advance (pre-registered):**

- **Partners:** a hub or regional adoption agency, plus Foundations or a university.
- **Registration:** publish the plan in advance on the Open Science Framework (OSF).
- **Design:** randomise at the level of each enquiry, or use a stepped-wedge design at council level.
- **Main outcome:** an expression of interest or registration of interest within 90 days.
- **Other outcomes:** application within 12 months; withdrawal reason; "felt welcomed" score; opt-outs.
- **Sample size:** set it from current rates. A single hub the size of Foster with North East has enough enquiries to detect only moderate effects, so pool data across hubs.
- **Ethics review, data protection impact assessment and transparency notice:** no deception, and publish the results whatever they show.
- **Openness:** publish the `Interaction` schema and the analysis code openly.

- **Fostering in England:** up to the application, or the start of the Form F assessment (Stages 1 and 2). Form F is the standard report used to assess foster carers.

- acknowledgements and first calls
- follow-ups, information packs and information events
- initial visits and peer contact
- follow-up messages (nurture) and handling "not now"
- hand-offs between services
- digital and AI tools
- conversations when someone withdraws

- UK official statistics, and evaluations paid for by the Department for Education (Ipsos, Kantar, and Ipsos with the Centre for Evidence and Implementation, CEI)

**The main limit.** We found no randomised or quasi-experimental study of any specific step after an enquiry in fostering or adoption. Examples would be call timing, the number of attempts, or how often to follow up. Grade A evidence comes only from:

- the hub evaluation, which tested a change to the whole model, not single steps
- studies of message wording that measured stated intentions
- similar fields, such as jobcentres and sales

## What we are less sure about

- The strongest controlled evidence found that a hub with faster first steps did not lead to more approvals. It is not yet proven that speed alone gets more people to approval.
- Several widely quoted drop-out figures (80%, 70–95%, under 2%, 96%) come from studies from 1990 to 2008, which we found through Sebba's review.
- A vendor linked to one of the authors supplied the Harvard Business Review data.
- Kantar's trial measured intentions in a research panel, not real enquiries.
- The Department for Education's "14%" and "9 of 10 hubs" statements come from programme documents whose methods are not published.
- Some standards come from procedure manuals, which may not show what actually happens.
- Randle et al.'s "never asked" share appears as both 22% and almost one-third in other sources that report it. We report it here only as "a notable share". *(Maintainer check, 14 November 2026: the abstract of Randle, Miller, Dolnicar & Ciarrochi (2014) gives "almost one-third" of 756 Australians surveyed in 2009. The 22% figure may come from a different paper by the same team; not yet traced.)*
