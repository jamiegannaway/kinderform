# Dropping out on the way to fostering: what other countries count, and what cutting paperwork has done

> **Status: first draft.** Desk review, 25 November 2026. It looks at global bodies, 15 countries and their states or regions. An AI-assisted research tool produced it. Some figures are old or secondhand; each is marked. See [how we made this](https://www.kinderform.org/about#how). Everything we propose is for discussion. It is not yet part of the Kinderform standard, the open standard that the project behind kinderform.org is drafting. The [research log](../research-log.json) says what each review could mean for the project.

Grades: A = randomised or strong quasi-experimental; B = controlled comparison or robust official statistics; C = descriptive, survey or qualitative; D = anecdote, single site or secondhand.

## In short

- **Most people who get in touch do not go on to be approved, everywhere that publishes figures.** In English-speaking systems the share is usually between 2% and 10%. In the Netherlands it is nearer 22% to 25%, because kinship carers are counted. The figures cannot be compared fairly, because "enquiry" means different things.
- **Once people are in assessment, most who leave choose to leave.** Where stages are split out (Flanders, Denmark), refusals are 0% to 6% of decided cases and withdrawals by the person are 40% to 50%.
- **Simpler and faster processes are now official policy in several places, but have hardly been tested.** Our search found no controlled study establishing more approvals from a faster or simpler process. The England and Denmark comparisons are not proof of no effect.
- **Some people report being put off by a poor or slow reply.** That is the strongest reason to keep a short form and a fast first reply: respect, not a promise of more carers.

## 1. What we can and cannot say

1. **The pattern is reliable; the numbers are not.** Every place that publishes figures shows large losses between first contact and approval (grade B overall). Any single comparison between countries is weak. "Enquiry" ranges from a click on a website to a finished phone conversation (Ipsos for DfE, 2023). Kinship carers are sometimes counted and sometimes not. Most figures divide one year's approvals by the same year's enquiries; they do not follow people through the stages.
2. **People leaving by choice is the main way out during assessment.** Flanders provincial figures (2018 to 2020) show about 40% to 49% of finished screenings ended with the candidate withdrawing, against 0 to 5 refusals a year. Danish regional supervisors report about 40% withdrawing and 6% refused in the Capital Region (2017). In England, Ofsted's 2024 to 2025 figures show 3,290 withdrawals against 2,050 approvals among completed applications. Grade B for the pattern.
3. **Some people who stopped said the service's reply put them off.** A study for the Children's Welfare Association of Victoria interviewed 91 people who had enquired. Only 4% had become carers. The rest were split about evenly between people whose own circumstances changed and people put off in some way by the agency's response (Keogh and Svensson, *Children Australia*, 1999). It is old and small. Related reviews describe similar experiences. These reports do not isolate the reply's effect from other reasons for stopping. Grade C.
4. **Kinship is where cutting paperwork has the clearest fairness case.** In New Zealand, 1 in 5 children are placed with caregivers who are not yet approved, and family (whānau) caregivers are most affected. Ireland had 244 relative carers not yet approved at the end of 2024. US federal rules now let states set lighter standards for kin. Grade B for the problem, C for the fix.
5. **Global bodies count children, not carers.** None of the global or European organisations we looked at publish figures on how people get from enquiry to approval.

## 2. Global and European bodies

- **DataCare** (Eurochild and UNICEF Europe and Central Asia, 2020 to 2023) mapped data systems in the 27 EU states and the UK. All collect some data on children in alternative care, with different categories and methods. We found no coverage of carers' enquiries, applications or withdrawals in this review. A small set of carer-journey measures could help; this is our proposal.
- **The UN Guidelines for the Alternative Care of Children** (2009), and the bodies that promote them, focus on moving from institutions to family-based care and on the quality of carers. We found no figures from them, or from IFCO, the Council of Europe or the World Bank, on adults dropping out or on how long assessment takes. Treat this as a gap, not as proof that nothing exists.
- **The OECD on paperwork.** *Fixing frictions: "sludge audits" around the world* (June 2024, with the NSW Behavioural Insights Unit) describes a structured way to find and reduce needless steps, waits and worry in a public service. It draws on 10 case studies from 16 governments in 14 countries. None is about fostering. A 2026 critique (Frolov, *Journal of Institutional Economics*) notes the paper says little about the benefits of procedures. That matters here: some steps in fostering are there on purpose, to protect children and to help people decide. *Does it carry over?* The method does. The results do not.

## 3. Country by country

### Ireland (Tusla)

- Tusla says its National Fostering Team gets "almost four" enquiries a day from the public: about 1,400 a year by our own sum, not a published total.
- Tusla's review for 2024 (published August 2025) reports 211 general and relative foster carers approved in 2024, up 22% from 173 in 2023; 34 private carers approved; 255 carers stopping; 3,809 carers on the panel at the end of 2024.
- From 2017 to mid-2022, Tusla approved about 209 of its own carers a year while about 273 a year stopped. 92% of those who stopped chose to.
- A rough share of enquiries ending in approval is about 15% (211 of an estimated 1,400). Grade D for the share, B for the approval counts.
- No published stage table, reasons for withdrawing or response-time standard were found. There is no evidence yet linking the 2024 rise to the new national recruitment team.

### New Zealand (Oranga Tamariki)

- The steps are: an online enquiry form, a call back, then an application pack. The pack asks for forms, a medical form from a GP, two referees, two forms of ID, consent to police checks, and ID for every adult in the home and regular adult visitors.
- The independent monitor, Aroturuki Tamariki, reports that the average time to approve a caregiver rose from 56 days (June 2023) to 101 days in 2023/24, then fell to 84 days in 2024/25.
- The share of children placed with fully or provisionally approved caregivers rose from 67% to 80%. Only about 17% of provisionally approved caregivers were monitored as the rules require. Oranga Tamariki told the monitor it has no caregiver recruitment strategy.
- No figures on enquiries, applications or withdrawals are published. Grade B for timings.

### United States

- *The Imprint's* survey of state agencies found 178,026 licensed foster homes in 2025, down 9% from 2023 and 19% from 2019.
- Definitions matter. Florida's 40% rise in licensed homes in the 2020 survey came mostly from a new licence level for relatives, not from new carers.
- A 2020 report for the Joint Economic Committee says "only 8 percent" of people who enquire become licensed, citing a study it does not name. Older reviews give 9%, from work dating from 1957 to 1989. Both are secondhand and old. Grade D.
- Federal rules from September 2023 let agencies use separate, lighter licensing standards for relatives. Guidance of May 2026 updates the national model standards to "reduce unnecessary administrative burden" and adds recommended practice on timeliness and on responding to families. No federal evaluation of the effect on approvals has been published.

### Canada

- The Ontario Association of Children's Aid Societies reported a 33.6% fall in available foster homes since 2020 (June 2024). No national or provincial figures from enquiry to approval were found. Grade B for the fall.

### Australia

- There is no national data from enquiry to approval.
- During South Australia's state-funded recruitment service (2004 to 2006), about 2% of people who enquired became carers (Delfabbro et al., 2008). Often quoted secondhand. Grade C, old. Campaign figures include many casual enquiries.

### The Netherlands

Pleegzorg Nederland's 2024 factsheet compares people who asked for information with newly accepted foster carers.

| Year | Asked for information | Newly accepted | Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2022 | 6,715 | 1,712 | about 25% |
| 2023 | 7,845 | 1,706 | about 22% |
| 2024 | 7,662 | 1,752 | about 23% |

In 2024, 1,048 of those accepted were kinship carers and 704 were general carers. For general carers the share is nearer 9%. In the same year 3,020 carers stopped. Grade B for the counts, C for the share. Useful as a model for counting kinship and general carers apart.

### Belgium (Flanders)

A written answer in the Flemish Parliament (20 August 2025) gave the first stage counts for general foster care across Flanders.

| Stage | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Information packs requested | 4,573 | 4,468 | 5,347 |
| Information session attended | 1,370 | 1,729 | 1,764 |
| Preparation and screening started | 566 | 468 | 445 |
| Approved | 387 | 232 | 237 |

In 2024 that is about 4.4% of people who asked for a pack and about 13% of people who came to a session. The minister said the data cannot tell withdrawal from refusal. Grade B. *Does it carry over?* Strongly, as a model of clear, published stages.

### Denmark

- Five regional supervisory bodies approve foster families. In the Capital Region in 2017, of 148 applications handled (foster families and other services together), 54% were approved, 6% refused and 40% withdrew.
- Across Denmark in 2021, about 600 applications led to 288 approvals (press reporting of the official annual report).
- The official evaluation of a shared approval model found the share of applicants newly approved fell from 57% to 54% between 2018 and 2020. It could not show that the change led to more approvals. Grade B/C. This is the clearest European finding of no effect from redesigning the process.

### Norway, Germany, Spain and Sweden

- **Norway.** The government's 2023 to 2024 white paper says there are no statistics on the stages from interest to foster home. The national agency recruited 189 new foster homes in 2023.
- **Germany.** No official figures on foster carer applicants dropping out were found.
- **Spain.** No official stage figures from Catalonia, Madrid or the Basque Country. Catalonia sets a 6-month legal limit for assessing foster families. Spain's ombudsman has recommended that regions shorten assessment times.
- **Sweden.** Nothing found in this review. A later scan found survey research; see the engagement review.

### The UK nations

- **England.** Ipsos for DfE (July 2023, 10 case studies) records that 6% of enquiries went on to an application in the year to March 2022. It found services define "enquiry" very differently and that Ofsted's return is open to interpretation. It warned that a low rate can reflect a careful, proper process. Grade C.
- **Scotland.** The Care Inspectorate reports 2,828 approved fostering households at 31 December 2024, down 5.7% in a year. CELCIS (October 2023) gives an overall rate of about 4% from enquiry to approval and 37% from application to approval in 2022. Grade B for counts.
- **Wales.** The Welsh Government (May 2025) reports 2,609 local authority fostering households. No national figures from enquiry to approval were found.
- **Northern Ireland.** No official stage data found.

## 4. States, provinces and councils

- **California.** Assessments must finish within 90 days unless there is good cause, and counties report monthly on delays. A backlog persisted. A 2018 law lets someone who withdraws apply again within 12 months. Grade C.
- **Victoria, Australia.** Fostering Connections, a single front door for the state since 2015 to 2016, says it has connected over 45,000 people with agencies. Money worries were the most common thing putting people off (66%). In 2023 the number of people going through accreditation fell by 26%. No published evaluation links the front door to approvals.
- **New South Wales.** A sector paper (September 2024) reports falls in authorised carers and in applications between 2021 and 2024, and no central record of carers.
- **Software suppliers' claims.** One US supplier reports approvals 18% to 75% faster and 30% more approvals. These are its own figures with no comparison group. Grade D. Do not cite as evidence.
- **Southampton City Council, 2024/25.** A rare published local stage table: 103 enquiries, 76 screening calls, 33 first home visits, 16 applications and 11 mainstream approvals. The year before, 179 enquiries led to 8 mainstream approvals.
- **Barnet, 2024/25.** 279 enquiries and 12 mainstream approvals.
- **Aberdeenshire, 2023/24.** 36% of enquiries moved on to the next stage.

## 6. Changes to the process, and what was measured

| Place | What changed | What was measured | Comparison? | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| England | Regional recruitment hubs | Faster first visits; no more approvals | Yes | B |
| Denmark | Shared approval model | Share approved 57% to 54%; no clear effect | Before and after | C |
| United States | Lighter licensing option for kin (2023); model standards updated (2026) | Not yet evaluated | No | C |
| California | 90-day rule; reports on delays; apply again after withdrawing | Backlog persisted | No | C |
| Florida | Licence level for kin | Licensed homes up 40%, mostly reclassified | No | C |
| Ireland | National recruitment team | Approvals 173 to 211 (2023 to 2024) | No | D |
| Victoria | One front door for the state | 45,000+ connected; accreditations fell in 2023 | No | C |
| New Zealand | Provisional approval | 67% to 80% of placements with approved or provisional caregivers | No | C |

Nowhere did we find a randomised or strong comparison study of simpler processes in fostering. The likely reason the two comparisons found no rise in approvals is that most people who leave do so for personal reasons, or after thinking hard about what fostering involves. Less paperwork does not change those decisions. A good reply still matters for respect and fairness.

## 7. What this could mean for the Kinderform standard

Ideas the evidence supports:

1. **Keep the form short, and do not add questions to learn why people leave.** Instead, offer an optional question when someone steps back, with fixed, kind choices.
2. **Describe a fast first reply as respect.** Say plainly that it is not a promise of more carers.
3. **Use shared stage names that keep withdrawal apart from refusal.** Flanders and Southampton show it can be done. Keep to Ofsted's own split of withdrawals "by applicant" and "by service", and add "paused" and "not approved".
4. **Keep kinship on its own route and count it apart.**
5. **Let people come back.** Someone who withdrew should be able to pick up again without starting over.

Ideas to reject or soften, because they would harm trust:

- **No tables that rank services.** Ipsos found that comparing councils with independent agencies explained little and could raise tensions.
- **Do not turn the 1-working-day reply into a stick.** Teams are small. Report it with staffing, never as a judgement on a person.
- **One kind follow-up after a missed call, not repeated chasing.**
- **A low rate from enquiry to approval is not failure.** It can show people making a careful choice.

## 8. Gaps

No usable evidence was found for: Northern Ireland stage data; Germany (foster care); Canadian provinces beyond Ontario; Queensland, Western Australia and Tasmania; Japan, South Korea, South Africa, Latin America and Eastern Europe; any controlled study of shorter forms, online portals or checks run side by side in fostering; and any count of people who start a fostering enquiry form and do not finish it.

## Check before public use

1. Ireland: 245 (Tusla annual report), 244 (RTÉ), 211 (Tusla review) and 210 (government press release) are all given for approvals in 2024. Confirm what each counts, and what "almost four enquiries a day" covers.
2. Ofsted 2024 to 2025: 2,050 approved plus 3,290 withdrawn makes 5,340, not 5,370. Confirm the other 30.
3. Netherlands and Flanders counts, against the factsheet and the parliamentary answer.
4. Denmark: the regional figures for 2024 and a "up to 50%" drop-out estimate were read from search results only.
5. Scotland: the 4% and 37% figures, and which stages they cover.
6. United States "8%": the report does not name the study. Trace it before use.
7. Delfabbro et al. (2008): confirm "2%" in the paper itself.
8. New Zealand: what starts the clock for the 101-day and 84-day figures.
9. Any software supplier's figures.
