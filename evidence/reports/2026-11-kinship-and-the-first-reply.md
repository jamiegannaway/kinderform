# Relatives and friends who get in touch: kinship carers and the first reply

> **Status: first draft.** Desk review, 26 November 2026. It was made with an AI-assisted search of official sources, charity surveys, research and the web. See [how we made this](https://www.kinderform.org/about#how). On 26 November 2026 we matched the laws, official figures and most survey figures to their sources, by computer. Some sources could be read only secondhand, and the tables say which. [Gaps and limits](#8-gaps-and-limits) lists what we could not read at all. No person has checked any figure yet. Everything we propose is for discussion. It is not yet part of the Kinderform standard, the open standard that the project behind kinderform.org is drafting. The [research log](../research-log.json) says what each review could mean for the project.

Grades:

- **A:** randomised or strong quasi-experimental
- **B:** controlled comparison, large observational study, or robust official statistics
- **C:** descriptive, survey or qualitative
- **D:** anecdote, a single site, guidance, a page we read, or read secondhand

**Kinship care** means a child being raised by a relative or a family friend. A **kinship foster carer** is approved as a foster carer for one child they already know, when the council looks after that child. Councils also call this "family and friends" or "connected persons" foster care. Most kinship care is not foster care at all.

This review is about the adult who gets in touch. It records nothing about any child.

## In short

1. **Most kinship carers do not arrive through a recruitment form.** They step in during a crisis, and the child's social worker usually contacts them (grade D).
2. **But some do reach a general fostering form.** GOV.UK tells people who want to look after a child in care to ask their council about "fostering a child you know". None of the 48 UK fostering enquiry forms we read asks if the person already knows the child (grade D, our own count).
3. **Most say they did not know their legal options.** In one survey, three-quarters of kinship carers said they did not know enough about their legal options when they took on the child. In another, it was 82% (grade C).
4. **The legal choice affects money, and people commit without knowing it.** So a first reply must name the options without ranking them, and point to independent advice.
5. **Councils in England now have a legal duty to publish a kinship local offer.** The duty began on 29 June 2026. In a 2025 survey, 13% of kinship carers had seen their council's offer (grade C).
6. **We found no published standard for how fast a kinship team should first reply.** The statutory guidance, updated in October 2026, sets no time. The time we suggest is reasoned, not taken from evidence.
7. **The best-evidenced support is a person, not a form.** In the United States, a federal clearinghouse rates nine kinship navigator programmes as promising or better. A navigator is someone who helps a carer find and get support. England's own practice guide recommends the same kind of help (grade B).

## 5. Kinship navigators in the United States

A navigator is a person who helps a kinship carer find and get support. A federal clearinghouse in the United States rates these programmes. On 26 November 2026 it listed nine rated promising or better. Many of its measures are about children. Kinderform works on the adult side only, so we report only what the programmes do and what happened for carers.

| Programme | What it does | What the rating says | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| Washington State Kinship Navigator Program | A navigator works with each carer on what they need. The fuller version adds a needs assessment that fits the family's culture. | Rated "supported", last reviewed September 2024. Carers in the fuller version reported better overall wellbeing than carers in the usual service: an effect size of 0.63, among 252 carers. Both groups were highly satisfied. The clearinghouse found no effect on which services carers used. | B. Wellbeing was reported by carers. The study compares two versions, not a navigator against none. |
| Arizona Kinship Support Services | Help to find services, legal referral, support groups and help with basic needs. | Rated "supported", September 2022, on measures about children that are outside our scope. An earlier study of 63 carers found fewer unmet needs afterwards. | B for the rated study. C for the earlier study, which had no comparison group and which we read secondhand. |
| Ohio's Kinship Supports Intervention | A support plan and regular visits from a kinship worker. | Rated "promising", last reviewed October 2021. | C |
| A model in New Jersey | Help to find services. | The clearinghouse says it "does not currently meet criteria to receive a rating". | Not rated |

**What England's own guide says:** Foundations publishes a practice guide on kinship care for councils in England. It recommends that councils "offer kinship carers specialist support to learn about, navigate and access the support that they are entitled to". It rates the evidence for that as good, and the evidence for peer support groups as promising.

**What carries over:** the programmes we looked at give each carer a person to talk to, and help with legal and money questions early. None of them is a form or a series of automatic messages. The United States pays for these services differently, so do not assume the same effects in England.

## 6. What it could mean for the Kinderform standard

All of this is for discussion. Our earlier review suggested one rule: send a kinship enquiry to the kinship team within 1 working day, with no follow-up messages. This review points to a fuller version.

It fits the national kinship standards. They say families should get clear information about their legal options "at the outset". For someone who arrives through a form, the first reply is the outset.

### A route for relatives and friends, in five parts

1. **Recognise.** Every form asks one plain question near the start: "Are you asking about a child you already know?" The hint gives examples, such as a grandchild, a niece or nephew, or a friend's child. Use everyday words, because many families do not call themselves kinship carers. Answering yes never blocks the form or makes the person start again. Our example form already works this way.
2. **Reply quickly, and as a person.** An automatic reply goes at once. A person replies the same working day where possible, and by the end of the next working day at the latest. The automatic reply also gives the council's children's services number and its out-of-hours number, for anyone who needs help today. It gives them to everyone who answers yes, so the form never has to ask about the child.
3. **Never recruit.** A kinship enquiry is never added to recruitment, reminder, event or marketing messages. It is counted apart from mainstream enquiries. This should be a setting that campaign software cannot override.
4. **Hand over, and confirm it.** The reply says which team will get in touch next, and how. That is the kinship team, or the child's social worker if there is one. The record holds only whether the hand-over was confirmed, and nothing about the child. If the council cannot be reached, the reply still gives independent advice.
5. **Friends and informal carers too.** The route covers relatives, friends and people already caring informally. A friend caring for a child for 28 days or more may need to know about private fostering. The council should explain that. Kinderform does not decide it.

### What a first reply could say

A person writes it, from a template. It should:

1. Thank them, and recognise the situation, without asking for the child's story.
2. Say which team will get in touch, by when and how.
3. Say there are different ways to care for a child in the family:
   - an informal family arrangement
   - approval as a kinship foster carer
   - a special guardianship order
   - a child arrangements order

   Say that each has different legal and money effects. The right one depends on their situation, and on decisions by the council and the courts. Do not rank them. Do not give an allowance as a reason to choose one.
4. Say they have a right to independent advice, and give at least two free services.
5. Link to the council's kinship local offer, and offer to post a paper copy.
6. If the council is in the allowance trial, say so as a fact, with a link to the council's own page. Promise nothing.
7. Offer a phone call and other formats, such as paper, large print or another language.
8. Give a way to raise concerns that sits outside any assessment.

### What software can do, and what a person must do

| Software can do | A person must do |
|---|---|
| Notice a "yes" to the question | Any reply about the options or the person's situation |
| Send the automatic reply, with the time and what happens next | Explain how the options differ |
| Keep the enquiry out of every recruitment message | Anything about a particular child |
| Send the enquiry to the kinship team, and flag it if nobody replies in time | Respond to distress, anger or a complaint |
| Attach the link to the local offer and the advice line numbers | Decide whether to involve the child's social worker |
| Record that the hand-over was confirmed | Agree the next step and a named contact |

### What to measure: the service, never the carer

- the time from a kinship enquiry to the automatic reply, and to the first reply from a person
- the share of kinship enquiries that were sent any recruitment message, where the aim is none
- the share of first replies that gave the local offer link and at least two independent advice services
- the share of hand-overs confirmed with a named team in time
- whether a person can get from the fostering enquiry page to the kinship local offer in two clicks
- whether the general fostering form asks about a child the person already knows

**What Kinderform will not do:** build or use anything that scores, ranks or predicts relatives and friends. The first assessment of a relative or friend is social work, set out in law. Kinderform will not copy it, fill it in ahead or screen people for it.

## 7. Cautions

- **Relatives and friends are not recruits.** Their approval is for one child, and Ofsted counts them apart. Mixing them into recruitment messages or totals would distort the figures and could hurt people in a crisis.
- **Never present fostering as the option that pays.** Half of kinship foster carers said they would change arrangement if the allowance were the same, so money weighs heavily. A reply that nudges anyone towards or away from fostering for money would break trust.
- **Do not promise what a council cannot give.** Support differs by council and by legal order. Kinship said the allowance trial would reach only around 4% of children in kinship care in England.
- **Stay on the adult side.** Kinderform acknowledges, informs and hands over. Whether a child can live with someone is for the council and the courts.
- **More scrutiny can put families off.** Forms should collect as little as possible, and say why.
- **Fairness.** Online-only routes, the word "kinship" and guesses about wider family help can shut out older, poorer and minority ethnic carers.
- **Charity surveys are also campaigns.** Much of the evidence comes from Kinship and Family Rights Group. Their surveys are large, but carers chose to take part, and both charities campaign for change. Independent research from 2008 and 2019 says similar things. We could read some of the charities' reports only secondhand.
- **Advice lines are stretched.** Sending everyone to one line could overload it.
- **Systems, not staff.** Every measure here is about pages and processes, not about any social worker or call handler.

## 8. Gaps and limits

- **We found no research on kinship carers who use general fostering enquiry forms.** Our own count is a first step. It is a word search, and a person has not checked it.
- **We found no published reply-time standard** for councils' kinship teams, or for the advice lines.
- **Ofsted collects no enquiry figures for kinship households.** Its guidance does not say how to count a relative or friend who uses a general fostering form.
- **Wales, Northern Ireland, Ireland, New Zealand and most of Australia** were not reviewed in depth. Scotland was covered only for its advice line.
- **The core studies are old.** Hunt and others (2008) came before the 2024 guidance and the 2026 Act.
- **No one has tested a way of handling first contact with kinship carers in England.** Every idea here needs testing, ideally with kinship carers helping to write the words.
- **The rules may change again.** The Law Commission's final report is still to come.
- **Read secondhand.** Family Rights Group's website refused our reader. So its 2019 survey, its 2024 policy audit and its advice line figures come from a search engine's copy or from news reports. The figures from *Lost in the legal labyrinth* come from a news report. The 4% to 39% range and the "one in two" figure come from the Ambassador's report.
- **Could not read at all.** Hunt's 2020 overview of UK kinship research, one council's page about the allowance trial, and one council's enquiry form. Nothing in this report rests on them.
- **The four council pages are examples, not a sample.** They cannot show how common anything is.
