# Evidence library

This is our reading list of research and policy on recruiting foster carers, handling their enquiries and keeping them. It also includes some studies on adoption. Each entry gives the source, a link and a short summary in our own words. We add new work as we find it.

Last updated: 2026-11-14

## The main points

- **How a service responds can put people off.** In a small, old study from Australia, only 4% of people who enquired went on to become carers. About half of the rest had been put off by how the agency responded to them.
- **Speed alone is not enough.** A regional hub in North East England made initial visits much faster. A hub is a group of councils that recruit foster carers together. But a comparison with similar areas found no statistically significant gain in applications or approvals. Statistically significant means unlikely to be down to chance.
- **Hand-offs are a weak point.** A hand-off is when one organisation passes a person on to another. In the same evaluation, the hand-off from the hub to the council was a point of friction.
- **The relationship with the service matters for whether carers stay.** Two reviews of many studies point to the relationship with the agency as a key factor.
- **Knowing a carer, or being asked, matters.** Meeting or knowing other foster carers is what most often leads people to consider fostering. In one survey, almost one-third had never considered it because no one had ever asked them.
- **Big gaps remain.** No experiment has yet tested follow-up with real people who enquire. There are no national figures on how quickly services reply.

The details and sources are in the entries below. Most summaries are based on the abstract, not the full text, so check the original before you quote it. [How to read an entry](#how-to-read-an-entry) explains the labels and notes.

## Who goes on after an enquiry, and what puts people off?

These studies follow people from their first enquiry or training. They look at who goes on, who stops, and why.

**Keogh et al. (1999). Why don't they become foster carers? A study of people who inquire about foster care.** *Children Australia.*\
The researchers followed up people who had enquired about fostering. Only 4% had become carers. For the rest, the reasons split about evenly: their own circumstances, or being put off by how the agency responded to them. This is the closest published study to the main claim behind this project. It is old and small, so treat it as a pointer, not firm evidence.\
Stage: enquiry · Type: qualitative (91 phone interviews) · Place: Victoria, Australia · [Source](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:31e6ebdc-13ba-46f2-ba3c-c881b781c1aa/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Engelhardt et al. (2025). The process of becoming a foster parent: agency effects on the retention and recruitment of others.** *Journal of Public Child Welfare.*\
Carers' satisfaction with their recruitment worker, and with how the agency communicated, was linked to wanting to continue fostering. It was also linked to recommending fostering to others. This supports treating the first contact as the start of keeping carers, not a separate task.\
Stage: enquiry, retention · Type: survey · Place: United States (one state) · [Source](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:d3bbe679-3e4b-4a0e-9e4d-9dcd2e14f28c/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Day et al. (2022). Who are our caregivers? Characteristics of foster parents at certain milestones across two states.** *Journal of Public Child Welfare.*\
Describes the people at each milestone, from training before licensing to the first time a child is placed with them. It raises concern about who the recruitment process leaves out. It offers a useful model for measuring progress milestone by milestone.\
Stage: enquiry, assessment · Type: survey (656 people) · Place: Missouri and Mississippi, United States · [Source](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:a3736bae-392d-49ee-b8e5-f7bf52559244/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Rhodes, Orme, Cox & Buehler (2003). Foster family resources, psychosocial functioning, and retention.** *Social Work Research.*\
Almost half of the families who began pre-service training did not finish it. This is the training people take before they start fostering. Many who finished had stopped fostering, or planned to stop, within 6 months. Families with more resources, especially income, were more likely to continue.\
Stage: assessment, retention · Type: longitudinal · Place: United States · [Source](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:2f2189c0-7e59-4e83-9f66-b8738c332ecc/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

## What happens after someone gets in touch, and what helps?

We added these entries in November 2026 from our [review of intake interactions](reports/2026-11-intake-interactions.md). Intake is everything that happens between someone's first enquiry and their formal application. Unless an entry says otherwise, its summary comes from that review. Those summaries are not yet checked by a person against the original sources.

**Ipsos and CEI for DfE (2026). Evaluation of Foster with North East Pathfinder Hub: year 3 report.**\
The share of people who had an initial visit within 5 days rose from 29% to 50%. Councils led the visits in 2024, and the hub led most of them in 2025. The evaluation used Ofsted data to compare change over time in the hub's area with change in similar areas. This method is called staggered difference-in-differences. It found no statistically significant positive effect on applications, households approved or carers approved. In 2023–24 the measured effects for Foster with North East were negative and significant. In 2024–25 none were significant. The hand-off from the hub to the council was a point of friction. Some people who withdrew said communication "ceased or diminished". This is the strongest controlled test so far of a faster start for people who get in touch. It is also a warning that speed alone is not enough.\
*Checked by a person on 14 November 2026. We compared the figures and quotations with the executive summary and impact results (about pp. 12, 42 and 50).*\
Stage: enquiry, assessment · Type: mixed (staggered difference-in-differences against similar areas, monitoring data, 51 interviews) · Place: North East England · [Source](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6aa8195b1a63d2d2be90460e/Evaluation_of_Foster_with_North_East_Pathfinder_Hub_year_3_report.pdf)

**Kantar Public for DfE (2022). Applying behavioural insights to fostering and adoption in England.**\
How the first contact felt was the factor people mentioned most. Many saw enquiring as the start of an application, and were afraid of asking "the wrong question". In the online experiment, every redesigned message did better than typical recruitment wording. For fostering, describing fostering and adoption as a continuum, one connected range of options, worked best. For adoption, a "safe space" framing worked best. The experiment measured what people said they intended to do, not real enquiries.\
Stage: awareness, enquiry · Type: mixed (literature review, 50 interviews, 8 groups, online experiment with about 2,200 adults) · Place: England · [Source](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/639b0743d3bf7f7f88f10fa8/Applying_behavioural_insights_to_fostering_and_adoption_in_England.pdf)

**Madden et al. (2017). Agency-related barriers experienced by families seeking to adopt from foster care.** *Adoption Quarterly*, 20(1), 25–43.\
The study followed families until they adopted or stopped, and 98 adopted. Families most often reported barriers in 3 areas: the practical side of the process, the agency's communication and responsiveness, and its emotional support. The study treats responsiveness as part of the core service, not an extra.\
*Checked against the abstract: the sample size and the outcome counts.*\
Stage: enquiry, assessment · Type: longitudinal (300 families, interviews then quarterly surveys) · Place: United States · [Source](https://doi.org/10.1080/10926755.2015.1121187)

**Geen, Malm & Katz (2005, year as given in the review). Listening to parents: overcoming barriers to the adoption of children from foster care.** Harvard Kennedy School working paper.\
Describes steep losses between the first call and adoption. It identifies the first call as the earliest point that agencies handle poorly.\
Stage: enquiry · Type: mixed (3 sites) · Place: United States · [Source](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/listening-parents-overcoming-barriers-adoption-children-foster-care)

**The Fostering Network and CEI (2023). Foster carer retention and recruitment in England.**\
Some applicants withdraw late, once they see what fostering involves in practice. One example is a weekly pattern of meetings that clashes with work. This supports giving honest information early, together with the support on offer.\
Stage: assessment, retention · Type: survey and focus groups · Place: England · [Source](https://www.thefosteringnetwork.org.uk/media/cxspoevy/cei_report_foster_carer_retention_and_recruitment_may23.pdf)

**Behavioural Insights Team (2015). Update report 2013–2015: Bedford Jobcentre trial.**\
One version of a text message named an adviser who had booked a place for the person at a recruitment event. This raised attendance from 10.5% to 26.8%. The trial is not about fostering, but it is the clearest evidence that a named, personal message can change what people do.\
*Checked by a person on 14 November 2026. The sample of 1,224 and the 4 attendance rates (10.5%, 14.8%, 17.4% and 26.8%) are in Figure 1.4, p. 9. We read which rate belongs to which message from the chart.*\
Stage: analogue (not fostering) · Type: randomised trial (1,224 jobseekers) · Place: England · [Source](https://www.bi.team/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/BIT_Update-Report-Final-2013-2015.pdf)

**AdoptUSKids / National Resource Center for Diligent Recruitment. Is your response system family friendly?**\
A self-assessment for agencies on how they respond to people who get in touch. It asks who answers, how quickly, and whether experienced families help design and test the way the agency responds. It suggests secret shopping, where someone poses as a person enquiring to test the service. We prefer declared test enquiries, which services know about and agree to.\
Stage: enquiry · Type: practice guidance (self-assessment) · Place: United States · [Source](https://adoptuskids.org/_assets/files/NRCDR-org/is-your-response-system-friendly.pdf)

## Who thinks about fostering, and what stops them?

These studies look at who might foster, what stops them, and what helps them decide.

**Sebba (2012). Why do people become foster carers? An international literature review on the motivation to foster.** Rees Centre, University of Oxford.\
Meeting or knowing other foster carers is what most often leads people to consider fostering. The first reply to an enquiry is often too slow. A large share of people drop out between asking for information and going further. The review recommends giving carers a stronger role as recruitment ambassadors, who help recruit new carers. Many of the drop-out figures it draws on come from studies published between 1990 and 2008. Summary from the key findings.\
Stage: awareness, enquiry · Type: review · Place: international · [Source](https://www.education.ox.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/Why-Do-People-Become-Foster-Carers-An-International-Literature-Review-on-the-Motivation-to-Foster.pdf)

**Gouveia et al. (2021). Foster families: a systematic review of intention and retention factors.** *Journal of Child and Family Studies.*\
Whether people intend to foster is shaped by their motivation, family circumstances, values, social context and how well they know the system. For whether carers stay, the relationship with the agency and support from professionals stand out as the most important factors.\
Stage: awareness, retention · Type: review (49 studies) · Place: international · [Source](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:cb06a59d-9e41-4892-85a7-5b6ca9aaa0fb/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Randle, Miller, Dolnicar & Ciarrochi (2014). The science of attracting foster carers.** *Child & Family Social Work*, 19(1), 65–75.\
Sorts people into 4 groups by the mix of barriers that stop them fostering. Almost one-third said they had never considered fostering because no one had ever asked them. This supports asking people directly. Summary from the abstract.\
Stage: awareness · Type: survey (756 adults, 2009) · Place: Australia · [Source](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2206.2012.00881.x)

**Lind et al. (2025). What prevents people from becoming foster carers?** *European Journal of Social Work.*\
The biggest barriers people named were lack of time or space. Next came doubt about their own ability, and worry about whether support would be available. The authors recommend that recruitment sets out the requirements clearly and explains the support on offer.\
Stage: awareness · Type: survey (about 2,000 adults) · Place: Sweden · [Source](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:9ff1d0a5-c6a1-478d-aa1c-0a7861371137/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Lind et al. (2025). Is there an unrecognized potential pool of foster carers?** *Child & Family Social Work.*\
Looks at the small group who say they are likely to apply in future. It asks why they have not applied yet, and what would lead them to apply. Half were interested in fostering as an alternative to having a job.\
Stage: awareness · Type: survey (2,106 adults) · Place: Sweden · [Source](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:5b250f1a-d7a1-46dc-9365-68ddf6770131/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Kunstler et al. (2025). Improving foster carer recruitment in Victoria: a mixed-methods approach to support tailored recruitment efforts.** *Journal of Public Child Welfare.*\
About 31% of the adults surveyed fell into a group inclined to foster. Agencies saw it as essential to tailor both the message and the audience.\
Stage: awareness · Type: mixed (8 agency interviews, 1,033 adults surveyed) · Place: Victoria, Australia · [Source](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:0cfe14a7-3600-4ecc-9c5e-0bd28cbffa92/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Ferreira et al. (2025). Why do people become foster parents and how to recruit new families? A multi-informant study.** *Children and Youth Services Review.*\
Uses behavioural theory, which looks at what makes people act, to describe what stops people and what helps them. Public knowledge of fostering is limited. The study suggests messages should explain how the system works and show evidence that fostering helps.\
Stage: awareness · Type: qualitative (29 interviews) · Place: Portugal · [Source](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:b064401c-f613-47ff-bdc9-5689f528acd3/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Ferreira et al. (2025). Theory-driven factors related to the decision to become a foster caregiver among Portuguese adults.** *Child & Family Social Work.*\
Willingness to foster was linked to awareness, reasons centred on the child, and how much time people felt they had. It was also linked to self-efficacy (belief in their own ability) and action planning (making a plan to act). This matters for designing the first contact around how people behave.\
Stage: awareness · Type: survey (462 adults) · Place: Portugal · [Source](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:395bff94-9253-46a4-95dd-afa03d0f70f8/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Rodger, Cummings & Leschied (2006). Who is caring for our most vulnerable children? The motivation to foster in child welfare.** *Child Abuse & Neglect*, 30(10), 1129–1142.\
Carers were motivated mainly by wanting to care for children and keep them from harm. Good working relationships with the agency brought satisfaction, and poor ones were linked to thinking about leaving. Our November 2026 review of intake interactions reports that 35% first learned about fostering through knowing other foster families. That figure is not yet checked by a person against the original.\
Stage: awareness, retention · Type: survey (652 foster parents) · Place: Ontario, Canada · [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chiabu.2006.04.005)

**Thomson et al. (2017). Recruiting and retaining foster carers.** *Institute of Child Protection Studies research to practice series.*\
A summary for people who work in fostering, on what helps people choose to foster and keep fostering.\
Stage: awareness, retention · Type: review · Place: Australia · [Source](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:fea34727-c173-4e42-b4e7-ac1892ccdfbc/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

## Why do carers stop fostering, and what helps them stay?

These studies look at why approved carers keep fostering or stop. Several point to how the agency works with carers.

**Hanlon et al. (2021). Systematic review of factors affecting foster parent retention.** *Families in Society.*\
The relationship with the agency, how well the system works, and money all affect whether carers stay. Training helps, and so does peer support from an experienced carer.\
Stage: retention · Type: review (US literature 1989–2018) · Place: United States · [Source](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:3d4e7a38-5796-4bcd-a041-7798dbda4c37/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Triseliotis, Borland & Hill (1998). Foster carers who cease to foster.** *Adoption & Fostering.*\
Of the carers who stopped, almost three-fifths did so because they were unhappy with some part of the fostering service. The study calls for services to be more responsive and more available.\
Stage: retention · Type: survey · Place: Scotland · [Source](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:9fc07a41-6f34-44c8-804c-4c82d0337a73/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Randle et al. (2016). What makes foster carers think about quitting?** *Child & Family Social Work.*\
Finds a group of carers who are unhappy with things the agency controls, and who also think most about leaving. The study recommends using initial training to manage what carers expect. This bears on the "process not as expected" reason that applicants give for withdrawing.\
Stage: retention · Type: survey (205 carers) · Place: Australia · [Source](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:cc0a8bf5-e9a8-478f-822a-27fdd5899c20/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Geiger, Hayes & Lietz (2013). Should I stay or should I go?** *Children and Youth Services Review.*\
Carers' intention to continue rises with their satisfaction, and with emotional and practical support. Finding the system hard to navigate is one of the reasons carers consider stopping.\
Stage: retention · Type: mixed (649 carers) · Place: United States (one state) · [Source](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:0d464625-e796-4692-b2d0-6746a60587b0/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**MacGregor et al. (2006). The needs of foster parents.** *Qualitative Social Work.*\
Carers most valued emotional support, trust and good communication with workers, and being treated as part of the team.\
Stage: retention · Type: qualitative (54 carers) · Place: Canada · [Source](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:de1e88c7-dc5f-4464-a862-b972b6063dc9/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Morton et al. (2026). From compassion to collapse: understanding the decline in US foster-parent retention.** *Journal of Public Child Welfare.*\
Themes include exhaustion, uneven support from caseworkers and inefficient bureaucracy. The sample is self-selected: people chose to post, so they may not speak for all carers.\
Stage: retention · Type: qualitative (1,058 social media comments) · Place: United States · [Source](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:3c51297d-8fc7-421c-9e41-c9e78ada0ba6/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Sinclair, Gibbs & Wilson (2004). Foster carers: why they stay and why they leave.** Book.\
A standard reference in England on carers who keep fostering and carers who stop. We have not yet summarised it from the text.\
Stage: retention · Type: mixed · Place: England · [Source](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:2a785cfc-1e40-4040-a196-8d9fa30f2d0a/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

## Official figures and policy for England

These are official statistics, policy papers and guidance. The Ofsted figures for 2024–25 come first because we have checked them.

- **Ofsted (November 2025). *Main findings: fostering in England 1 April 2024 to 31 March 2025.*** Statistics. Services received 8,290 applications. Of the 5,370 completed, 2,050 were approved and 3,290 were withdrawn. That is 61% of completed applications, not of all those received. Of the withdrawals made by applicants, 20% gave "the fostering process not being as expected" as the reason. ## What the research does not tell us yet

These are the gaps we know about.

- No experiment has tested follow-up with real people who enquire about fostering or adoption. No experiment has tested how fast services reply, how many times they try to make contact, or when and how often they follow up. None has tested peer contact, meaning contact with experienced carers or adopters, or hand-offs. The closest is the Department for Education and Kantar's 2022 online test of message wording. It measured only what people said they intended to do. In November 2026 we found no trial by Foundations or Nesta on following up enquiries.
- There are no national figures on how quickly services reply. There are none on how people who enquire experience that first contact.
- No published analysis looks at whether early closures affect some groups of people more than others.
- No one has published an evaluation of chatbots or other artificial intelligence (AI) tools used in fostering or adoption intake.
- There is little recent peer-reviewed work from the UK on the enquiry stage itself. Peer-reviewed work is checked by other researchers before it is published. Most of what exists is grey literature: reports published outside academic journals, for example by government or charities.
- Our November 2026 review did not find standards for how services respond at intake in Scotland, Northern Ireland, Ireland or New Zealand. It did not find evidence from these places either.
- There is little evidence from Spain and the rest of Europe on how people move from enquiry to approval. A 2022 Spanish review calls for this stage to be evaluated. It also calls for exit interviews with families who stop.

## How to read an entry

Each entry starts with the source: authors, year, title and where it was published. Next comes our summary and any note on checking. The last line gives labels for the stage, type and place of the study, and a link to the source.

**Where summaries come from.** Summaries are based on the abstract, the short summary at the start of a study. The exceptions are entries marked "full text read" and entries that say where the summary came from. Check the original before you quote it.

**Checked.** "Checked by a person" means someone compared the entry with the original source. The note says when, and what they compared. "Not yet checked by a person" means nobody has done this yet.

**Linked.** When a summary says two things were linked, they went together in the study. That does not prove one caused the other.

**Stage** is the step in the journey that the study looks at:

- `awareness`: before someone gets in touch
- `enquiry`: from first getting in touch to applying
- `assessment`: from applying to approval, including training
- `retention`: whether approved carers keep fostering
- `analogue`: not about fostering, but the finding may carry over

**Type** is the kind of study:

- `review`: brings together the findings of many other studies
- `survey`: asks many people the same questions
- `qualitative`: looks for common themes in what people say or write, for example in interviews
- `mixed`: uses more than one method
- `longitudinal`: follows the same people over time
- `randomised trial`: puts people into groups by chance, then compares the groups
- `policy` and `statistics`: official policy papers and figures
- `practice guidance`: advice for services

**Place** is the country or region where the study took place.
