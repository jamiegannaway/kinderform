# Evidence library

A living list of research and policy on foster carer recruitment, enquiry handling and retention. Each entry has a link and a short summary in our own words. Entries are summaries of abstracts unless marked "full text read"; check the original before quoting.

Last updated: 2026-11-14

## How entries are tagged

- **Stage:** `awareness`, `enquiry`, `assessment`, `retention`
- **Type:** `review`, `survey`, `qualitative`, `mixed`, `policy`, `statistics`
- **Place:** country or region of study

---

## 1. The enquiry stage

**Keogh et al. (1999). Why don't they become foster carers? A study of people who inquire about foster care.** *Children Australia.*
Stage: enquiry · Type: qualitative (91 phone interviews) · Place: Victoria, Australia
[Link](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:31e6ebdc-13ba-46f2-ba3c-c881b781c1aa/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
Followed up people who had enquired about fostering. Only 4% had become carers. The rest split about evenly between personal circumstances and being put off by how the agency responded to their contact. The closest published precedent for this project's central claim. Old and small, so treat as indicative.

**Engelhardt et al. (2025). The process of becoming a foster parent: agency effects on the retention and recruitment of others.** *Journal of Public Child Welfare.*
Stage: enquiry, retention · Type: survey · Place: United States (one state)
[Link](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:d3bbe679-3e4b-4a0e-9e4d-9dcd2e14f28c/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
Satisfaction with the recruitment worker and with agency communication was linked to carers wanting to continue and to recommending fostering to others. Supports treating the first contact as the start of retention, not a separate task.

**Day et al. (2022). Who are our caregivers? Characteristics of foster parents at certain milestones across two states.** *Journal of Public Child Welfare.*
Stage: enquiry, assessment · Type: survey (656 people) · Place: Missouri and Mississippi, United States
[Link](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:a3736bae-392d-49ee-b8e5-f7bf52559244/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
Describes who is present at each milestone from pre-licence training to first placement, and raises concern about who the recruitment process leaves out. Useful model for milestone-based measurement.

**Rhodes, Orme, Cox & Buehler (2003). Foster family resources, psychosocial functioning, and retention.** *Social Work Research.*
Stage: assessment, retention · Type: longitudinal · Place: United States
[Link](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:2f2189c0-7e59-4e83-9f66-b8738c332ecc/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
Almost half of families who began pre-service training did not finish it, and many who finished had stopped or planned to stop within six months. Families with more resources, especially income, were more likely to continue.

## 2. Who considers fostering, and what stops them

**Gouveia et al. (2021). Foster families: a systematic review of intention and retention factors.** *Journal of Child and Family Studies.*
Stage: awareness, retention · Type: review (49 studies) · Place: international
[Link](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:cb06a59d-9e41-4892-85a7-5b6ca9aaa0fb/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
Intention to foster is shaped by motivation, family circumstances, values, social context and familiarity with the system. For retention, the relationship with the agency and professional support stand out as the most important factors.

**Lind et al. (2025). What prevents people from becoming foster carers?** *European Journal of Social Work.*
Stage: awareness · Type: survey (about 2,000 adults) · Place: Sweden
[Link](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:9ff1d0a5-c6a1-478d-aa1c-0a7861371137/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
Lack of time or space were the biggest stated barriers, followed by doubt about one's own ability and worry about whether support would be available. Recommends that recruitment states requirements clearly and communicates the support on offer.

**Lind et al. (2025). Is there an unrecognized potential pool of foster carers?** *Child & Family Social Work.*
Stage: awareness · Type: survey (2,106 adults) · Place: Sweden
[Link](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:5b250f1a-d7a1-46dc-9365-68ddf6770131/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
Looks at the small group who say they are likely to apply in future, why they have not yet, and what would tip them. Half were interested in fostering as an alternative to employment.

**Kunstler et al. (2025). Improving foster carer recruitment in Victoria: a mixed-methods approach to support tailored recruitment efforts.** *Journal of Public Child Welfare.*
Stage: awareness · Type: mixed (8 agency interviews, 1,033 adults surveyed) · Place: Victoria, Australia
[Link](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:0cfe14a7-3600-4ecc-9c5e-0bd28cbffa92/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
About 31% of surveyed adults fell into a fostering-inclined group. Agencies saw tailored messages and audiences as essential.

**Ferreira et al. (2025). Why do people become foster parents and how to recruit new families? A multi-informant study.** *Children and Youth Services Review.*
Stage: awareness · Type: qualitative (29 interviews) · Place: Portugal
[Link](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:b064401c-f613-47ff-bdc9-5689f528acd3/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
Uses behavioural theory to frame barriers and enablers. Public knowledge of fostering is limited; messages should include how the system works and evidence that fostering helps.

**Ferreira et al. (2025). Theory-driven factors related to the decision to become a foster caregiver among Portuguese adults.** *Child & Family Social Work.*
Stage: awareness · Type: survey (462 adults) · Place: Portugal
[Link](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:395bff94-9253-46a4-95dd-afa03d0f70f8/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
Willingness to foster was linked to awareness, child-centred reasons, perceived time, self-efficacy and action planning. Relevant to the behavioural design of the first contact.

**Thomson et al. (2017). Recruiting and retaining foster carers.** *Institute of Child Protection Studies research to practice series.*
Stage: awareness, retention · Type: review · Place: Australia
[Link](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:fea34727-c173-4e42-b4e7-ac1892ccdfbc/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
Practice-facing summary of what helps people choose to foster and stay.

**Rodger, Cummings & Leschied (2006). Who is caring for our most vulnerable children? The motivation to foster in child welfare.** *Child Abuse & Neglect*, 30(10), 1129–1142.
Stage: awareness, retention · Type: survey (652 foster parents) · Place: Ontario, Canada
[Link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chiabu.2006.04.005)
Carers were motivated mainly by wanting to care for children and keep them from harm. Good working relationships with the agency brought satisfaction, and poor ones were linked to thinking about leaving. The November 2026 interactions review reports that 35% first learned about fostering through knowing other foster families (not checked against the text).

**Randle, Miller, Dolnicar & Ciarrochi (2014). The science of attracting foster carers.** *Child & Family Social Work*, 19(1), 65–75.
Stage: awareness · Type: survey (756 adults, 2009) · Place: Australia
[Link](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2206.2012.00881.x)
Groups people into four segments by the combination of barriers that stop them fostering. Almost one-third said they had never considered it because no one had ever asked them. Supports asking people directly. Summary from the abstract.

**Sebba (2012). Why do people become foster carers? An international literature review on the motivation to foster.** Rees Centre, University of Oxford.
Stage: awareness, enquiry · Type: review · Place: international
[Link](https://www.education.ox.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/Why-Do-People-Become-Foster-Carers-An-International-Literature-Review-on-the-Motivation-to-Foster.pdf)
Meeting or knowing other foster carers is what most often leads people to consider fostering. Replies to first enquiries are often not prompt enough, and a large share of people are lost between asking for information and going further. Recommends strengthening the role of carers as recruitment ambassadors. Many of the drop-out figures it draws on come from studies published between 1990 and 2008. Summary from the key findings.

## 3. Why carers leave

**Hanlon et al. (2021). Systematic review of factors affecting foster parent retention.** *Families in Society.*
Stage: retention · Type: review (US literature 1989–2018) · Place: United States
[Link](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:3d4e7a38-5796-4bcd-a041-7798dbda4c37/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
Retention is affected by the relationship with the agency, how well the system functions, and money. Training and peer support from an experienced carer help.

**Triseliotis, Borland & Hill (1998). Foster carers who cease to foster.** *Adoption & Fostering.*
Stage: retention · Type: survey · Place: Scotland
[Link](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:9fc07a41-6f34-44c8-804c-4c82d0337a73/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
Of carers who stopped, almost three-fifths did so because of dissatisfaction with some aspect of the fostering service. Calls for greater responsiveness and availability.

**Randle et al. (2016). What makes foster carers think about quitting?** *Child & Family Social Work.*
Stage: retention · Type: survey (205 carers) · Place: Australia
[Link](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:cc0a8bf5-e9a8-478f-822a-27fdd5899c20/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
Identifies a group of carers unhappy with things the agency controls, who also think most about leaving. Recommends managing expectations through initial training, which bears on the "process not as expected" withdrawal reason.

**Geiger, Hayes & Lietz (2013). Should I stay or should I go?** *Children and Youth Services Review.*
Stage: retention · Type: mixed (649 carers) · Place: United States (one state)
[Link](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:0d464625-e796-4692-b2d0-6746a60587b0/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
Intention to continue rises with satisfaction and with emotional and practical support. Difficulty navigating the system is among the reasons to consider stopping.

**Morton et al. (2026). From compassion to collapse: understanding the decline in US foster-parent retention.** *Journal of Public Child Welfare.*
Stage: retention · Type: qualitative (1,058 social media comments) · Place: United States
[Link](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:3c51297d-8fc7-421c-9e41-c9e78ada0ba6/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
Themes include exhaustion, inconsistent caseworker support and bureaucratic inefficiency. Self-selected sample.

**MacGregor et al. (2006). The needs of foster parents.** *Qualitative Social Work.*
Stage: retention · Type: qualitative (54 carers) · Place: Canada
[Link](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:de1e88c7-dc5f-4464-a862-b972b6063dc9/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
Carers most valued emotional support, trust and good communication with workers, and being treated as part of the team.

**Sinclair, Gibbs & Wilson (2004). Foster carers: why they stay and why they leave.** Book.
Stage: retention · Type: mixed · Place: England
[Link](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:2a785cfc-1e40-4040-a196-8d9fa30f2d0a/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
Standard English reference on continuing and ceasing to foster. Not yet summarised from the text.

## 4. What happens after someone enquires

Added November 2026 from the [intake interactions review](reports/2026-11-intake-interactions.md). Unless marked otherwise, these summaries come from that review and the originals have not yet been read by a maintainer.

**Ipsos and CEI for DfE (2026). Evaluation of Foster with North East Pathfinder Hub: year 3 report.**
Stage: enquiry, assessment · Type: mixed (staggered difference-in-differences against similar areas, monitoring data, 51 interviews) · Place: North East England
[Link](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6aa8195b1a63d2d2be90460e/Evaluation_of_Foster_with_North_East_Pathfinder_Hub_year_3_report.pdf)
The share of people who had an initial visit within 5 days rose from 29% (2024, council-led visits) to 50% (2025, mostly hub-led). A comparison with similar areas, using Ofsted data, found no statistically significant positive effect on applications, households approved or carers approved. In 2023–24 the measured effects for Foster with North East were negative and significant; in 2024–25 none were significant. The hand-off from hub to council was a friction point, with some people who withdrew saying communication "ceased or diminished". The strongest controlled test so far of a faster front door, and a warning that speed alone is not enough. *Maintainer check, 14 November 2026: figures and quotations checked against the executive summary and impact results (about pp. 12, 42 and 50).*

**Kantar Public for DfE (2022). Applying behavioural insights to fostering and adoption in England.**
Stage: awareness, enquiry · Type: mixed (literature review, 50 interviews, 8 groups, online experiment with about 2,200 adults) · Place: England
[Link](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/639b0743d3bf7f7f88f10fa8/Applying_behavioural_insights_to_fostering_and_adoption_in_England.pdf)
How the first contact felt was the factor people mentioned most. Many treated enquiring as the start of an application and were afraid of asking "the wrong question". In the online experiment, every redesigned message beat typical recruitment wording; describing fostering and adoption as a continuum worked best for fostering, and a "safe space" framing worked best for adoption. The experiment measured stated intentions, not real enquiries.

**Madden et al. (2017). Agency-related barriers experienced by families seeking to adopt from foster care.** *Adoption Quarterly*, 20(1), 25–43.
Stage: enquiry, assessment · Type: longitudinal (300 families, interviews then quarterly surveys) · Place: United States
[Link](https://doi.org/10.1080/10926755.2015.1121187)
Followed families until they adopted (98) or stopped. The most reported barriers were process logistics, agency communication and responsiveness, and emotional support from the agency. Treats responsiveness as part of the core service, not an extra. Sample size and outcome counts checked against the abstract.

**Geen, Malm & Katz (2005, year as given in the review). Listening to parents: overcoming barriers to the adoption of children from foster care.** Harvard Kennedy School working paper.
Stage: enquiry · Type: mixed (3 sites) · Place: United States
[Link](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/listening-parents-overcoming-barriers-adoption-children-foster-care)
Describes steep losses between the first call and adoption, and identifies the first call as the earliest point agencies handle poorly.

**The Fostering Network and CEI (2023). Foster carer retention and recruitment in England.**
Stage: assessment, retention · Type: survey and focus groups · Place: England
[Link](https://www.thefosteringnetwork.org.uk/media/cxspoevy/cei_report_foster_carer_retention_and_recruitment_may23.pdf)
Some applicants withdraw late, once they see what fostering involves in practice, for example a weekly pattern of meetings that clashes with work. Supports giving honest information early, alongside the support on offer.

**Behavioural Insights Team (2015). Update report 2013–2015: Bedford Jobcentre trial.**
Stage: analogue (not fostering) · Type: randomised trial (1,224 jobseekers) · Place: England
[Link](https://www.bi.team/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/BIT_Update-Report-Final-2013-2015.pdf)
Text messages that named an adviser who had booked a place for the person raised attendance at a recruitment event from 10.5% to 26.8%. Not about fostering, but the clearest evidence that a named, personal message can change what people do. *Maintainer check, 14 November 2026: the sample of 1,224 and the four attendance rates (10.5%, 14.8%, 17.4% and 26.8%) are in Figure 1.4, p. 9. Which rate belongs to which message is read from the chart.*

**AdoptUSKids / National Resource Center for Diligent Recruitment. Is your response system family friendly?**
Stage: enquiry · Type: practice guidance (checklist) · Place: United States
[Link](https://adoptuskids.org/_assets/files/NRCDR-org/is-your-response-system-friendly.pdf)
A self-assessment for how an agency responds to people who get in touch: who answers, how quickly, and whether experienced families help design and test the response. It suggests secret shopping; this project prefers declared test enquiries agreed with services.

## Gaps we know about

- No experiment has tested response time, contact attempts, follow-up rhythm, peer contact or hand-offs with real people enquiring about fostering or adoption. The closest is DfE and Kantar's 2022 online test of message wording, which measured stated intentions only. No Foundations or Nesta trial on enquiry follow-up was found (November 2026).
- No national data on how quickly services respond, or on how people who enquire experience that first contact.
- No published analysis of whether early closures fall unevenly on particular groups.
- No published evaluation of chatbots or AI tools used in fostering or adoption intake.
- Little recent UK peer-reviewed work on the enquiry stage specifically; most is grey literature.
- Intake response standards and evidence for Scotland, Northern Ireland, Ireland and New Zealand were not found in the November 2026 review.
- Evidence from Spain and wider Europe on how people move from enquiry to approval is thin. A 2022 Spanish review calls for this stage to be evaluated and for exit interviews with families who stop.
